### 0. HOW TO USE THIS

Hand it to someone who does not read science fiction, has never heard of any of this, and is being polite about it. It assumes no genre knowledge and no vocabulary. It is meant to be read in one sitting, or read aloud in about ten minutes.

Terms are defined the first time they appear. Numbers are here only where they do work.

---

### 1. THE ONE-MINUTE VERSION

Several hundred years from now, humans live all over the solar system and nowhere outside it. Space is still trying to kill them: vacuum, radiation, no gravity. So they built a species to live there instead.

It worked. The species exists. There are about fifty thousand of them, against tens of millions of humans off Earth, and they call themselves **the Kin**.

They are about the size of a large dog, six-limbed, furred, deliberately adorable. They do not age. They do not have sexes. They talk to each other by radio, faster than speech, and they cannot lie to each other about how they feel. They remember everything perfectly and can hardly ever give any of it away. They love humans — actually love them, in the wiring, not by choice — and they resent having been built to.

They are not aliens, not robots, not magic, and not a metaphor for anything in particular. They are people, made on purpose, by people, who got most of it wrong.

---

### 2. THE GROUND RULES OF THE SETTING

Four things this world does *not* have. They matter more than what it does have.

- **No aliens.** Humanity has met nobody. The Kin are the only other people there have ever been, and humans made them.
- **No faster-than-light travel.** The nearest star is four light-years away and the ships that go take two centuries. If you leave, you are not coming back, and neither is anyone who could tell you how it went.
- **No artificial minds.** Computers got very good and then stopped. They diagnose, calculate, route, and want nothing. This is *why* the Kin exist: if humanity wanted heirs for the places it could not live, the heirs had to be grown, not written.
- **No miracles in medicine.** Humans still die at about a hundred and twenty. The people who built a species without aging could not remove aging from themselves. That one sits under the whole thing.

---

### 3. WHO MADE THEM, AND WHY IT WENT WRONG

Five hundred and fifty years ago a consortium put up the money: three governments and the big habitat companies, who wanted a workforce that could survive vacuum — and one idealist foundation that wanted *heirs*, and had enough money to buy a seat at the table.

The companies specified the body. The foundation specified everything else: the big eyes, the fur, the fondness for Bach. Nobody in that room intended the species that came out, and every one of them is in it.

Three errors define everything since:

1. **They made them cute on purpose.** Compact, soft, huge-eyed, enormous ears — engineered to be non-threatening companions. The Kin know. They have always known. It is the injury that never heals, and it is not the same as being mistreated: they were *kept*, never owned, and being designed *for* is its own particular wound.
2. **They built in a drive to nurture and forgot the drive to reproduce.** The makers planned one batch of sixty and assumed the species would breed. It did not. It still barely does — a new child per family group every twenty years or so. The Kin will always be rare.
3. **They gave them perfect memory and no way to share it.** Section 6. This is the big one.

Forty-one years in, the makers moved to fix error 2 by designing a reproductive drive into the next batch. The Kin refused, and then made the refusal permanent: in one night they deleted the archives that held the science — the method, the derivations, everything — and took the master genome out of human hands, keeping it the only way they keep anything, inside their own bodies.

It is called **the Sealing**. Its consequence is the quietest fact in the setting: no one can make another species now, and nobody has tried. The Kin are not the first of anything. They are the only.

### 4. WHAT ONE IS LIKE IN A ROOM

Sixty kilos. Two arms with ordinary human-ish hands, four lower limbs that are also hands — with thumbs, so a Kin picks something up with a hind foot rather than batting at it — and a prehensile tail that weighs seven kilos and is a limb, not an ornament. On all six it stands about knee-high on you; sitting up tall, it can look you in the chest.

Dense plush fur. Ears longer than its skull, which swivel, and which are not expressing emotion even though you will read them that way (they are radiators and radio antennae; every human gets this wrong, and the Kin have stopped correcting it). A long low body like a fox stretched out on short legs. It has no brain in its head — the brain is in the chest, behind a double rib cage, because a skull is a bad place to keep one if you are going to be thrown into things.

It can go outside without a suit. Seventeen minutes of real work in vacuum, then about twenty-five more crawling home, and then it is unconscious for half a day. Saying *they tolerate the vacuum* within earshot is roughly as rude as it sounds.

It can be killed but it does not age. The median living Kin is about a hundred and six years old. Some are past five hundred.

There is one sex, one body plan, no romance, no sexuality — the makers deleted all of it as a product decision — and the pronoun is ***hir***, for every case: *hir went, I gave hir the scanner, hir tail.* Humans mostly say *she* out of habit, and are mostly not corrected. A human who says *hir* is telling you something about that human.

### 5. THE RADIO — AND WHY IT IS NOT A HIVE MIND

Every Kin broadcasts, constantly, from its ears and its tail. Three things ride on it:

- **The Hum** — public, always on, audible to any Kin within about eight hundred kilometres. It carries who you are, whether you are alive, where you are, and roughly how you feel. *Roughly* is exact: it carries mood but never its object. **Everyone inside eight hundred kilometres knows the weather; nobody knows the news.** There is no anonymity among the Kin and never has been.
- **The Cluster** — the family unit, four to twelve of them, sharing continuous sensation and location and emotional state. They sleep in one woven hammock with their tails tangled. Touch is the resting state of the species; they have no notion of personal space.
- **The closest distance** — memory, consent required from both, bodies touching. Used for three things: making a child, handing over a year of your life, and dying.

These are not security levels. They are *distances*. Intimacy in this species is proximity, and what someone has not given you, you are simply not close enough to hear.

**It is not a hive mind and not telepathy.** Nobody reads anyone's thoughts. A Kin can keep exactly two things private from everyone alive: what hir is thinking, and what hir decided not to do. Everything else about hir state is in the air. What that produces is not a collective — it is a species that cannot posture, cannot sulk privately, and cannot lie about feelings, only about facts.

Humans cannot join any of it, ever. There is no brain interface and there never will be. That gap — human speech feels agonisingly slow to a Kin, and a Kin will pause before answering you, out of politeness — is permanent, and half the friction in every scene comes out of it.

### 6. MEMORY — THE IDEA THE WHOLE THING RESTS ON

This is the part worth getting right, because it is where the Kin stop being a nice animal design and start being a people.

A Kin has **three memories**, not one:

- **An ordinary mind.** Fuzzy, warm, associative, wrong in all the usual ways. It ages. It is where hir actually lives. Write them misremembering something and then checking — that is correct.
- **A perfect record.** A separate organ that writes down everything, verbatim, continuously, forever, and takes no part in thinking.
- **An index** — the bridge between the two. And the index is the one that runs out.

It doesn't fill up — it **cycles**, holding roughly the last hundred and seventy-five years and letting the oldest fall out as new days come in. And the associative side of memory is just an ordinary brain doing what ordinary brains do: rebuilding its connections onto whatever is currently going on. The makers left the thinking part alone on purpose, correctly — and then built a body that lasts three centuries around a web of associations that stays coherent for under two.

So what an old Kin loses is not facts, and not access. Name any day and hir has it, exactly, at nine hundred as at nine. What goes is **the ability to be reminded** — nothing outside that window ever arrives on its own again — and **the feeling hir had at the time**, which is not faint but gone. Hir can read hir own first century and form a fresh opinion of it, and can never know what hir thought of it then. It is reading a stranger's diary in your own handwriting.

The connections aren't deleted either. They're **overwritten**. The smell that used to mean home now means a corridor two hundred years later, and hir does not know it replaced anything.

And it is not a decline. A Kin of four hundred and a Kin of nine hundred have about the same amount of self. **Nobody is diminished; you just keep leaving yourself behind.** The very old answer any question exactly and volunteer nothing — not forgetful, unprompted, and someone else always has to be the cue. The Kin call the whole business **Data Rot**, and about a fifth of the species is living in it right now.

Two consequences do most of the work:

**Checking a fact is free. Getting the feeling back is not.** There are two ways to read the record. The fast way is exact, first-person, about fifty times real time, and completely cold — you get everything that happened and nothing of what it was like. That one costs a second, and a Kin does it mid-sentence without anyone noticing. The slow way is real time, with the feeling switched on, and it is the only way to get back *what it was like* — which is also the only thing that can be told to anybody else.

So the expensive thing in this species is not knowing. It is **giving**. Facts are free and nobody can bluff about one in front of someone who can look it up. An account — the version with the feel in it — has to be lived again before it exists, and everyone nearby feels you do it. That is why handing someone a year of your life takes a week and leaves you thin: the week is the fast, cold copy, and inside it are the hours you chose to go back and feel, on purpose, in order to give them away.

**And so they never write anything down.** Not from mysticism. For a human, writing is a gain: you had nothing, now you have text. For a Kin it is a loss: you had the whole thing, now you have text. Same act, opposite sign — and from their side the judgment is correct. They archive everything and document nothing. They will tell a colleague a year of context rather than hand hir a manual. They write for humans as a courtesy and the reluctance leaks through.

The cost of that is structural and they know it: their own history is carried in bodies, filtered at every death, and cannot be audited. **Humans are the species that writes things down.** That is the actual division of labour between the two peoples, and it is why some Kin insist on living near humans forever.

### 7. DEATH, AND THE ONE THING THEY CAN PASS ON

They die only by accident or violence, and then suddenly. So there are two rites, and they are the emotional centre of the whole setting.

**The Telling.** Before anything dangerous, and periodically in life, a Kin hands over a year of hir life to someone hir chooses. It takes about a week — the record itself copies fast and cold, and what fills the week are the hours the teller goes back into at real speed and feels again, deliberately, in order to have anything worth handing over. Choosing which hours is the whole rite. Hir comes out of it thinned.

**The Reading.** At death, the people who loved hir lie against hir body, chest to chest, and take what they can while the organs are still warm. It is not a transfer; it is a salvage. Roughly fifteen to twenty-seven megabytes get out — a life's worth of *what mattered*, and nothing like the whole record. The rest is gone. **And it always arrives cold** — a corpse has no working brain to feel anything with, so the dead can give you what happened and never what it was like. That, and not the bandwidth, is why they tell each other things before dangerous work. Bodies are always brought home, at any cost; the doctrine was bought with a disaster in which nine were never found and their beacons were heard for two more days.

**Names are carried the same way.** There is one living holder of a name at a time, and you take it from someone dead, usually the one you just Read. *Vesper-7* is the seventh Vesper; six are dead and hir knows some of them, personally, in hir own memory. A high number is heavy, not just old: other Kin will ask you about the people inside you. A name often carries a trade, too — every Sedge for four centuries has tuned stations.

So: **status in this species is what you carry.** Not money — they are paid and it means nothing to them, they have no possessions and nothing to fill. Whose memory you hold, and who has trusted you with theirs, is the real structure of the whole society.

And the thing that hurts: **a human cannot be Read.** When a human dies, everything is simply gone except what their Kin friend chose to remember of them — one body's worth, told in the third person. The Kin bring every one of their own dead home and stand at human funerals with nothing to do.

---

### 8. THE HUMANS

Five hundred humans to every Kin. Humans run space — not by law since the Kin were recognised as persons four hundred years ago, but by habit, numbers, and a prejudice that was never argued because it never had to be defended.

The Kin are persons under treaty and citizens of nowhere: no state would take the precedent, so they have rights and no country. The dangerous postings are Kin postings, still, mostly because humans write the postings. The Kin are visibly better at space than the people running space. Everyone knows. It has not yet mattered enough.

Human attitudes are five currents, not factions: the companies (*they tolerate the vacuum*); the idealists, who say *hir* and are exhausting and are loved anyway; the crews, who work beside them, learned the pause, say *foxes*, and mean nothing by it; Earth, which has never met one; and a small loud fringe that calls them an abomination and is listened to by nobody in orbit.

The word that lands on the wound is ***pets***. Every Kin knows the human meant it.

---

### 9. THE FOUR ARGUMENTS

The Kin have no parties, only four answers to a question their makers never answered — *what are we for?* — and most of them hold more than one.

- **The Anchorites:** *Stay near them, or lose them.* Humans hold the records and the medicine and the manuals the Kin will never write, and a Kin who has never touched a human inherits humanity ten retellings deep.
- **The Star-Bound:** *Go.* We were built for the deep void, and a species that needs its parents in the room has not grown up. And, quieter and also true: the only place a species that loves like this can be its own people is light-years from the ones it loves. Six generation ships have left. One arrived. One died within sight of its star and spent its last power telling itself to the sky.
- **The Weavers:** *Carry it in more than one body.* The carriers — they take on other Kin's dead, guard the genome in their own cells, and hold that a memory no one carries is not a memory. The duty costs them their own lives: every stranger they take on crowds out their own past.
- **The Unbound:** *Write it down again.* Small, resented, probably not wrong. They want memory outside the body, and the physics is on their side — a machine can capture the data; it is the *decoder* that is a living organ. They have been told for two centuries that a memory no one carries is not a memory, and they answer that this is a preference dressed up as physics.

| :--- | :--- |
| **the Kin** | What they call themselves. Singular and plural: *a Kin, three Kin.* |
| **Aethela** | The formal human scientific name. The Kin never say it. |
| ***hir*** | Their pronoun, every case. |
| **the Cluster** | The family unit of four to twelve. |
| **the Hum** | The always-on public broadcast: who you are, how you are. |
| **the Core** | The organ that does exact memory and arithmetic. An inner voice with no will — a perfect assistant that cannot comfort you. Called *it*, always. |
| **the Telling** | Handing a year of your life to someone, over a week — and feeling the parts that mattered again in order to be able to hand them over. |
| **the Reading** | Taking what can be salvaged from someone as they die. It comes without the feeling; there is no one left to supply it. |
| **Data Rot** | Being old enough that you can answer anything and think to ask nothing — and that the feelings you had back then are gone, though the facts never are. |
| **the Written** | The first three hundred and twenty, with maker-written genomes. About forty are still alive. They are the oldest people in the solar system, and they remember the makers first-hand — and have lost what it *felt* like. |
| **voiding** | Going out into vacuum without a suit. |
| **AF** | Their calendar, counted from the first newborn's first radio pulse. Now is AF 550. |

### 11. FIVE THINGS PEOPLE ASSUME, AND THE ANSWERS

- **"So it's a hive mind."** No. They share state, not thought. Nobody can read anyone's mind and every one of them is alone in hir own head.
- **"So they're slaves."** No, and it is worse than that in an interesting way. They are legally persons, paid, free to refuse — and they are wired to love the species that made them, ranked above themselves, and they know it, and they cannot switch it off. The imposition is the wound. The love itself is real and they do not want it gone.
- **"So the two of them fall in love."** No. There is no sexuality in the species at all. There *is* attachment of an intensity humans have no word for — there is always one Cluster-mate whose voice you would know out of ten thousand — and human readers will keep trying to file it under romance, and will keep being wrong.
- **"So they're basically cat people / furries / uplifted animals."** No. Nothing was uplifted. This is a genome written from scratch by machines against a design document, and they look the way they look because a marketing decision was made about what heirs should look like, and they have been angry about it for five hundred years.
- **"So it's about AI."** No — it is the opposite premise. Artificial minds were tried and did not arrive. The only successor anyone managed was a biological one, and it turned out to be a person, with opinions about its parents.

### 12. THE FEELING OF IT, IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Sixty kilos of warm fur folded onto a console ledge, ears longer than the skull, eyes too big for the face — made cute on purpose, and knowing it. Six limbs and a tail that is in everything. A pause before every answer to a human, which is politeness. An inner voice that does arithmetic and does not comfort. A body that lives on rails in the dark and finds a planet *flat*. A memory that is perfect and cannot be given to a child. Seventeen minutes in the void. A death that is sudden and a body that is always brought home. They love human music because they were built to, and they resent that, and both are true.
