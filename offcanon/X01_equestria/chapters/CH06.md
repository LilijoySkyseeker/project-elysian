# X01 — Chapter 6
**POV:** Yarrow-3 · **Form:** Chapter

---

A little after one in the morning the big door began to knock in its frame, a small dull knock every few seconds, so Yarrow got out of the heap to see to it.

The straps were where hir had moved them, and the pins were tight; it was the wind working the door against its stop, and a wedge would cure it. Hir was feeling along the sill for where the wedge should go when the sound on the roof changed. For an hour it had been a dry scratching, which the Core had called leaves. Now it was a close, fine hiss down the whole length of the barn, as if every shingle had been set working at once.

Hir opened the door a body's width, and went out.

Out in the stubble it came down on hir everywhere. For a few breaths the fur along hir back held it off, in beads that hir could feel sitting on the guard hairs; then the fur gave, and the water reached the skin along hir spine and went down both flanks in thin cold lines. Hir ears folded flat without being asked. Hir tail took it last, from the root down, until hir felt the weight of it in the muscle over hir hips. Across the river the smell of the forest had changed. For ten days it had been smoke. Now the smoke was under something darker, wet ash and wet leaf and a third thing hir had no record of, coming over the water on a wind that did not stop.

*[Yarrow. In,]* Madder sent, from the heap, not awake enough for more.

Hir went in and lay down at the edge of the heap, where Haw's shoulder found hirs and jerked away.

*[You're wet! Why are you wet? Did you go in the river? Is it the river? Is it on the roof, is that what that is?]*

Yarrow sent nothing. Haw's shoulder came back, slowly, and stayed.

At two hir went out again, and Madder sent hir in. The third time hir stood in it until hir could not feel the pads of hir lower hands, and Madder's *[In]* had a rider on it, very small, that hir did not read. The fourth time Madder sent nothing at all.

Through the frame they had it the whole night: something steady and bright in hir, low down, that hir did not send and could not strip, and that came off hir the way heat comes off a pump housing. Haw could not leave it alone.

*[What's it like? Is it cold? Does it go in your ears? Madder, can I go and—]*

*[No,]* Madder sent.

*[Yarrow. Yarrow. What's it LIKE ~ wide awake now, and furious to be in here]*

*[Wet,]* Yarrow sent.

*[~ Sloe, laughing into the straw, trying not to wake Teasel]*

Hir came in the last time with the light grey in the door, and lay down against Teasel's back, where hir lower hand went round onto Teasel's side as it always did in a heap. There were more ribs under it than there had been a week ago. Teasel was asleep, or doing it well, with the ribbon tight on hir wrist. After a while the hand stopped counting.

*[Radish stallion's knee,]* Sloe sent, to nobody. *[A week late. I'll tell him]*

---

The crew's line ran along the foot of the trees on the far side of the river, from the mill down as far as the Pinwheel fields. Yarrow set out on four in the rain with hir upper hands carried, but the road was mud now, and it took each lower hand to the second knuckle and let it go with a pull, so that by the mill bridge hir was on six.

Twilight had found hir the job at the end of the first week, by asking. It paid six bits the day, and half of that was Applejack's before Yarrow earned it; Sloe had seen to that.

Ruddock was a heavy bay stallion with a coil of line over his withers. On the first morning he had looked at Yarrow for a long time.

"It drives stakes," he had said, to the captain, not to hir. "It hauls. It don't touch a knot."

So hir drove stakes.

They were ash, split out of the round and pointed with an axe, a metre long and as thick as hir wrist, stacked under a tarpaulin at the head of the line. There was a snail on the top one this morning, on the flat of the head, with its body out and its horns up in the rain. Yarrow lifted it off by the shell and set it on the next stake down, and took the first.

The maul was pony-made, with a short handle meant for a mouth, and hir choked it right up in both upper hands. Hir gave the first blow softly, to learn the ground. Yesterday the ground had been soft on top and firm under. Today the maul came down and the stake went in a hand's breadth at a stroke, easy, into something that closed round the wood like a mouth.

Hir set it the way a pad eye is set: in the line of the pull. The clouds were up; the pull would come up; so the stake went in straight, plumb, with the load along its length. The earth ponies raked theirs back, away from the trees, at a slant hir had watched for three days and taken for habit. Hir drove eleven that morning, all of them plumb, and moved the snail from each one to the next before hir drove it.

On the ninth stake the maul came down on a knot in the grain. There was a short dry tick under the head, hardly a sound at all, and then a long tearing as the stake split down half its length and folded open like a hand. Hir stood with the maul up and hir ears half-lifted in spite of the wet. The tick had come first, by most of a second. Hir had felt the fibres let go in the handle before hir heard them.

Hir pulled the two halves out, threw them on the pile for burning, and took another.

*[What was that?]* Haw sent, from somewhere in town. *[You went all—what was that? Yarrow?]*

*[Stake,]* hir sent.

---

The tethered clouds lay along the edge at the height of the lowest branches, one to each stake, each with a new hemp line off its underside down to Ruddock's knot. They were dark grey in the rain and rained steadily into the trees, where the ground under the burn steamed.

At the seventh stake the cloud had come down so low that its underside was level with Yarrow's head. The line ran down past hir shoulder to the stake, and when hir put a hand on it, it was hard as a bar and sang very faintly in the wind.

Hir asked the Core what the line was carrying, from its stretch and its lay.

*Eight hundred newtons, approximately.*

Hir asked what the cloud held, from its size and how dark it was.

*Water content twelve kilograms, approximately.*

Eight hundred newtons was eighty kilos hanging. Twelve kilos of water. And the pull on the line was upward, away from the ground, toward nothing.

Hir put hir hand up into it.

It was cold and wet, colder than the rain, and there was nothing there. Hir fingers closed on air that clung to them, then opened, then closed again, and came away with water running off them to the elbow. The line under hir other hand stayed hard as a bar the whole time. It did not flicker. Hir went in again, up to the shoulder, and felt about for a skin or anything with an edge to it, while the line went on pulling eighty kilos off the stake beside hir.

"Leave it be," said the dun mare, going by with a stake on her back. She didn't stop. She was humming, as she had hummed every day on the line, the same four bars that went up and came round and started again at the top without ever reaching a fifth.

Yarrow took hir arm out.

---

Rainbow Dash came on the line at nine.

She came down out of the rain over the trees with a cloud in front of her, pushing it with her forehooves and her chest and a short hard stroke of the wings at the end of each push. She had soot on one wing still, or again. She brought it down to the eighth stake, where Yarrow was standing, and the line trailed off the bottom of it and came swinging down past hir face.

Hir took it. Hir walked it in to the stake, keeping the weight on it so it would not foul, and stood there holding it while Ruddock came up and made it fast. Dash went up for the next one.

When she came down with that one, Yarrow was already at the ninth stake with both upper hands up, and the line came down into them. For an hour it went like that, down the line: she would drop a cloud to a hand's breadth of where it needed to be and hir would take the line on the first swing and have the weight off it before it could start to walk, so that when Ruddock got there with his teeth and his hooves there was nothing for him to do but the knot.

Ruddock tied a thing like a round turn and two half hitches, with the tail cut short and nothing to seize it. Hir would have left the tail longer, and stopped it. Every time, hir hands closed a little on nothing while hir held the line.

Dash said, "Left of that one," once, when the wind moved a cloud. She said, "Heavy," before the twelfth, and it was, and hir had the load before it pulled hir off hir feet in the mud. At the fourteenth, when a line came down short and hir went up the bank on six to reach it, she said, "Got it?" and hir said, "Yes," aloud, which was the first word hir had said on the line in three days.

At the top of the hour Dash landed by the stack to drink. She opened her saddlebag for a flask, and on the inside of the flap, where nopony would see it unless it was open, a badge was sewn in, blue and gold, three wings on it, with the stitching done very small and even. She shut the flap on it.

"She's quick," she said to Ruddock, with the flask still in her teeth, and tipped her head at Yarrow. "Leave her on the lines with me this morning."

Ruddock looked at Yarrow, and then at Dash.

"It's quick," he agreed, and went back to his knots.

*[You're happy,]* Haw sent, from town. *[You're happy! You're doing a happy. What is it? Is it the water? Is it still doing it? Is it a pony?]*

Yarrow went up the bank for the next one.

---

At half past eleven a young pegasus with a notched ear sat down on the fourth cloud from the end to eat her lunch.

Its underside was a metre off the grass. She had come down on top of it and folded her wings, and then she was sitting there, on nothing, with a bag of oats between her forehooves and her tail hanging over the edge. The cloud gave a little under her, the way a web gives, and came back.

Yarrow stood under it.

Hir asked the Core what she weighed, and it told hir. Hir put a hand on the edge of the cloud, and it went in. Hir put the other hand beside it, and that went in too. A rigger climbs what is there, and she was up there, so something was. Hir got a lower hand up onto the bank for purchase, reached high, and put hir weight on hir arms.

Hir went through it into the wet grass.

It came up round hir, cold and blind, and then the grass was under hir back, with the cloud raining into hir face and the pegasus looking over the edge at hir with an oat on her lip. The crew laughed. It was not an unkind sound. Ruddock laughed down his nose. The dun mare stopped humming to laugh, and then started the four bars again from the top.

Hir lay there a moment, looking up through the grey at the shape of the pegasus sitting where hir hands had gone through.

*[Did you fall?]* Madder sent. *[Yarrow. Position]*

*[Under it,]* hir sent.

---

They had it out at noon, while the crew ate under the tarpaulin and Yarrow sat on the stack of stakes in the rain with a cold egg Sloe had put in hir hand at the barn door.

*[Through it? All the way through?]* That was Haw, and it had been Haw for some time.

*[Eight hundred newtons off twelve kilos,]* Madder sent. *[Say that again. Slowly]*

*[Eight hundred,]* Yarrow sent. *[Twelve]*

*[Then the Core's wrong,]* Sloe sent, from the market. *[It's guessing at the water. It can't see into it]*

*[So it's buoyant. Warm air goes up—]*

*[Not at eighty kilos it doesn't. You'd want a cloud the size of the barn]*

*[And it's cold inside,]* from Yarrow. *[Colder]*

*[Then the pony holds it up,]* Sloe sent. *[Not the cloud. The pony's doing it. The one that brings it down]*

*[There's no pony on it, and the line's pulling,]* Madder sent. *[It pulls up. The pony's gone and it still pulls up]*

*[There was a pony on the one that went through,]* Haw sent. *[Sitting on it! Eating on it! Why can she sit on it and Yarrow can't? What's she doing that Yarrow isn't? Is it her feet? Is it the wings? She had them shut, Yarrow said she had them shut—]*

*[Folded,]* Yarrow sent.

*[So it's her,]* Sloe sent. *[Not the cloud. It knows her]*

*[A cloud doesn't know anything ~ sharper than hir meant]*

*[Then it's us,]* Teasel sent. It came out clean. *[It isn't the cloud that's wrong. It's our numbers that are wrong, here. And we don't know which ones]*

That stopped Haw for nearly a second.

*[Which ones?]* hir sent. *[The gravity reading? The air? The Core says one g, is it one g?]*

*[Everything we've weighed since we came,]* Sloe sent, slower. *[Eggs. Walnuts. A sack of seed I lifted in the duck house. I lifted it and I knew what it was. I always know what it is ~ and hearing hirself, and not liking it]*

Out on the line the pegasus's cloud had gone up again on its tether, and Yarrow sat and looked at it with the egg in hir hand. Through the frame hir felt Sloe's tail go round hir own leg in the market, and Teasel's go round the door frame at the barn. Hir own was round the bottom stake of the stack. Hir had not put it there.

Madder had not sent anything.

All four of them waited on hir, and nothing came. Yarrow had never known Madder go silent that long on a working day. Hir asked the Core how long. *Forty-one seconds.*

When it came, it was the rota.

*[Sloe, food status at dusk. Teasel, in. Haw, nowhere near the edge, not the bank, not the water. Not the trees]*

*[I wasn't going to—]*

*[Not the trees,]* Madder sent.

Yarrow ate the egg, shell and all, and went back to the stakes.

---

The wind got up a little after two. It came down off the forest through the wet trees, so that the rain came at the line sideways and every cloud leaned out on its tether toward the river. Yarrow, at the stack, heard it come on down the bank: a long low creak, stake after stake taking the load.

The eighth stake came out of the ground.

It came out whole, with a short wet suck, and lay a moment in the mud with Ruddock's knot still on it and the snail on its head; then it was going along the bank at the end of its line, bouncing, while the cloud swung broadside to the wind and went off loose over the water toward the fields.

"Three!" the captain said, from his map board. "Three on it, now—Skylark, Gust, you—"

Dash was already gone.

She went up past the cloud in a climb so steep that for a moment she was standing on her tail in the air, then turned over on her back at the top of it, so close that a streamer of grey came away round her, and dropped down the far side in a tight curl that Yarrow's eyes could not follow and hir RADAR only just kept. She came out of the bottom of it with the line in her teeth. Then she was in front of the cloud, both forehooves in it and her wings going short and hard, and it was turning; it was coming back; it was over the line again, over the bank, where it had been, with the stake swinging below it at the end of the rope.

Hir asked the Core. *Ten point four seconds.*

The three the captain had called had got as far as the air above the stack.

Ruddock got the stake, and Dash came down beside him and stood with her sides going, wiping the line-grease off her mouth on her foreleg.

The captain had come down the bank, an old grey stallion with a white muzzle and the map board under his wing. There was a brown ring on its corner, over the forest, where somebody had once set down a mug of tea.

"Dash," he said.

She looked up.

"What's the procedure for a loose cloud?"

It was a quiet voice. All down the bank the crew had stopped.

"Three flyers, sir."

"Three flyers. Ten minutes, and nobody in a hurry." He did not raise it at all. "And if you put a turn like that through a wet cloud that size, over the Pinwheels, and tear it open, where does it come down?"

Dash said nothing.

"On somebody's roof. All at once. You know that, you've done the course. I've had three houses in my career, Dash, and I'll not have a fourth because you wanted to see if you could." He took the board out from under his wing and looked at it, and not at her. "You're in the book already. I told you what that meant. You're off the line for the rest of the day. Go home."

She went. She did not go up. She walked off along the bank toward the mill bridge with her wings shut so tight to her sides that the feathers at the shoulder stood up. Nopony watched her go. Ruddock was looking at the knot on the stake, and the dun mare at the river. One of the three said something to the others, low, and the others didn't answer.

Nobody looked at the hole in the bank where the stake had been.

Yarrow stood by the stack with a stake across hir upper hands and waited for somebody to say it. It was hir stake. Hir had driven it plumb, into ground that closed round the wood like a mouth, and everybody on the bank had seen it come out whole. Hir waited while the captain went back up to his board and Ruddock stood the stake in its hole and the crew went back to their lines, and nobody said it.

On the ship, when a rigger got a load back that had gone, somebody put a bulb in hir hand at the end of the watch. Dunlin always did, and made a noise about it on the channel. Hir had got two that way. Hir had bought three.

Hir went down to the eighth stake. Ruddock stepped back from it without a word and went up the line.

Hir moved the snail to the next stake, and set this one again a metre up the bank on firmer ground, raked back from the trees at the slant the ponies used, and drove it until the head was a hand's breadth off the mud. When it was in, hir put hir weight on the line with both upper hands and all hir legs set, and the stake did not move.

Nobody watched hir do it. The captain was bent over his board. The dun mare was humming.

---

The wagon came out of the burn at half past three.

Two pegasi brought it low over the trees, slung on ropes between them, swinging so that they had to come slowly. They set it down on the bank by the captain's board, on its side, the way they had found it. The wheel was gone at the front left. The spokes that were left on the hub were black stubs. The bed was charred through along one edge, and the paint on the side that faced up had gone to bubbles, red ones, in letters a child had painted with a brush: APPLE BLOOM, with the B the wrong way round.

The captain looked at it for a moment over his board.

"That's the Apple kid's," he said, without much in it. "Name's on it. Somebody run it up to the farm when the cart goes."

Yarrow was halfway down the line with a stake on hir back. Hir stopped. Hir lower left hand closed, in the mud, on nothing.

Through the frame it went out of hir before hir could hold it: one short hard spike, like a line parting.

*[Yarrow?]* Madder sent.

*[Nothing,]* Yarrow sent. It went out clean, with nothing under it, and it cost hir.

Madder did not send again.

Haw sent, *[What? What was it? Was it the wind? Did something go?]*, and got no answer from anyone.

Hir went on down the line and set the stake. From there hir could see the tailboard, on the underside now, against the grass. The cart came at four, with a heavy mare in the shafts, and two of the crew lifted the wagon onto it and roped it down. It went off over the mill bridge and up the far road into the rain, with the wheel that was left turning slowly in the air.

Yarrow drove the stake. Hir moved the snail.

---

Haw had come out at three, as far as the mill bridge, with hir tail held straight up behind hir to keep it out of the mud.

*[I'm not near the edge,]* hir had sent. *[I'm near the bridge. The bridge isn't the edge. I only want to see one close]*

*[Haw,]* Madder had sent.

*[I'm on the road, I'm on the road!]*

Hir had stayed on the road for most of an hour, sitting in the mud, sending everything hir saw: the pegasi, the heavy mare with the cart, a heron at the bend of the river, which the Core would only call a bird. Yarrow felt hir through the frame all afternoon, bright and restless.

When hir went off the road, it was quiet. Hir sent nothing about it.

It was going on for five, and the wind had dropped a little, and Yarrow was at the end of the line by the fields with a stake in hir hands when hir heard Haw send.

*[~ nothing in it but delight, rising]*

Hir looked up.

At the end of the line, where the bank turned, there was a tree taller than the rest, an ash, old, with a straight grey trunk and its first branches very high. The crew called it the corner tree, and the last cloud on the line was moored under its crown, low over the fields, at the height of its upper limbs. Haw was in it. Hir was a long way up, out along a limb that went toward the cloud, on all six, with hir tail wrapped round the limb behind hir. Hir fur was dark with the wet and hir ears were flat, and hir had one upper hand out in front of hir, open, going toward the grey.

*[It's right there. It's right THERE. I can feel it cold on my face. I'm nearly in it. Yarrow, I'm nearly in it, I'm going to put my hand in—]*

*[Haw. Down,]* Madder sent, from the barn, and under it the frame went tight all at once in four bodies, and Haw was too happy to feel it.

Yarrow stood on the bank with the stake across hir upper hands. Hir did not send anything. Hir had hir eyes on the limb, where it went out from the trunk, and on the way it bent under hir, which was more than a limb that thick should bend, in the wet.

Up in the ash, the wood gave a short, dry tick.

It was the sound the stake had made that morning, under the maul, most of a second before the grain went.
