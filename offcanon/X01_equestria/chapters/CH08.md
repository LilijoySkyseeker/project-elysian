# X01 — Chapter 8
**POV:** Teasel · **Form:** Chapter

---

Sloe did the count at dusk on the lid of the seed bin, where the last of the light came flat across the stubble and in at the door.

Hir laid it out as hir always did: walnuts in fives, eggs in a row, and the bean sack at the end with its neck rolled down so that hir could see how far it had gone. Yarrow's three bits for the day lay beside the eggs. The other three had gone to Applejack's stall at noon, as they went every day, before Yarrow had carried them further than the end of the crew line.

*[Walnuts, thirty-one. Eggs, nine ~ counting the eggs a second time, and liking the second count less]*

Teasel sat on the sill with hir back against the door post. Hir had the number before Sloe did. All week hir had watched the pile go down a walnut at a time, and could have said who had taken which, since hir watched everything in the barn that was eaten: what it was, who ate it, how fast, and whether they looked round first.

Haw never looked round. Hir sat in the straw with the bound arm across hir chest, in the sling the night nurse had knotted for hir out of a flour sack, and took an egg off hir own pile and cracked it against hir knee. Hir did it one-handed, with a short hard knock, then tipped the egg into hir mouth and the shell in after it.

*[Did you see that? Two halves. CLEAN ~ enormously pleased with hirself]*

*[Seen,]* Sloe sent. *[That's your fourth today]*

*[Is it? The arm's hungry. Madder said it would be. Is there a fifth?]*

There was not a fifth, and Sloe sent nothing more. Teasel had hir own egg under a lower hand on the sill. It had been warm from the hen house when hir took it, and it was cold now. When Sloe turned to roll the bean sack shut, Teasel got up and crossed the barn toward the trough, and on the way the egg went into the straw beside Haw's head, into the hollow Haw rolled into at night. Haw found an egg there most mornings, and had stopped asking how.

At the trough hir drank for a long time. Hir had asked the Core once what water was worth. *Zero kilocalories.* It sat in the stomach, though, and made a weight there, which was something.

*[Teasel. Status,]* Madder sent from the back of the barn, where hir was going over the door straps with Yarrow.

*[Fine,]* Teasel sent.

Hir felt it go out flat and smooth, with nothing under it, and felt what it cost to send it that way. It was the same cost every time now, like a door that needed a shoulder. Sloe's ears moved. Yarrow's did not. Nobody sent anything after it, and underneath all five of them the low, steady wrongness went on in the frame as it had for three weeks: a cold place that each of them kept putting a hand to without finding its edge.

That night Teasel lay on the outside of the heap, where hir tended to end up. Some time after midnight Haw's lower hands came looking for warmth in hir sleep, as they did every night, and found Teasel's flank, and went off it again, over hir, to Yarrow.

At first grey the swallows' second brood started up in the rafters. Teasel lay still and counted the feeds. Before the sun reached the ridge there had been fifty-three, and every one was the same: a shape in the gap under the eaves, a shriek from the nest, and a beak being filled. On the river road into town that morning hir fell behind Sloe at the mill, and then further, and Sloe waited at the far end of the bridge without sending why.

---

The fillies came on the fifteenth day, in the afternoon, after the rain had stopped.

Teasel smelled them from the sill before hir heard them. There were three small ponies and a larger one, and with them came apples, wet wool, something sweet gone stale in a bag, and over all of it a thin sharp smell that hir had last had on the rise with the ferns burning. Hir ears went round to the road. Hir tail came in against hir flank and wound itself tight.

They came down the track from the river road with Applejack behind them. At the field gate Applejack stopped and said something low to Apple Bloom, who nodded without looking at her. Then Applejack stood by the gatepost and let them go on alone. Teasel watched her pull a stem of grass from the verge and put it in her mouth, and stand there chewing it without swallowing.

The three of them stopped in the doorway in a row. Apple Bloom was in the middle, with a saddlebag on and no bow in her mane. Sweetie Belle had her eyes on the floor already. Scootaloo stood half a step behind the other two with her wings clamped shut.

"We came ourselves," Apple Bloom said. "Nobody sent us. Rarity told you already, but it was ours to tell, so." She stopped. "So we came."

Nobody in the barn moved. Sloe had a walnut in hir hand, halfway to the bin. Haw was in the straw with hir back against a post, Madder by the door straps with Yarrow beside hir. Teasel was nearest the fillies of all five, close enough on the sill to have touched Scootaloo's tail.

To the fillies, Teasel supposed, the barn had only gone quiet.

*[Facts are logged,]* Madder sent. *[We have the day, the hour, the place and the object. Nothing they say changes the rota]*

*[Madder, say something to them, say something kind, they're children, look at the little one's knees ~ all of Sloe's warmth at once, far too much of it]*

*[Three of them! Three, with a WAGON, and they PUSHED it ~ laughing]* That was Haw, and it came out aloud as well, a high breathy chirr of a laugh that made Sweetie Belle flinch. *[All that, and it was THEM—]*

Then Yarrow sent, and nothing rode under it at all.

*[I knew. The wagon. The day it came out of the burn]*

It reached the four of them in the same instant. Teasel felt it land in Sloe like a missed step, and in Haw, whose laugh went out. Hir felt it in hirself, low, where the hunger lived. In Madder it did not land so much as set: hir whole weight in the frame went hard and still, like a line when the load comes on.

*[Ten days?]* Sloe sent. *[Yarrow. Ten days?]*

Yarrow sent nothing.

All of it had happened while Apple Bloom drew one breath. She let it out, looking from one of them to the next at five strangers gone still.

Then Madder spoke aloud. Hir voice was careful and slow, with each word set down on its own.

"What was it like?"

Apple Bloom's ears went back. "What?"

"The night." Madder left the pause that Kin left for ponies. "Rarity told us what happened. She could not tell us what it was like. What was it like, for you?"

Teasel smelled it go into them cold. Sweetie Belle's smell sharpened. Apple Bloom's jaw set, and she said, "It was bad, what do you think it was like," and would have gone on, except that Scootaloo came forward past her into the light.

"Loud," Scootaloo said.

They waited for her.

"It was loud, and then it went all quiet in my ears, like after a firecracker. And there was a heap on the path. You. Right next to me, moving, and I couldn't move. I wanted to fly and my wings wouldn't." She stopped, and started again. "Then I ran. I left them. Apple Bloom was stuck in the harness and I just went." Her voice had gone flat and small. "That's not the bit, though. Everybody thinks it turned over by itself. It didn't. There was a crack down the side, like a seam, and I had a stick with a bent end, and I put it in the crack because I wanted to see what was inside. Apple Bloom said no. She said it twice. I did it anyway, while she was pulling and couldn't see. And it went *tick*." She was looking at the floor between Teasel's lower hands. "So it was me that broke it. Not the stream or the stone or anything. Me."

In the frame Haw started to send something, and didn't. Teasel could smell the cost on her: salt, and the sourness of a filly who had left her breakfast in the bowl.

"I didn't tell either," Apple Bloom said quickly, as if it were a race. "Sweetie told Rarity everything but that. We said we wouldn't. And I lied to my sister about the wagon, and she knew, and she never said nothing, which is worse." She swallowed. "And there was a hand. On my wagon. It came over the side and grabbed on, and it wouldn't let go, and I was stuck in the traces and it kept pulling me backwards, and I thought—"

Across the barn, Yarrow's lower left hand lay open in the straw. As Teasel watched, it turned over, palm down, and stayed like that.

"—and I got out, but my bow was caught in the buckle, and I left it. It was my good one." Apple Bloom stopped. She was looking at Teasel's wrist.

The ribbon had been there since the third morning, when Sloe had tied it on in the dark and said to keep it on. Teasel had stopped feeling it weeks ago. The burned edge had gone hard as bark, and the rain had pulled the knot so tight that hir was not sure it would open at all.

Hir put the wrist to hir mouth and worked at the knot with hir teeth, and then got a thumb of the other hand under it. It took longer than it should have. Hir could feel all four of them watching through the frame, and the three fillies watching with their eyes, and Applejack at the gate, too far off to see anything, watching too. The knot gave. Teasel held the ribbon out across the sill on hir open hand, where it lay in a curl, red on one side and brown on the other.

Apple Bloom looked at it and did not take it. Then she reached out and took it in her teeth by the middle, carefully, so that her lips did not touch hir palm. She turned it over and looked at the burned edge for a moment. Then she put it in her saddlebag and did the buckle up.

Somewhere at the back of the frame Sloe felt the only thing they had ever had to trade go into a filly's bag, and sent nothing about it.

Sweetie Belle had not said a word yet. Now she said, all at once, "The fire smelled like hot pennies, and the ferns made a noise like screaming, I know ferns can't scream but that's what it sounded like, and there was a little animal dead in the clearing, all curled up with smoke coming off it, and Scootaloo was just standing there looking at it, and I thought if we stayed one more second we'd—" She stopped dead, with her mouth still a little open. Then she lifted her eyes off the floor for the first time, and looked straight at Madder.

"What was it like for you?"

The barn was silent. The frame was not.

*[That isn't theirs to have,]* Madder sent.

*[She asked you, Madder, a child asked you straight out, you can't just—]* from Sloe.

*[Tell her about the frog! Tell her the FROG ~ laughing again, and hurting with it now]* from Haw.

Yarrow sent nothing.

"Your account is received," Madder said aloud, in the same careful voice. "The Cluster thanks you for it. It will be kept."

The three fillies stared at hir. Sweetie Belle's ears went slowly down.

"Oh, it was a night, wasn't it," Sloe said aloud, quickly, warmly, with the words coming over each other. "It was a night for everyone. We had it rough too, you know, we'd no idea where we were, and none of us is saying you— well. It's all water under the mill now, as they say here. And you came all the way out in the wet, the three of you, with your sister waiting up at the gate, would you take a walnut, any of you? We've got—" Hir stopped, with the walnut still in hir hand. Teasel, who knew the count, watched hir fail to hold it out.

Haw was still laughing, softer now, into hir good shoulder, with hir eyes shut. Yarrow said nothing at all.

Teasel heard hirself begin before hir had decided to.

"I smelled you," hir said.

All three fillies turned to hir. To get the rest, hir had to go back to the rise, and there was only one way to go back: slowly, at the speed it had happened. Hir went. Hir had the heat on hir back again, and the crack and spit of the ferns, and the ground pulling at a body that did not know yet how to hold itself up. There was Yarrow's grip locked on something hir could not see. Under the smoke, before anything else in the world had a name, there were three small sharp smells going away downhill, fast. Hir felt the other four feel hir go. Nobody stopped hir.

"Before I could see," hir said. "Before I was properly awake. Three of you, going away downhill. You smelled the way you smell now." Hir stopped, and made hirself finish. "It was the first thing here I knew what it was."

Scootaloo looked at hir. Sweetie Belle shut her mouth. Apple Bloom said, "Okay," like somepony who has been handed something heavy and cannot see where to put it down. Then she said, "We gotta go. My sister's waiting."

They went up the track in a row. At the gate Applejack took the grass stem out of her mouth and dropped it, and they went off up the river road with Applejack half a step behind Apple Bloom, close to her, not touching.

---

They were barely past the gate when Madder sent.

*[Ten days]*

*[Yes,]* Yarrow sent.

*[You stood on that bank and sent me Nothing. Clean. And I let it go]*

*[It was their wagon]*

*[It was the Cluster's to know. All five of us. And you decided, on your own, on a working day, that—]*

The send stopped in the middle. Teasel had never felt Madder do that. Hir went out by the big door into the stubble and stood there with hir back to the barn, and Yarrow sat down where hir was, and nobody went after either of them. After a while Sloe put the walnut back on the pile and began the count again from the start.

---

In the fourth week Teasel went to the river more and more.

Hir went to drink, and to sit somewhere with nobody counting anything. The frame came with hir, as it came everywhere, but the noise of the weir filled hir ears, and with the water loud the four of them felt further off than they were. Hir was slow on the road now. Hir left the barn on four with hir upper hands carried, and by the mill hir was on six.

The weir crossed the river below the mill in a long slope of stone, with the water going over it in one smooth sheet and breaking white at the foot. Beside it, on the town side, there was a low cottage with a tarred roof, and for a gatepost it had the tiller off a barge, painted green so long ago that the green was mostly in the cracks of the grain.

It was there, on the third evening, that Teasel smelled the fish.

Under the smells of weed and wet stone there was a thicker one that came and went with the current, oily and rich. It had not occurred to hir to ask what it was. Haw had put a hand in the river in the first week and sent *[There are THINGS in it! Moving things!]* to everyone, and nobody had asked anything further. Hir ears came forward now, and hir asked the Core what a fish would be, as food.

*Protein, high. Fat, moderate. Zinc and copper present. Suitable.*

Hir asked how much fish would make a day.

*One point three kilograms, approximately.*

Hir sat on the bank with hir hands in hir lap. Then hir reached for the frog. Hir had it back whole: the flat stone in the stream hollow, the gold eye, the throat going, and the way it had sat there under Madder's face and not moved, until it was gone. Hir reached for the grey doe at Fluttershy's, which had gone over the plank bridge toward the smoke and not come back, and Fluttershy's voice when she stopped at the tree line. Fluttershy lived downstream. Everything that went over this weir went past her meadow, where she fed the larks at four.

Teasel had never killed anything in hir life.

The old mare from the cottage had seen hir the first evening, from her step, where she sat eating fried potatoes out of a paper. They were cold, by the smell, with vinegar on them. She had not said anything. On the second evening she nodded. On the fourth she came down to the gate with the tiller in it and leaned there, looking at the river, not at Teasel.

"Slack water, under the apron," she said, and tipped her head downstream, to where the white water ran out and went dark and slow. "Deep hole there. Big 'uns lie up in the bottom of it while the river's cold. They come up at dusk."

Then she went back indoors and shut the door.

---

Teasel went down to the pool the next evening, at dusk.

Below the weir the white water spread and slowed and went black, and the bank dropped away under it so that hir could not see bottom. Hir lay flat on the stones at the edge and put both upper hands in to the elbow.

The cold went in to the bone at once. *Ten degrees.* Hir kept the hands still. Kin hands were fast, faster than anything hir had seen in this place, and when they closed they locked, and neither of those was something hir needed to think about. The stillness was the part hir had to do. Hir did it while the light went off the water and a bat came out over the weir, and went on doing it after hir fingers had stopped telling hir anything.

Hir felt the fish before hir saw it. The water moved against the backs of hir fingers, and then something long and slow slid along the inside of hir wrist, touching it, as if the wrist were a root.

Hir closed.

It was stronger than it had any right to be. The whole length of it bucked and threw water in hir face, and the lock held, and hir came up off the stones onto hir knees with it, a fish as long as hir forearm, cold and fighting, its tail slapping hir chest. Hir did not know what to do next. Hir hit it on the stones and it slid; hir hit it again, wrong, across the tail end, and it went on fighting, and a thin high whine came out of hir that hir could not stop. Then hir got both thumbs in behind its head and bent it back until something went. It shook along its whole length and stopped.

The spike went out of hir into the frame before hir could do anything about it.

*[Teasel?]* That was three of them at once.

Hir ate it there on the bank, sitting back on hir heels, raw. The skin was tough and the flesh under it was pale and cold and dense, and hir stomach took it as if it had been waiting. Hir spat out the larger bones and ate the rest, the head last. It took a while. Hir did not look at anything but the fish.

*[Teasel. Status,]* Madder sent.

Hir tried to strip it and could not. It went out with everything riding under it that hir had held down for three weeks: *[At the pool ~ blood on hir mouth, and full, and shaking with it]*

*[FULL? Full of what? What have you got? Is it a thing from the river? Teasel, is it a fish, did you get one, can I—]* from Haw.

Madder sent nothing back, and neither did Sloe.

Then there were hooves on the towpath. A mare was coming along it from the mill end in the last of the light, with two foals. The smaller foal was carrying a jam jar with a stickleback in it, holding it up to see the fish turn. They stopped. The mare looked at a Kin on the bank with a fish's tail in hir hand and hir mouth dark to the chin, and without a word she put a hoof to each foal and turned them round, and walked them back the way they had come, not fast. The older foal looked back once. "Gryphon," he said, in the voice for a word you don't say in front of your mother.

"Walk," the mare said.

The jam jar went away up the towpath with the stickleback turning in it. Teasel sat on the bank and rinsed hir hands in the river. By the bread queue at five, hir thought, the miller would have it.

---

The town had it by the bread queue at five.

Pinkie Pie was at the bakery's back door when Teasel and Sloe went by in the dark, with flour to her elbows and the oven open behind her, eating a heel of bread standing up. She stopped chewing when she saw them. Then she started again, and said "Morning," round the bread, a beat later than she used to.

Twilight sat on the library steps at noon with a sandwich in wax paper going soft in the drizzle, which she had forgotten about, and a new card box under her wing, tied with string. She said hello and asked nothing. Her eyes went once to Teasel's mouth, and away. On the step below her Spike was polishing a stone with a cloth. When he was done he looked at it against the light, and put a chip of something green in his mouth from a twist of paper at his side, and crunched it. Whether anyone had paid him yet, Teasel could not tell.

At the boutique window Rarity stood with a cup of tea on the sill beside her and a letter in her hoof. The tea had stopped steaming some time before, and she had not touched it.

Applejack ate an apple at her stall, core and pips and all, while she crossed a line out in her book. Sloe came back up the road with the empty basket and sent *[Tab's under two]* to all of them, and nobody answered.

It rained on the Tuesday, and again on the Thursday. The stallion with the bad knee said he had known it would. Yarrow sent from the crew line that Dash was back on it, which was the longest thing Yarrow had sent all week, and that Ruddock had given hir a knot to tie.

Madder sent the rota every dusk. Every day somebody was somewhere else. Haw went to the mill bridge anyway and sat on the road, and Sloe went to market at the wrong hour and came back with nothing, and Teasel went to the weir, and Madder did not send the rota twice. At the fountain a mare called her colt in from the rim when Teasel came by, and he went, looking back. The egg mare at the market put her price up for the Kin by a bit a dozen, and said it was the rain.

Once, far off, on the path along the far side of the meadow, Fluttershy went by with a basket on her back. She stopped and looked toward the barn for a while. Then she went on.

---

That night Teasel came in late from the pool, smelling of the river, and lay down on the outside of the heap. Before hir lay down, hir put the egg in the straw by Haw's head, where it always went.

Some time after midnight Haw's lower hands came looking for warmth in hir sleep, as they did every night, and found Teasel's flank, and stayed there.
