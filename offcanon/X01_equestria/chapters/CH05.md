# X01 — Chapter 5
**POV:** Twilight · **Form:** Chapter

---

Spike was halfway through the business of Mrs. Hay Bale's brooch when he held out the letter, and he didn't stop talking while he did it.

"—so I told her it's not a garnet, it's glass, and she said her mother paid four bits for it at the Cloudsdale fair, so I said it's very nice glass. That came up at six. It's got the seal on. I didn't charge her in the end. For the brooch, I mean. You can't charge somepony for glass."

"No," Twilight said. She had the letter open before he'd finished. It was short, as the Princess's letters were when she was busy, and most of it was about the weather in Canterlot. One line in the middle was not.

*The Mayor tells me you have visitors, and that she is at her wits' end; would you look after them for me?*

She read it twice. Then she put it down on the desk beside the shoebox, and her eye went to the shoebox without being asked.

It had been a boot box once, for a pair of Rarity's winter boots, and it held the index. There was one card for every manuscript in the royal library's catalogue of pre-Nightmare holdings, and there were about three thousand manuscripts. The cards were in the Princess's own preferred order, which was not alphabetical and took a page of notes to explain. A rubber band held the lid on. It had snapped in the second week of March, and Twilight had knotted it, and the knot sat on top of the lid like a small hard bead. Inside there were four hundred and twelve cards. She knew the number without counting.

Spike thought it was nearly finished. She had told him so, in February, when it had seemed like the kind of thing that would soon be true.

"Is it the index?" he said. "Does she want it early?"

"No. It's the visitors." She took a fresh sheet and wrote *Visitors* at the top of it, and under that, in a column: *Food. Water. Warm enough at night? Anyone ill.* She looked at it. Under that she wrote *Index, two hours after*, and underlined it, and then she went downstairs and made a cup of tea, which she left on the windowsill when Spike called up to ask where the good polishing cloth had gone.

---

The river road went out of town past the mill, and after the mill there was nothing on it but fields.

She heard the market bell go for eight behind her as she passed the last fence. It was a flat, cracked bell that hung from a post by the fountain, and she had hated it for about a month when she first came to Ponyville, and now she would have missed it. The cabbages were up in long rows on her left. On her right, across the river, the forest edge still had smoke lying along it, and the weather team's clouds sat low on the trees in a grey line all the way down toward the Pinwheel fields. One pegasus was out, very small, pushing a cloud along the edge. Twilight watched it until the road bent.

She ate half her sandwich as she walked. It was cress and hay on brown bread, and she had made it at six, and it had gone a little soft.

The seed barn stood alone in a stubble field with the river going by at the bottom. She had passed it a hundred times without looking at it. Now there was somepony on the ridge of the roof, or something, very high up, and as she came off the road into the stubble she saw that it was hanging off the ridge by its back feet, head down, looking at the river.

Then it saw her, and it went still.

So did the others. She had not seen them until they stopped moving: one at the big door with its hand on the frame, one sitting in the sun against the wall, two at the corner of the barn by a heap of walnut shells. Every head came round to her at the same moment. It was so quick and so level, like a row of sunflowers, that she stopped in the stubble with one hoof up.

"Hello," she said. "I'm Twilight Sparkle. From the library. The Princess asked me to—" She had rehearsed this on the road and it had gone. "To see how you're getting on."

The one by the wall got up. It walked toward her on its back four legs with its front hands held up off the ground, and it was thinner than she'd expected from the stories, and dustier, and it had a crease of tiredness under each eye that she recognised from her own mirror.

"Twilight," it said. There was a pause before it, but not much of one. "I'm Sloe. The Mayor said a unicorn would come out, and you're a unicorn, so I'd say we're on schedule. Hir on the roof is Haw, the big one at the door is Yarrow, that's Madder, and the small one is Teasel. Haw, come down."

"I'm fine up here!" called the one on the roof. Its voice was very loud and delighted.

"Come down anyway."

It came down. It came down the corner of the barn face first, hand over hand over hand, and dropped the last two metres onto the stubble in a heap, and got up laughing. It walked straight up to Twilight and looked at her from very close, and before she could step back it had put out a hand and touched her mane, once, lightly, as though checking it was real.

"It's all one colour at the ends," it said. "Why is it a different colour at the ends?"

"Haw," said Madder, from beside the door, without raising its voice at all.

Haw took the hand back. It didn't look sorry. It looked as if a question had been taken away from it before it was finished.

---

She asked her questions in order.

Food first. Sloe answered it, and did it well, without being asked twice: eggs, walnuts, beans at a push, anything that had been an animal if they could get it. Not greens. They had tried greens on the second day, from Fluttershy's garden. "It went badly," Sloe said. "Haw will tell you how badly, in detail, if you let hir. Don't let hir."

"How much, in a day?"

Sloe's ears came forward, both of them, slowly, and stayed there a moment. "At yesterday's prices, for all five, about sixty bits. If it's walnuts. More if it's eggs."

Twilight did the arithmetic before she could stop herself. The chit was twenty-five a week. She said nothing about it, and neither did Sloe, and she saw that Sloe had seen her do it.

Water: the river. Madder asked her, before she could move on, how far upstream the mill was and whether the mill put anything into the river, and when she said she didn't think so, it asked who would know. She told it the miller. It asked the miller's name. It asked what hour the mill started. She realised, somewhere around the third question, that she was being interviewed back, and that she didn't mind. It was like talking to one of the senior archivists in Canterlot, who never wanted to know what you thought, only what you knew and where you'd got it.

Cold at night: "Yes," Sloe said. "It's very cold. We've got each other. It's fine." Then, as if that had been too short: "It's the first cold any of us have had. We didn't know ears did that."

Anyone ill: there was the pause, a proper one this time. "No," Madder said.

The small one had come a little closer while they talked. It sat at the edge of the others with its long tail wrapped round its own feet, and it was watching the sandwich. Twilight had taken it out of her saddlebag again without thinking, and was holding the second half in her mouth by the crust. The small one watched it go up to her mouth and come down. It watched her chew. When she looked straight at it, it looked away at the river.

She took the sandwich out of her mouth. "Would you like—"

"We can't," Sloe said, gently. "Thank you. It's the hay. Teasel knows."

"I'm fine," the small one said. Its voice was quiet and careful. "Thank you."

Its ribs showed. The others were lean, but on the small one she could have counted them, down one side, where the fur lay close. She put the sandwich away and wanted, suddenly, to write that down before she forgot it, along with the sixty bits and the miller, so she opened her saddlebag with her magic and lifted out her notebook and her quill, the way she had done every morning of her life, and let them hang in the air in front of her while she found the page.

All five of them went still.

It was not the stillness from before, when she had come across the stubble. That had been a turning. This was a stopping, and it came so evenly together that the quiet it left behind was loud: she could hear the river, and a swallow somewhere inside the barn. Their ears went flat against their heads, all ten of them, and then, slowly, out wide to either side, stiff, the way a cat's ears go when it hears a dog through a wall. The big one at the door took its hand off the frame and stepped back, one step, off the sill and into the shadow. The small one made a sound, high and thin, through its nose. Haw had been standing near Sloe; its tail came round Sloe's hind leg and wound there, twice, tight.

Nobody said anything. The notebook turned a page by itself in the air, because she had asked it to before she'd noticed, and all five pairs of eyes went to the page.

*Oh*, she thought. *Of course.*

The story in the market that week had a horn in it now. She had heard it on Tuesday from two different ponies, neither of whom had been there: something about green fire, and a light from a horn, and a crack like a tree coming down. The fillies who had started it had long since stopped saying anything at all, and the story had gone on without them. Whatever had happened to these five in the forest, perhaps it had looked like this. Perhaps it had had a horn. Or perhaps there were no unicorns where they came from, and a quill that stood up in the air on its own was one more thing in a week of things.

She felt it anyway, small and sharp under her ribs, like being flinched from by a foal at the library desk.

She let the quill down into her saddlebag, and the notebook after it, slowly, so as not to make it worse. Then she dug one-hoofed in the bottom of the bag until she found the stub of pencil that lived there, and put it in her mouth, and took the notebook out with her hoof and balanced it open on her foreleg.

"Sixty," she said round the pencil. "Bits. A day. And the miller."

It was a long moment before the ears came in. Then Madder said, "Yes. Go on," and the big one came back onto the sill, and put its hand back on the door frame, where it had been. Haw's tail stayed where it was.

She wrote the rest of the visit like that. Pencil writing had never been her best, and pencil writing held in the teeth was worse, so that her sixes came out like her zeros and the page got damp at one corner. She didn't mention it. None of them did either.

She asked the names again, for the form she didn't have yet, and Madder gave its own as *Madder-four*, very clearly, and waited for her to write the four. Nobody else gave a number. She wrote down what she was given.

Sloe walked her back across the stubble to the road. At the edge of the field it stopped, and she stopped, and it looked back at the barn, where the others were watching them go.

"Twilight," it said. "The pen."

"I'm sorry about the pen."

"No. It was—" A pause, longer than any of the others. "It was like weather. That's all. It was like weather, up close." Its ears had gone forward. They came back. "Sorry. That's all I've got."

She didn't understand it, and she didn't think she was meant to. She wrote it down anyway, at the bottom of the damp page, *like weather*, with the pencil, and Sloe watched her do it and said nothing, and then went back across the field to the others.

---

The Mayor already knew.

"Sixty," she said, when Twilight told her, and took her spectacles off and rubbed the bridge of her nose. "I had it at fifty. I'd hoped fifty." She had a ledger open on the blotter with a column in it headed *Visitors*, and the column had four entries and a great deal of empty space. "Twilight, the chit is twenty-five because the poor fund is a hundred and ten bits for the whole spring. For the whole town. There are two widows on it and the Dawdle family and the Pinwheel colt who broke his leg."

"I'll write to Canterlot."

"Please do."

She wrote that afternoon, a proper letter, to the Royal Treasury rather than to the Princess, with the figures set out in a table and a note on the prices. She sent it by Spike. Then she did six index cards before supper and one after, which made four hundred and nineteen.

---

The miller came into the library on the sixth morning with his hat on.

It was a flat grey felt hat with a band that had once been green, and he kept it on the whole time he stood at her desk, and twice, when he got to the part he minded most, he took it off and hit his own leg with it and then put it back on.

"On the roof," he said. "At dawn. I come round the corner with the barrow and there it is, sat up on the ridge over the wheel, looking down at the race like it's thinking of going in. My shingles are older than you are, Miss Sparkle. It goes through them, it goes in the race, and then what? Then I've got a—" He took the hat off. He hit his leg with it. "—one of those, in my wheel."

"I'll speak to them."

"I'd be obliged."

She walked out that afternoon and spoke to them. Madder listened, and said "Understood," once, and while Madder said it she could see Haw on the barn roof behind it, lying flat along the ridge with her chin on her hands, listening too. When Madder turned round, Haw came down. Twilight didn't know whether that was the end of it. She suspected not.

On the way back she passed a mare she knew from the Tuesday reading group, walking her colt the long way round by the top field to the school. The long way was a mile further. The mare saw Twilight see it.

"I'm sure they're very nice," the mare said, and kept walking.

---

Spike had chalked his slate again. It hung on a nail by the library door where customers could see it, and it said, in his best capitals: *POLISHING — rubys, sapphires, anything shiny. NO opals (to fiddly).* Underneath, smaller, there was a price list, and under that he had rubbed out something and written it again.

He was at the back table with a sapphire the size of a hazelnut and a square of chamois, working at the same facet with the same small circular movement he had been using when she went out.

"Is that Rarity's?" she said.

"Mm. Thirty of those. For the big Canterlot thing." He held it up to the lamp and looked through it, and put it down and went on. "She says she'll settle up when Canterlot settles up with her. She said that last week too."

"That's good," Twilight said. She was looking for the Treasury's reply in the post, which had not come. "That's a big order for you."

"Yeah," Spike said. He didn't say anything else about it. He went on with the facet.

She took a cup of tea upstairs and left it on the stairs when she stopped to find a pencil.

---

The reply from Canterlot came on the seventh day. It was a printed form with a covering note, and the covering note said that the matter was *being considered*, and that the enclosed form should be completed in full and returned to expedite it. The form wanted the number of persons, the daily requirement per person in bits, the period for which relief was sought, and in a box at the bottom marked *Names of persons (with numbers)*, their names.

She did the rest of it at the kitchen table after supper and stopped at the box.

*With numbers.* It might mean how many there were. It might mean an age, or a registry number, which was what Canterlot usually meant. It might mean Madder-four. She had Madder-four, and she had four other names with nothing after them, and she did not know whether the others had numbers they hadn't given her, or whether the four was the kind of thing you didn't ask about, or whether putting it on a Treasury form would be the wrong thing to have done with it.

It was nearly eleven. The index was upstairs. She had done no cards that day.

She put the form in her saddlebag, and her scarf round her neck, and went out.

---

It was four kilometres. She had never walked the river road at night, and there was a moon, and the fields were grey and silver under it, and the smoke over the forest edge had thinned to almost nothing. Somewhere across the water an owl was working the tree line. She thought about the form, and then, since it was dark and nopony could see her do it, about the chapter in *The Winter Orchard* where the sisters find the letters in the wall, which she had been meaning to reread since Christmas, and then about the form again.

The barn was dark. The big door that Yarrow had rehung stood open a crack, a hand's width, as if it had swung a little on its own after somepony pulled it to. She stood outside it and knocked on the frame, very softly, and nothing answered.

She lit her horn, low, and put her head round the door.

They were at the back of the barn, in the straw. At first she couldn't tell where one of them stopped. They lay in one heap, curled in toward the middle with their foreheads together, almost touching, and their tails had come round the outside and been wound through each other, over and under, so that the whole of them was tied up in one knot. Sloe was on the near side. Twilight could see her face. Her eyes were shut, and her mouth was a little open.

None of them was moving.

She waited for the sides to rise. When a pony sleeps you see it, even in bad light, a long slow lift of the barrel and a longer fall. She looked at Sloe's flank. She looked at Haw's, where it showed over the top of the heap. She looked at the big one's back, which was the broadest thing there and should have been the easiest to see. Nothing lifted. Nothing fell. The straw under them did not stir.

She counted them. Five. She counted again, as though a sixth might be breathing.

The light from her horn lay across them, very steady, and the swallows up in the dark shifted on the beam and were quiet. She thought, quite clearly: *the doctor on Mulberry Lane*. She thought that it was four kilometres back, and four out again, and that the doctor was sixty and did not like being woken. She thought of the letter she would have to write, and began it, and found she could not get past the part where she said how many.

Five, in her town. All at once. After she had asked them, in order, whether anyone was ill.

She went in two steps and stopped. She didn't know what she would do when she got to them. Her horn was still lit.

A shiver went through the heap.

It went through all five of them at once, from the foreheads outward, one movement in five bodies, like a single animal shuddering in its sleep. The tails drew in tighter round each other, all together, the knot pulling close. Then it was over, and they were still again, just as still as before, and nothing rose or fell.

Twilight stood where she was.

After a while, when nothing else happened, she understood that it had been a movement, and that the dead don't make movements, and that she was going to believe this whether or not she understood the rest. She put her light out. In the dark she could hear the river and the swallows. She could not hear them breathe.

She backed out of the door. She pulled it to, carefully, until it was open the same hand's width it had been before. Then she walked back along the river road to the town, and passed the mill, where a lamp was on in the house, and passed the fountain, where the bell hung dark on its post, and let herself into the library, and did not go to Mulberry Lane.

---

Spike was asleep in his basket at the foot of the stairs, curled round the chamois, with the sapphire in his fist.

The index was open on the desk where she had left it that morning. The card on top was half written: *Herbal (partial). Forty-one leaves, vellum, hoof unknown; binding later, calf over boards; three leaves*, and then nothing, where she had stopped to go down for the post. The rubber band lay beside the box with its knot turned up. There was a cup of tea on the windowsill, and one on the stairs, and one by her elbow on the desk, and all three had skins on them.
