# X01 — Chapter 5
**POV:** Twilight · **Form:** Chapter

---

Spike was halfway through the business of Mrs. Hay Bale's brooch when he held out the letter, and he didn't stop talking while he did it.

"—so I told her it's not a garnet, it's glass, and she said her mother paid four bits for it at the Cloudsdale fair, so I said it's very nice glass. That came up at six. It's got the seal on. I didn't charge her in the end. For the brooch, I mean. You can't charge somepony for glass."

"No," Twilight said. She had the letter open before he'd finished. It was short, as the Princess's letters were when she was busy, and most of it was about the weather in Canterlot. One line in the middle was not.

*The Mayor tells me you have visitors, and that she is at her wits' end; would you look after them for me?*

She read it twice. Then she put it down on the desk beside the shoebox, and her eye went to the shoebox without being asked.

It had been a boot box once, for a pair of Rarity's winter boots, and now it held the index, which was to have one card for every manuscript in the royal library's catalogue of pre-Nightmare holdings, and there were about three thousand manuscripts. The cards went in the Princess's own preferred order, which was not alphabetical and took a page of notes to explain. A length of string held the lid on. When it frayed through in the second week of March, Twilight had knotted it, and the knot sat on top of the lid like a small hard bead. Inside there were four hundred and twelve cards. She had counted them on Sunday, twice.

Spike thought it was nearly finished. She had told him so in February.

"Is it the index?" he said. "Does she want it early?"

"No. It's the visitors." She took a fresh sheet and wrote *Visitors* at the top of it, and under that, in a column: *Food. Water. Warm enough at night? Anyone ill.* She looked at it. Under that she wrote *Index, two hours after*, and underlined it. Then she turned her notebook over and opened it from the back, and wrote *Questions (not to ask)* across the top of the last page. Under that she wrote *Where are they from?* The quill was already on the next line. She lifted it off, shut the notebook, and went downstairs and made a cup of tea, which she left on the windowsill when Spike called up to ask where the good polishing cloth had gone.

---

The river road went out of town past the mill, and after the mill there was nothing on it but fields.

She heard the market bell go for eight behind her as she passed the last fence. It was a flat, cracked bell on a post by the fountain, and she had stopped wincing at it some time in her second month. Across the river the Everfree stood up dark, with a brownish haze still hanging in front of it, thin as weak tea. The weather team had set a line of clouds low over the treetops to keep them damp. Somepony had laid them out ruler-straight, which she approved of. One pegasus was out, very small, nudging a cloud back into line. Twilight watched it until the road bent.

She ate half her sandwich as she walked. It was cress and hay on brown bread. She had made it at six, and it had gone a little soft.

The seed barn stood by itself in a stubble field, with a willow at the far end where the field met the water. She had passed it a hundred times without looking at it. Now there was somepony on the ridge of the roof, or something, very high up, and as she came off the road into the stubble she saw that it was hanging off the ridge by its back feet, head down, looking at the river.

Then it saw her, and it went still.

So did the others. She had not seen them until they stopped moving: one at the big door with its hand on the doorpost, one sitting in the sun against the wall, two at the corner of the barn by a heap of walnut shells. Every head came round to her together. It was so quick and so level that she stopped in the stubble with one hoof up.

"Hello," she said. "I'm Twilight Sparkle. From the library. The Princess asked me to—" She had rehearsed this on the road and it had gone. "To see how you're getting on."

The one by the wall got up. Its ears stood straight out to either side, wide and stiff, and Twilight's heart sank a little. On a pony that meant somepony was put out, and she hadn't said anything yet. It walked toward her on its back four legs with its front hands held up off the ground. It was thinner than she'd expected from the stories, and dustier, and its eyes were tired.

"Twilight," it said. There was a pause before it, but not much of one. "I'm Sloe. Come in, come in. We don't bite the hoof that feeds us." It seemed pleased with that. "Hir on the roof is Haw, the big one at the door is Yarrow, that's Madder, and Teasel. Haw, come down."

Teasel was the smaller of the two at the corner. It ducked its head when its name was said.

"I'm fine up here!" called the one on the roof. Its voice was very loud and delighted.

"Come down anyway."

It came down. It came down the corner of the barn face first, hand over hand over hand, dropped the last six feet or so onto the stubble in a heap, and got up laughing. Then it walked straight up to Twilight and looked at her from very close. Before she could step back it had put out a hand and touched her mane, once, lightly.

Twilight jerked her head away. She took a step back into the stubble without meaning to.

"It's a different colour at the ends," it said. "Why is it a different colour at the ends?"

Haw's hand came back to its chest. Nobody had said anything. It came back all at once, the way a pony's hoof does when somepony has told her to put that down. Then Madder said "Haw," from the corner, not loudly. It came a beat late. Madder was looking at Twilight while it said it.

Twilight didn't know what to make of the order of that. Haw didn't look sorry. It was still looking at her mane.

---

She asked her questions in order.

Haw had sat down against Sloe's side and was leaning on it with its whole weight. Sloe didn't seem to notice. Teasel sat hunched at the edge of the others with its tail pulled in close underneath it. Madder had one hind hand resting on its back, flat, the way you'd rest a hoof on a stack of books to keep it from sliding.

Food first. Sloe answered it, and did it well, without being asked twice: eggs, walnuts, beans at a push, anything that had been an animal if they could get it. Not greens. They had tried greens on the second day, from Fluttershy's garden.

"It went badly," Sloe said. "Very. Haw was— no. Ask Haw. Don't ask Haw."

"How much, in a day?"

Sloe's ears came forward, both of them, slowly, and stayed there a moment. "At yesterday's prices, for all five of us, about forty-five bits a day. That's if we can get walnuts for half of it. Walnuts are cheap and they keep. If it's all eggs it's seventy. Seventy-two if the egg mare's in a mood, and she's been in a mood. And there aren't enough walnuts. I think we've had all of Ponyville's, apart from the ones in cakes."

Twilight did the arithmetic before she could stop herself. The chit was twenty-five a week. She said nothing about it, and neither did Sloe, and she saw that Sloe had seen her do it.

Water was the river. Before she could move on, Madder asked her how far upstream the mill was. Then it asked whether the mill put anything into the river. When she said she didn't think so, it asked who would know. She told it the miller. It asked the miller's name.

"Does the wheel go all the way r—" Haw began, and stopped dead with its mouth open. Nobody had said a word to it. It shut its mouth.

Madder asked what hour the mill started. Somewhere around there Twilight realised she was being interviewed back. She didn't mind. It was like talking to the senior archivists in Canterlot, who took notes while you talked and asked where you'd got it.

Cold at night: "Yes," Sloe said. "It's very cold. We've got each other. It's fine." Then, as if that had been too short: "It's the first cold any of us have had. The ears are the worst. They go—" It made a flat shape with both hands against its own head, and let the rest go.

Anyone ill: there was the pause, a proper one this time. "No," Madder said.

Teasel had come a little closer while they talked, and Madder's hand had come with it. It was watching the sandwich. Twilight had taken it out of her saddlebag again without thinking, and was holding the second half in her mouth by the crust. Teasel watched it go up to her mouth and come down. It watched her chew. When she looked straight at it, it looked away at the river.

She took the sandwich out of her mouth. "Would you like—"

"We can't," Sloe said, gently. "Thank you. It's the hay. Teasel knows."

"I'm fine," Teasel said. Its voice was quiet and careful. "Thank you."

Its ribs showed. The others were lean, but on Teasel she could have counted them, down one side, where the fur lay close. She put the sandwich away. She lifted her notebook and her quill out of the saddlebag with her magic, the way she had done every morning of her life, and let them hang in the air in front of her while she found the page. She wanted the ribs written down before she forgot them, along with the forty-five bits and the miller.

All five of them went still.

Their ears went flat against their heads, all ten of them, the way hers went in a gale, as if something were blowing straight at them. Then, slowly, the ears went out wide to either side and stayed there, stiff. The big one at the door took its hand off the doorpost and stepped back, one step, off the sill and into the shadow. Teasel made a sound, high and thin, through its nose. Madder huffed once, short and hard. Haw had been leaning on Sloe; its tail came round Sloe's hind leg and wound there, twice, tight. Twilight could hear the river, and a swallow somewhere inside the barn.

Nobody said anything. The notebook turned a page by itself in the air. She had asked it to before she'd noticed. All five pairs of eyes went to the page.

*Oh*, she thought. *Of course.*

The story in the market that week had a horn in it now. She had heard it that morning from two different ponies, neither of whom had been there: green fire, a light from a horn, a crack like a tree coming down. Perhaps whatever had happened to them in the forest had looked like this. Or perhaps they had never seen magic at all.

It hurt anyway, small and sharp under her ribs. Foals fell asleep on her at story hour.

She let the quill down into her saddlebag, and the notebook after it, slowly, so as not to make it worse. Then she dug one-hoofed in the bottom of the bag until she found the stub of pencil that lived there, and put it in her mouth, and took the notebook out with her hoof and balanced it open on her foreleg.

"Forty-five," she said round the pencil. "Bits. A day. And the miller."

The ears came in slowly, one pair at a time. Sloe's came first. Yarrow's came last, back in the shadow of the door. In between, the five of them looked at each other, one to the next and not at her, as if something were being passed along. Then Madder said, "Yes. Go on." The big one came back onto the sill and put its hand on the doorpost again, where it had been.

Haw's tail stayed where it was. Haw leaned toward her saddlebag as far as the tail would let it, with one hand out. "Can it do that with anything? Could it do a—"

Sloe put a hand flat on Haw's chest without looking round. Haw stopped.

She wrote the rest of the visit like that. Pencil had never been her best, and pencil held in the teeth was worse, so that her sixes came out like her zeros and the page got damp at one corner. She didn't mention it. None of them did either. Once, while all their eyes were on Madder, she turned to the back and wrote *the pause before they answer*, and under it *five heads at once*, and turned back before anybody looked round.

She asked the names again, for the form she didn't have yet. Madder gave its own as *Madder-four*, very clearly, and waited for her to write the four. She wrote it. The next word was already in her mouth behind the pencil, and it was *why*. She took the pencil out.

"With a hyphen?"

"Yes," Madder said.

*Look after*, the Princess had written. Not *look into*. Nobody else gave a number. She wrote down what she was given.

Sloe walked her back across the stubble to the road. At the edge of the field it stopped, and she stopped, and it looked back at the barn, where the others were watching them go.

"Twilight," it said. "The pen."

"I'm sorry about the pen."

"No. It was—" A pause, longer than any of the others. "It was like weather. It was like weather, up close. It's—" Its ears went forward and stayed forward a while. They came back with nothing. "No. Like weather."

She didn't understand it. She wrote it down anyway, at the bottom of the damp page, *like weather*, with the pencil. Sloe watched her do it and said nothing. Then it went back across the field to the others.

On the road, out of sight of the barn, she turned to the back and added *the four*, then copied *like weather* under it, then *the ears*. By the mill she had added *Teasel's ribs*, which was not a question.

"Not to ask," she said round the pencil, to nopony, and put the book away.

---

The Mayor already knew.

"Forty-five," she said, when Twilight told her, and took her spectacles off and rubbed the bridge of her nose. "I had it at thirty. I'd hoped thirty." She had a ledger open on the blotter with a column in it headed *Visitors*. The column had four entries and a great deal of empty space. "Twilight, the chit is twenty-five because the poor fund is a hundred and ten bits for the whole spring. For the whole town. There are two widows on it and the Dawdle family and the Pinwheel colt who broke his leg."

"I'll write to Canterlot."

"Please do."

She wrote that afternoon, a proper letter, to the Royal Treasury rather than to the Princess. She set the figures out in a table, with a note on the prices, and sent it by Spike. Then she did six index cards before supper, which made four hundred and eighteen.

---

After supper she meant to do twenty. She did one, and then went for *Hoofwright's Bestiary*, as a break between cards.

Hoofwright was a book for fillies, and had nothing. After it she took down the older bestiaries from the top shelf, then the travellers' accounts, then the zoology shelf. She was looking for six limbs, for hands where the hooves should be, and for anything that paused before it answered. Some time after midnight she unrolled the old atlas of the lands past the Everfree and went along its edges, where the cartographer had drawn mountains and then stopped drawing.

There was nothing. There was not a footnote. The travellers had been everywhere and eaten everything, and not one of them had seen a creature with hands on its feet.

She found that her forehooves were pressed flat on the atlas, holding it down. She lifted them. The edge curled a little, and she put them back.

She opened her notebook from the back and read what she had.

*Where are they from?*  
*the pause before they answer*  
*five heads at once*  
*the four*  
*like weather*  
*the ears*  
*Teasel's ribs*

The clock on the landing struck three. The count stood at four hundred and nineteen.

In the morning Spike put the books back an armful at a time, with a small noise in his throat at every shelf.

"Leave the ones on the desk."

He looked at the desk, and left them. "They're library books," he told the shelf. "They live on shelves."

---

The miller came into the library on the sixth morning with his hat on.

It was a flat grey felt hat with a band that had once been green, and he kept it on the whole time he stood at her desk. Twice, when he got to the part he minded most, he took it off and hit his own leg with it and then put it back on.

"On the roof," he said. "At dawn. I come round the corner with the barrow and there it is, sat up on the ridge over the wheel, looking down at the race like it's thinking of going in. Two and a half mile it's come, Miss Sparkle. From that barn, in the pitch dark, all along the river road, to sit on my roof. There's roofs out there. And my shingles are older than you are. It goes through them, it goes in the race, and then what? Then I've got a—" He took the hat off. He hit his leg with it. "—one of those, in my wheel."

"I'll speak to them."

"I'd be obliged."

She walked out that afternoon and spoke to them. Madder listened, and said "Understood," once. While Madder said it she could see Haw on the barn roof behind it, lying flat along the ridge with her chin on her hands, listening too. When Madder turned round, Haw came down. Twilight didn't know whether that was the end of it.

On the way back she passed a mare she knew from the Tuesday reading group. The mare was walking her colt the long way round by the top field to the school. The long way was a mile further. Her ears went back a little when she saw Twilight coming from the barn.

"I'm sure they're very nice," the mare said, and kept walking.

The colt looked back over his shoulder at the barn roof all the way up the field. Twilight stood in the road until they were over the top.

---

She got home a little after four. Spike had chalked his slate again. It hung on a nail by the library door where customers could see it, and it said, in his best capitals: *POLISHING — rubys, sapphires, anything shiny. NO opals (to fiddly).* Underneath, smaller, there was a price list. Under that he had rubbed something out and written it again.

He was at the back table with a sapphire the size of a hazelnut and a square of chamois. He was working at the same facet, with the same small circular movement, that he had been using when she went out.

"Is that Rarity's?" she said.

"Mm. Thirty of those. For the big Canterlot thing." He held it up to the lamp and looked through it, and put it down and went on. "She says she'll settle up when Canterlot settles up with her. She said that last week too."

"That's good," Twilight said. She was looking for the Treasury's reply in the post, which had not come. "That's a big order for you."

"Yeah," Spike said. He went on with the facet.

She took a cup of tea upstairs and left it on the stairs when she stopped to find a pencil.

---

The reply from Canterlot came on the seventh day. It was a printed form with a covering note. The note said that the matter was *being considered*, and that the enclosed form should be completed in full and returned to expedite it. The form wanted the number of persons, the daily requirement per person in bits, and the period for which relief was sought. In a box at the bottom marked *Names of persons (with numbers)*, it wanted their names.

She did the rest of it at the kitchen table after supper and stopped at the box.

*With numbers.* It might mean how many there were. It might mean an age, or a registry number, which was what Canterlot usually meant. It might mean Madder-four. She had Madder-four, and she had four other names with nothing after them. She did not know whether the others had numbers they hadn't given her. She did not know whether the four was the kind of thing you didn't ask about, or whether putting it on a Treasury form would be the wrong thing to have done with it.

It was nearly eleven. The index was upstairs. She had done no cards that day.

The form was a perfectly good reason to walk out to the barn, and she let it be the reason. She put the form in her saddlebag, and her scarf round her neck, and went out.

---

It was two and a half miles, and she had never walked the river road at night. There was a moon, and the fields were grey and silver under it. The smoke over the forest edge had thinned to almost nothing. Somewhere across the water an owl was working the tree line. She thought about the form, and then, since it was dark and nopony could see her do it, about the chapter in *The Winter Orchard* where the sisters find the letters in the wall, which she had been meaning to reread since Christmas. Then she thought about the form again.

The barn was dark. The big door stood open a crack, a hand's width, as if it had swung a little on its own after somepony pulled it to. She stood outside it and knocked on the doorpost, very softly. Nothing answered.

She lit her horn, low, and put her head round the door.

They were in the far corner, in a hollow they had trodden into the old straw. At first she couldn't tell where one of them stopped. They lay in one heap, curled in toward the middle with their foreheads together, almost touching. Their tails had come round the outside and been wound through each other, over and under. Sloe was on the near side. Twilight could see her face. Her eyes were shut, and her mouth was a little open.

None of them was moving.

She waited for the sides to rise. She was looking for the slow lift of a barrel, the way Spike's went in his basket, and the longer fall. She looked at Sloe's flank. She looked at Haw's, where it showed over the top of the heap. She looked at the big one's back, which was the broadest thing there and should have been the easiest to see. Nothing lifted. Nothing fell. The straw under them did not stir.

She counted them. Five. She counted again, as though a sixth might be breathing.

The light from her horn lay across them, very steady. Up in the dark the swallows shifted on the beam and were quiet. She thought, quite clearly: *the doctor on Mulberry Lane*. It was two and a half miles back to his door. Then it was two and a half miles out again, with him, and he was sixty and did not like being woken. After that there would be a letter. She began it in her head, and found she could not get past the part where she said how many.

Five, in her town. All at once.

She went in two steps and stopped. She didn't know what she would do when she got to them. Her horn was still lit.

The heap breathed.

All five of them breathed in at once, one long slow breath, so that the whole heap swelled and the tails drew in tight round each other. It went on longer than any breath she had ever taken. Then it went out of them, all together, just as slowly, and they were as still as before, and nothing rose or fell.

Twilight stood where she was.

She counted, without meaning to, the way she counted anything. She had got to ninety-one when the next one came, all five at once again, as long and as slow as the first. Some part of her had been taking notes the whole time: the foreheads together, the tails wound over and under, and now ninety-one. Her face went hot, and she hated that part of her a little. She did not wait for a third. She put her light out. In the dark she could hear the river and the swallows, and nothing else.

She backed out of the door. She pulled it to, carefully, until it was open about as far as she had found it. Then she walked back along the river road to the town. She passed the mill, where a lamp was on in the house, and the fountain, where the bell hung dark on its post. She let herself into the library, and did not go to Mulberry Lane.

---

Spike was asleep in his basket at the foot of the stairs, curled round the chamois, with the sapphire in his fist.

The index was open on the desk where she had left it that morning. The card on top was half written: *Herbal (partial). Forty-one leaves, vellum, hoof unknown; binding later, calf over boards; three leaves*, and then nothing, where she had stopped to go down for the post. The string lay beside the box with its knot turned up. The books were still piled at the other end of the desk, open at the pages she had stopped on, and one of the bestiaries had slid off the pile onto the card. There was a cup of tea by her elbow, with a skin on it.
