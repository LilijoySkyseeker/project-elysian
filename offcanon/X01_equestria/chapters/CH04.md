# X01 — Chapter 4
**POV:** Sloe-5 · **Form:** Chapter

---

Fluttershy had brought nineteen eggs at four o'clock, under a cloth in a basket, and set them down at the door of the duck house without coming in. With them came a sack of sunflower seed that had a painted finch on the front. Sloe had watched her decide about the sack. She had stood at the edge of the long grass with it on her back, looking from the finch to the five of them, before she put it down and went.

That was yesterday. This morning Sloe had the eggs out on the boards in rows of five, which was how hir had laid out stores for fifty years: the gap at the end of a row told you what you were short. Three rows, and four over. Hir had weighed the seed by lifting it, and called it a little over a kilo.

Hir ears came forward while hir asked.

*Seven thousand two hundred, approximately,* the Core said.

Hir asked what five of them burned in a day, on this ground. Hir knew, but hir wanted to hear it said by something that wasn't hungry.

*Ten thousand, approximately.*

So it was most of one day, and nothing at all for the day after. Hir had run stores on worse.

*[Is it all eggs? Are they all eggs? Can I have the cracked one?]* Haw had hir head in through the door, which was the only part of hir that fitted.

*[Nobody has anything till I've counted]*

*[You've counted. I felt you count. You did it twice]*

*[Three times ~ not sorry at all]*

The duck house had been built for ducks. The five of them had slept in it the way cargo goes into a hold that is one pallet too small: Yarrow's hindquarters out of the door, Madder wedged in the corner, and the rest of them fitted between. It smelled of old feathers, damp straw and something sweet that the Core could not name.

Hir gave out three eggs each, a handful of seed each, and kept the four over and the rest of the seed back against the evening. They ate the eggs whole, shells and all. Haw ate hirs in one go and then looked at the four over for so long that Sloe moved them behind hir tail.

Through the frame the hunger came in from all four of them, a dull pull under the ribs, and went round and back to hir so that hir could not have said how much of it was hir own. Under that, in Teasel, there was something else. It was low and steady, like a pump running in a compartment nobody used. Sloe went looking for what it was attached to and found nothing.

*[I'm fine,]* Teasel sent, clean. *[Is anyone else hungry?]*

*[Everybody is,]* Sloe sent. *[You've eaten yours. I watched you]*

*[I know. I only wondered]*

Madder had been sitting in the doorway with hir back to them, looking up the meadow at the cottage roof.

*[Sloe. Food status, by dusk,]* hir sent. Then, after a moment: *[And a roof. This one's a duck's. I have been in charge of a duck house for fourteen hours, and I would like to hand it back]*

*[Logged ~ laughing]* hir sent, and Madder's ears moved very slightly, which was as much as Madder laughed.

---

*Three point four kilometres,* the Core said, when hir asked it at the end of Fluttershy's path.

On the ship, three point four kilometres was a spine corridor and back, pulling along the rail with one hand and drinking something with another. Here every step was a matter of lifting sixty kilos off the ground and putting it down somewhere else, then doing it again. Hir went on four with hir arms up, as Madder did, until the first bend took hir out of sight of the cottage. After that hir came down onto six and stayed there. The sun came up over the fields on the left, where somepony had planted something in long green rows, and hir ears went out wide and stiff and stayed out. Twice hir sat down in the verge and let hir tail lie in the road behind hir.

*[How far are you? What can you see? Is it a town yet? Are there lots of them?]* Haw sent, from the meadow.

*[Grass,]* hir sent. *[More grass. A post with a number on it ~ tired, and hiding it badly]*

Four people felt hir sit down in the verge the second time, and nobody said so.



The town began with a fence and then a house, and then houses on both sides. They were round-shouldered and bright, with thatch on them like the grass on Fluttershy's roof, and window boxes full of things the Core called *ornamental*. There was a mare out front of the third house, sweeping her step. She stopped sweeping when she saw hir, and went in with the broom and shut the door. Across the road a shutter came to. Further up, somepony put her head out of a door, said a name sharply, and a foal Sloe had not even seen went in past her legs.

Hir walked up the middle of the road on four, with hir arms up and hir ears as close to hir head as the sun would let them go.

Nopony said anything to hir. What hir got instead came through walls, which ponies did not seem to know were thin. Hir heard: *—out of the fire, the girls said—*, and *—more legs than a—*, and *—teeth, all teeth, Scootaloo says—*, and a door slammed on the rest. The fear-smell came off the houses in waves, sour and sharp, like a pump seal going. Hir had smelled it last night off the small running things in the forest and had not known what it was. Hir knew now.

*Six,* hir thought, about the legs, to nobody. *If anybody's asking.*

Nobody was. Sloe put hir hand over hir own wrist, where the ribbon was, and held it there until the street opened out.

The town hall stood in the square, taller than the rest, with a clock in its gable. The clock said four minutes to eleven. Hir ears came forward.

*Ten fifty-two,* the Core said.

Hir filed it. A clock that was wrong in public, where the whole town could see, was interesting. Either nopony could fix it, or nopony could agree who should.

---

The Mayor's office had a desk with a blotter on it, a window, and more paper than Sloe had seen in one place in hir life. The Mayor sat behind the desk with both her forehooves flat on the blotter. She was a tidy mare with her mane done up high and a pair of spectacles on a cord, and she smelled of the same sour thing as the street, held down under something like soap.

She did not offer to shake. Sloe, who had seen two ponies do it on the steps outside, did not either.

"Mayor," hir said. "I'm Sloe. There are five of us. We're at Fluttershy's, in a building for ducks. We'll need somewhere to live and something to eat, and I don't have any money. I'd like to talk about the second one first, if that's all right. The first one can wait a day."

The Mayor took a moment over that, as ponies did with everything hir said.

"I have had," she said, "a great many visitors this morning. The weather team captain has been in twice about overtime. I have had three families about their children." She straightened a pile of letters that was already straight. "I did not expect to have you."

"No," Sloe said. "Neither did we."

The Mayor looked at hir properly for the first time. Then she took a sheet of paper off the nearest stack and turned it round.

There was an old seed barn on the river road, she said. It had been empty since the co-op moved to the new building by the mill. It had a roof and four walls, and she could not promise more than that. It was, she said, a long way from anything, and she said it the way somepony says a thing that she has already decided is an advantage. For food there was a town chit. She wrote it out while she talked: *reasonable costs, food only, bearer to present to any trader in the town, not to exceed twenty-five bits in any one week, to be redeemed at quarter-day.* She signed it and blotted it, and slid it across without her hoof leaving the paper until the last moment.

Sloe read it twice. It was a good piece of paper. Hir would have been proud of it on the ship.

"When's quarter-day?" hir said.

"Midsummer."

Hir asked. *Nine weeks and four days,* the Core said.

So every trader who took it would be lending the town twenty-five bits for nine weeks, at no interest, to feed strangers the town hadn't asked for. Hir knew to the minute what a stallholder would think of that, because hir had issued paper like it hirself, and had watched the faces.

"Thank you," hir said, and meant it. "It's a good chit."

The Mayor looked as if she didn't know what to do with that. She rang a small bell, and a stallion came in from the outer office.

"Would you show it out, please," she said to him. "And find it the key for the seed barn." Then she turned to Sloe. "The clock's fast, by the way. Four minutes. Everypony knows. I'd hate you to think we didn't."

---

*[Roof,]* hir sent from the river road, in the afternoon. *[A barn, on the river. Four kilometres out of town and three from the meadow, so everybody walks. And paper that says the town will pay for food, in nine weeks ~ pleased with hirself for as long as that took, then less]*

*[A barn! How high? Can we climb it?]*

*[Moving by fifteen hundred,]* Madder sent. *[Yarrow, rear. Haw, you are not climbing anything until I've seen it]*

*[Door,]* Yarrow sent, from some way off. It was all hir sent, and it was not a question.

Sloe came in last, at dusk. The barn was long and grey, and stood alone in a field of old stubble with the river going by at the bottom. The big door was already hanging straighter than it had at noon. Inside, the air was cool and smelled of chaff and mice, and it went up and up into the dark, where things moved along the beams and made small sounds.

*[Swallows!]* Haw sent. *[Madder says the Core says swallows. There are nine. There were eleven, two went out. It's eleven metres to the ridge. I haven't climbed it. Madder, I haven't. Tell hir I haven't]*

*[Hir hasn't ~ as if it had cost hir a great deal]*

The four of them were at the back of the barn, in the straw, already a heap. Sloe did not send anything. Hir went across the floor on six and got in. Haw's arm came over hir and Teasel's tail came round hir leg, while somebody's hind hand found hir hip and stayed there. Hir had not been touched since the duck house door, which was nine hours ago, and hir had not known until now that it was a number hir was keeping.

Hir lay there doing the chit. Twenty-five bits a week. Nine weeks. Hir did it three times, and then a fourth, until Madder's hand came down flat on hir back without Madder waking, and hir stopped.

---

The stallion who drove the egg cart had told hir, the day before, when hir caught up with him on the way out of town. He did the bakery first, at five, he said, and he said it while he was already shaking the reins.

So Sloe was at the back of the bakery at a quarter to five, in an alley that smelled so strongly of bread that hir stomach turned over at it. It was dark and very cold. Hir had walked the four kilometres in the dark without trouble, since the dark was only geometry, but hir hands were numb from the road.

*[Sloe. Time,]* Madder sent, from the heap. Madder was always awake.

*[Four forty-six. Bakery ~ cold, and cheerful about it, mostly]*

The back door was propped open with a sack. Heat and light came out of it, and a noise of metal, and then a pony came out backwards, bumping the door wider with her rump, with a sieve in her mouth. She was pink under the flour. The flour went up to her elbows and over one ear. She banged the sieve twice on the step, turned round, and saw hir.

She stopped. Then she took the sieve out of her mouth.

"You're in the way of the flour," she said.

Sloe moved. The pony banged the sieve twice more where hir had been, and a cloud went up and settled on both of them.

"Sorry," Sloe said. "I'm Sloe. I'm here about the eggs."

"Pinkie." She looked hir up and down, all of hir, the way a stallholder looks at a load before she prices it. "The eggs aren't here yet. The eggs aren't anybody's except Mrs. Cake's, anyway. She has a contract. It's a whole thing."

"Who's the farmer?"

"Clover Patch. He comes Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays, and he won't sell at the door, because of the contract. Don't ask him. He'll just go red." She pushed the sack back against the door with a hind hoof. The frame beside her had a hoofprint in flour on it, at the height of her shoulder, and under the paint, if hir looked, there was another one just like it. "The ponies with too many hens are down by the fountain. Get there early. Everybody's got too many eggs in spring. Nobody knows what to do with them."

"I know what to do with them," Sloe said.

Pinkie looked at hir again. Her eyes went to Sloe's flank, where the fur lay closer over the ribs than it had on the ship, and stayed there a moment. She didn't say anything about it.

"Hey," she said instead. "Have you guys even had a—"

She looked back through the door. There was a slate on the wall inside with chalk on it: *2 x wedding (Sat). School, 90 buns. Filly Delight, sponge, NO nuts.* The word *Saturday* was underlined twice.

"Nope," Pinkie said. "Never mind. Door."

She went in. The door swung to on the sack. Sloe stood in the alley a little longer in the bread smell, with flour on hir ears, and then went to find the fountain.

---

The market was four rows of stalls round the fountain, with doors behind it into the streets. Hir did it the way hir had always done a market: once through without buying, to learn where the prices sat, and then back.

Eggs were everywhere, as Pinkie had said. A mare at a blue door sold hir a dozen for three bits and seemed glad to see them go. Two doors on, a grey mare wouldn't take the chit, and a colt at the fountain would. Cheese hir found once, at the dairy cart, and the dairy mare looked at the chit for a long time before she cut hir half a round. Every pony hir bought from took a long look at hir first, then a longer one at the paper, and nopony touched hir hand.

A stallion at the end of the second row sold only radishes. He had them in heaps, red and white, very clean. Hir couldn't eat a radish and told him so, and he didn't seem to mind. He told hir about his knee instead. He'd done it on the ice in the winter of the big freeze and it had never been right, and it was worse in the damp, and it told him when rain was coming better than the weather team did, no offence to them. Sloe asked him how long before rain. He considered it and said Thursday. Hir liked him.

By eight hir had used four bits of the chit, and hir arms were full.

*[Eggs. Two dozen. A cheese. The cheese is the best thing I've seen on this planet]*

*[Bring the cheese!]* Haw sent. *[Is it far? Can I come?]*

*[There are nine swallows,]* from Teasel, clean, and a moment later: *[I'm fine. Is anyone else hungry?]*

Under the jokes the frame held the hunger in all four of them, and the low, steady thing in Teasel, which had not moved since the morning before. Sloe went looking for its cause again, and again there was nothing to find.

*[Moving to the barn door,]* Madder sent. *[Yarrow has a hinge off it]*

*[Yarrow always has a hinge off something,]* Sloe sent, and felt Yarrow, from four kilometres, not bother to answer.

The general store would not take the chit at all. The storekeeper was a brown mare with a pencil behind each ear, and she had a list on the counter headed STOCK, with nothing written under it. She said so frankly: the town had never paid her on time in her life, and she wasn't starting with strangers.

Sloe looked at the list, then at the shelves.

"I can count that," hir said. "Your stock. All of it, today, written up. For bits, in the hand, at closing."

The storekeeper looked at hir for a while. "How much?"

Hir had been walking since four. Hir asked the Core what ten bits would buy at the blue door, and it told hir, and that looked like a lot.

"Ten," hir said.

"Done," said the storekeeper, very fast.

Hir counted until six. It was not quick. Hir had to be shown what half the things were, and the Core had never heard of the other half. At two the storekeeper said, to nopony, "I gave that Dawdle colt thirty last spring, and he missed a whole crate of nails." Hir was on the ladder with a tin of something called saddle soap in each hand when hir heard it. Hir took the thought to the end, then put the tins back.

Thirty. Hir had priced hir own day at a third of the going rate, in a town where hir had no other way to earn a bit. Hir laughed, up the ladder, a short chirr through the nose, and the storekeeper looked up.

"Nothing," Sloe said. "Tins."

---

The last stall to pack up was at the end of the fourth row. It had sacks of dried beans, a crate of walnuts and another of hazelnuts from last autumn's store, eggs in straw, and a cart behind it with a yoke and no pony in it yet. The mare behind the stall wore a hat. She was folding a cloth over the eggs when hir came up, and she didn't stop folding it.

"We're shutting," she said.

"I've got cash," Sloe said.

The mare stopped folding. She looked at hir, at the paper in hir hand and at the ten bits in the other. It was a flat look with nothing in it, and Sloe liked it at once. It was the look of somebody who was going to charge hir exactly what she meant to charge.

"Applejack," she said. "What do you want?"

"Sloe. Eggs, three dozen. Walnuts, as many as this buys. Beans."

Applejack weighed the walnuts on a pan scale hung from the stall's crossbar. The needle went past the line and she took one nut off, and then a second, until it sat on the line and stayed there. She tipped them into a sack and wrote the weight in a ledger that lay open on the board. The pencil was a stub tied to the corner post with a length of twine, and the twine was too short for her to reach the far column. She wrote the last figure with the ledger tipped up on its edge to meet it.

"Walnuts is five the pound," she said. "Eggs is four the dozen. Beans is two. You want to see me weigh the beans?"

"Yes, please," Sloe said.

Applejack almost smiled, and didn't. She weighed the beans.

It came to thirty-one, and a bit for the sack, which the chit would not cover, being a sack. Sloe put down seven bits and held the chit out. Applejack read it, without taking it, all the way to the bottom.

"Quarter-day," she said.

"Midsummer. Nine weeks."

"Town paid the last one of these in August." She took it anyway. She wrote *21 on chit* in the ledger, stood a moment looking at the paper, and put it under the cash box rather than in it. Then she counted the seven bits. She counted them again. She put them in the box, shut it, and had it under the board before Sloe had taken hir hand back. Sloe noticed that, and filed it, and did not know what hir had filed.

"That leaves you four short," Applejack said. "Call it three and the sack."

"Put me in the book," Sloe said. "I'll be back tomorrow."

"I don't doubt it." Applejack tipped the ledger up on its edge again to reach the column. "Name?"

"Sloe."

She wrote. Sloe read it upside down, since they had the same letters and it was only a matter of turning them round: *the strange one (Sloe). Seed barn. It says the town's good for it.*

Hir ears went out, though the sun had been down an hour.

*[Walnuts ~ very pleased, and very, very tired]* hir sent, on the river road, with the sack on hir back and the eggs in both arms. *[A kilo and a half, and beans, and three dozen eggs. Ask me what it comes to. Somebody ask me]*

*[What does it come to?]* four of them sent at once, Yarrow included, which Yarrow never did.

Hir asked the Core, although hir already knew. *Fourteen thousand six hundred, approximately.*

*[A day and a half,]* hir sent. *[One day of a market and it's a day and a half. It works. It can work]*

---

On the fourth day the mare at the blue door wanted five bits for a dozen, and she did not look glad.

The grey mare who had refused the chit said six. The colt at the fountain said he'd sold all his to a pony from the other side of town who'd heard eggs were going, and didn't have any. The radish stallion's radishes were the same price as the day before, since nopony wanted radishes any more than they had yesterday. His knee was a little better. He said he'd put Thursday back to Friday.

Sloe walked the rows once, the way hir always did, and the prices were all in the wrong places. Hir did not need to reach for anything to know why. It was the first thing hir had learned on the ship, in hir first year on the stores deck: you did not go into a small market with your own face, and buy all of one thing in one day. You spread it. You sent somebody else. You bought through a third hand, and you certainly did not go back the next morning. Hir had taught it to three apprentices since. Hir had done it yesterday in front of the whole town, with a sack on hir back, because hir had been up since four and the numbers had been going so well.

Applejack's eggs were six the dozen, and her walnuts were six the pound.

"You put it up," Sloe said.

"I did." Applejack didn't look away. "You bought near every egg on this row yesterday. Everypony here's heard about it. Mae's asking five at her door, and Granite's asking six. I'm not selling under the whole row for anypony." She moved the pencil on its twine. "And there's your paper. I'll wait till summer for twenty-one bits, and that's twenty-one bits I don't have in spring. That costs me too."

"The chit's gone," Sloe said. "It's twenty-five a week. You've got twenty-one of it and the rest went at the doors."

"Then it's cash."

It was not unkind, and it was not wrong. If a stranger had done to Sloe's own market what Sloe had done to hers, Sloe would have put the price up to hir face as well, and she would have told hir why, to hir face, just as Applejack had.

Hir had three bits.

"Half a dozen," hir said.

Applejack counted six eggs out of the straw, one at a time, and put them in hir hands. She took the three bits and counted them, twice. She wrote in the ledger, with it tipped up on its edge.

"That's you square for today," she said. "Still four from yesterday. I'd want that before there's any more in the book."

"Yes," Sloe said. "That's fair."

Hir asked the Core on the road home, with the six eggs held against hir chest, what the week came to, at today's prices, with what hir had left. It told hir. Hir asked again, a different way, and it gave hir the same answer.

*[Sloe. Status,]* Madder sent.

*[Working on it,]* hir sent. It went out clean, with nothing under it at all. Hir felt Madder feel that, from four kilometres off, and not ask.

*[Nine swallows,]* Haw sent. *[Two went out]*

*[Door hangs,]* Yarrow sent.

*[I'm fine,]* Teasel sent. *[Is anyone else hungry?]*
