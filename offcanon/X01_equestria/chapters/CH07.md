# X01 — Chapter 7
**POV:** Rarity · **Form:** Chapter

---

The Countess wanted the bodice to catch the light when she turned, and not before, so Rarity had spent the afternoon setting the sapphires a hair's breadth deeper than was sensible. The thirtieth went in at a quarter past six. She stepped back from the form to look at it from the side, which was how most of Canterlot would see it, and Sweetie Belle said from the window seat, "What's that?"

Sweetie had been doing her reading on the window seat since four. She had turned one page.

"What's what?"

"In the street."

Rarity came round the worktable with a needle still in her mouth. Up the street from the mill end, where the lamps weren't lit yet, something was coming fast and low through the mud. For a moment she took it for a cart that had lost its pony. Then the miller came out in front of it with a lantern swinging from his teeth and his hat jammed down over his ears, going at a canter, and the thing behind him came apart into bodies.

There were five of them. She had heard there were five. The biggest was in the middle, walking on four legs with its front two held up against its chest, and it was carrying another across them. Three more were packed round it so close that they kept knocking into each other, and each of those went on six, hands and feet both, in a lurching scramble that looked like falling and never quite was. One had a hand flat on the carried one's back. One had hold of the big one's flank. The smallest came last, with its hand on the one in front, and slipped, and did not let go.

They passed under her window close enough that she could have tapped on the glass. The carried one's head was tipped back over the big one's arm, eyes open. One of its forelegs, or arms, hung down loose from the elbow, and it hung wrong: there was a bend in it where no bend should be. Down the big one's chest, and in a line along the street after them, there was blood. It was very dark, almost black under the miller's lantern, and it did not thin out in the puddles the way she would have expected.

Then they were past, and turning at the corner toward Mulberry Lane, and the street was empty except for the rain.

Rarity took the needle out of her mouth and put it in the pincushion. Her own hoof was quite steady, which she noticed with some surprise.

"Upstairs, Sweetie."

She was braced for *why*. Sweetie closed her book and went, and her hooves on the stairs were quick and light and did not stop until the door at the top shut.

Later she went up and knocked on Sweetie's door, and Sweetie said through it that she was doing her reading.

---

The Countess's letter came with the morning post, and would not fold flat.

It was on a cream paper so heavy that it had been folded once and then had simply refused. It lay open on the counter, curling at both ends like a pastry, while Rarity read it for the third time. The Countess was *so looking forward*. The Countess wondered whether a little reticule might be managed, in the same silk, by Thursday. There was no word, anywhere in the letter, on either side of the paper, about the last gown, or the forty bits outstanding on it, or the hundred and ten for this one.

"A reticule," Rarity said to nopony. "By Thursday. Certainly. Out of the air."

She put the letter under the cash box, where it went on curling, and lifted Opal out of the gown box for the first time that day. Opal went limp in the air, which was her protest, and was set down on the stairs, and walked back.

The customers began at nine, and every one of them had news.

Mrs. Tansy came for a hem and told her, with the pins going in round her, that they had taken the creature's whole arm off, at the shoulder, and it had screamed the hospital down. Lemon Drop came for her collar and said no, it was only the hand, and it hadn't made a sound, not one, which was worse, if you asked her. A mare Rarity didn't know put her head round the door only to ask whether it was true that the thing's blood was black. Rarity said she had no idea, which was a lie.

"It's the big one I'm sorry for," said Mrs. Tansy, turning on the stool. "Carried it all the way up from the mill, the miller said. Wouldn't let anypony else take it."

"Mm," said Rarity round the pins.

Rarity turned her a quarter-turn and did not answer.

---

Twilight came in a little before eleven, looking grey.

She had a stub of pencil in her mouth, crossways, like a dog with a stick, and did not seem to know it was there until she tried to say hello round it. She took it out and held it in her hoof instead.

"I'm not staying," she said. "I only wanted to sit down somewhere that wasn't there."

Rarity cleared the fitting stool of Mrs. Tansy's offcuts. Twilight sat on it and looked at the thirty sapphires down the bodice, and did not say anything about them. Rarity understood from that that Spike had told her.

"They took her hand," Twilight said. "In the night. The hand, and the arm up to the elbow. The bone had gone in four places and the doctor couldn't— he said he couldn't tell what was hers and what wasn't. What was meant to be there." She turned the pencil over. "He didn't know how much to give her. For the pain. He did it by weight, and then he halved it to be safe, and it wasn't enough, and then he was frightened to give her any more. I held the lamp."

"Oh, Twilight."

"I'm fine." She put the pencil back in her mouth and took it out again. "They said it'll grow back."

Rarity waited.

"Madder said. The one who does the talking, when it's serious. She said it would grow back, and I said how long, and she said six years." Twilight looked at the pencil. "Six. Years. I asked her to say it again, in case I had it wrong, and she said it exactly the same. She said it the way you'd say it would be dry by Thursday."

"Six years."

"Six years," Twilight said, and it came out of her like a question the second time too. She shook her head. "I can't make it sound like anything. I've been trying all morning."

Rarity had nothing to say to that, so she made tea. Twilight drank half of it and went back to the hospital, and left the other half on the counter by the Countess's letter.

---

At noon Rarity sewed the last of Spike's sapphires into their settings, one by one, with a silk thread so fine she had to wet it twice to see it.

He had done them beautifully. That was the trouble. She had brought him thirty rough stones in a twist of paper and he had brought her back thirty that looked as if they had been poured, every facet crisp, not a scratch on the tables, and she had said *two bits apiece, Spike, and worth every copper*, and he had gone pink to the ends of his spines. Sixty bits. There were eleven in the cash box, and the Countess's forty had been *outstanding* since February.

She lifted Opal out of the gown box for the second time. Opal had taken the tissue paper for a bed and made it into one. She was put on the stairs with a lecture about Canterlot, which she did not attend to.

---

Sweetie had not eaten her breakfast. She had moved the oats from one side of the bowl to the other until it was time to go, and then gone, and Rarity had been at the door with Mrs. Tansy and seen it only afterwards, from the bowl. She came home at four and stood in the workroom door with her satchel still on.

"We were all at school," she said. "All three of us. Apple Bloom told a lie last night."

Rarity had eleven pins in her mouth and a sleeve over one foreleg. "Mm?"

"About her wagon. It came home on a cart. From the weather ponies. It was all burned, the wheel was gone, and Applejack wanted to know how it got up there, and Apple Bloom said we'd left it down by the creek and somepony must've taken it, and Applejack just looked at her, and Big Mac—" She stopped. She stopped the way she had stopped as a very small filly, when she ran into the edge of a table: all at once, with her mouth still open.

Rarity took the pins out. "And Big Mac what?"

"Nothing," said Sweetie. "It doesn't matter. I've got reading." She went upstairs.

Rarity stood holding the pins. It came to her, very clearly, that she had not asked her sister a proper question in two weeks, and that she did not have time to ask one now. She put the pins back in her mouth and went on with the sleeve.

---

The first sleeve was set by ten, and it was the best she had ever done.

The cap sat on the shoulder as if it had grown there, and the seam ran down the back of the arm straight as a ruled line. She thought, as she did whenever something came out right, of the christening gown. She had made it for Mrs. Cobble next door, the summer she was eight, out of a lawn so thin it tore if you looked at it. The hem dipped at the back by the width of a finger. The foal had been carried the whole day, and nopony at the christening ever saw the back of it, and Mrs. Cobble had cried and paid her a bit and a boiled sweet. Rarity had seen the hem every time she thought of it since.

She cut the second sleeve at eleven, and had it pinned by half past, when Sweetie came down in the dark.

She came down without a light, which was how Rarity knew it was serious. She stood at the bottom of the stairs in her nightgown with one hoof on the last step, and Rarity turned the lamp up a little so she could see her face, and then wished she hadn't.

"We took a mirror," Sweetie said.

Rarity put the sleeve down.

"From the castle. The old one in the forest, the sisters' one. Scootaloo said Rainbow Dash had been right inside it once and it wasn't even scary, and Apple Bloom said she bet we couldn't, and I said it was a stupid idea, I *said*, and then I went anyway, because they were going. There was a mirror in a room at the top that was broken, with a sort of metal edge round it, and it was the creepiest thing in there, so we put it in Apple Bloom's wagon. We were only going to scare Diamond Tiara with it." She took a breath. "It was that Friday. Before last. And on the path coming home, after the little stream, it started going all colours. And then there were *places* in it, other places, one after another, really fast, and it was making this noise, and we couldn't get it out of the wagon, and then it broke. It sort of broke from the inside. And there was fire. Everywhere round the wagon. And there were things in the fire, sort of shapes, in a heap, and one of them moved, and we ran." She stopped dead.

Rarity waited. The lamp hissed.

"We ran down the hill," Sweetie said. "Scootaloo stopped by something dead and Apple Bloom pulled her. We came out by the river and went home the long way and we said we'd been at the clubhouse. We didn't tell. We've never told. Apple Bloom lied about the wagon and Applejack knew she was lying and she didn't say, and now the one in the tree—" She stopped again. When she went on her voice was quite level. "It's our fault. They're here because of us. All of it."

Rarity found she was on her feet, and could not remember standing. She did not go to her. Sweetie had the look of a pony holding a very full cup, and Rarity was afraid of what a hug would spill.

"Who else knows?"

"Nobody. Just us."

The clock on the landing said twenty to twelve. The train was at eight. The second sleeve lay on the table with its pins in, and there was Applejack, who had asked her sister a question and been lied to, and there were Scootaloo's people, whoever was minding Scootaloo these days, and there was a hospital on Mulberry Lane with the lamps still lit, and a creature in it who had been carried past this window with her arm hanging wrong.

She thought: Sweetie is not going to stand in a room full of grown ponies and strangers and say that. Not while I'm alive. I will say it. I'll say it tonight, to them, quietly, before the town can make a meal of it, and then first thing tomorrow I'll go to Applejack myself, and to Scootaloo's, and nopony will hear it from anypony but me. The sleeve can be set by dawn. I have set sleeves in less.

"Get your coat," she said.

Sweetie looked up.

"We're going to the hospital. You needn't say a word. I'll do it."

Sweetie opened her mouth, and shut it, and went for her coat.

---

The hospital on Mulberry Lane was a doctor's house with a ward built on the back, and the night nurse who let them in wore a cap that had been starched by somepony who hated her. She took them down the passage without a word.

The ward had four beds and one lamp. Three of the beds were empty, with the blankets folded square at the foot, grey wool in a loose cheap twill that would pill if you breathed on it. The fourth was by the far wall, and the lamp was beside it on a stool, turned low.

The miller was asleep on a hard chair by the door, with his head against the wall. His hat was on the chair beside him, the flat grey felt one with the faded band, and it was still dripping, one drop at a time, off the back of the brim onto the floorboards, where a small dark patch had spread. He must have been home to see to something and come back. Twilight was in the armchair at the end of the bed, curled over the arm of it with her notebook open on her chest. She was asleep with the pencil still in her mouth. Rarity decided not to wake her.

Then she looked at the bed.

The one in it lay on her back with her eyes half open, and the four others were round her so closely that at first they were one shape: a single knot of dark fur, with the lamp on it. The big one was at the head of the bed, sitting on the floor, with one arm laid along the pillow and a hand on the sleeper's head. One was on the far side with a hand flat on her chest. The smallest had got up onto the foot of the bed somehow, folded small, holding the good hand in both of hers; she had a scrap of red ribbon tied round one wrist. Another had a hand on the big one's back and a foot on the smallest one's flank. Their tails were on the floor in one heap, wound through each other, and the rise and fall Rarity looked for in them came very slowly, and all at once.

The arm on the near side ended at the elbow. It was bound in clean linen, wound in a figure of eight, firm and very even; somepony who knew what they were doing had wound it. Near the end the linen had gone dark, the same dark as the street.

They had all turned to look at her. They had done it together, heads coming round in one movement, and they had not got up.

Rarity had prepared a speech on the walk over, and it went. She said, "Forgive me. My name is Rarity. I have the boutique on the square. This is my sister, Sweetie Belle. I'm sorry to come at this hour. I have to tell you something, and I thought you ought to hear it from me and not from the market."

There was a pause. It was not long, but it was there, as if each of them were listening for a moment to somepony in the next room. Then the one with her hand on the sleeper's chest said, "Yes. Go on."

So Rarity told them. She told it plainly, which she found she could do, as long as she didn't look at the bed: three fillies and a dare, a castle, an old broken mirror in a wagon, and the mirror waking on the path home and breaking, and a fire, and shapes in the fire, and the fillies running. She said that nopony had known, until tonight. She said she was not asking them for anything.

When she had finished, all four of them went still.

It was not a pony's stillness. A pony who has had a shock goes on moving, a little, somewhere; an ear goes, or a hoof. These four simply stopped, all at the same instant, the way a clock stops. The big one's hand lay on the sleeper's head without a tremor. Two of them had their ears out wide to either side, stiff. The small one's ears had gone flat. Rarity heard the drip from the miller's hat and counted four drops.

Then the one with her hand on the chest said, "Which day?"

"The Friday before last."

"The hour."

"Evening. I believe." Rarity looked round for Sweetie, who knew. Sweetie was standing half behind her with her eyes on the floorboards, on the damp patch under the miller's hat. "About sunset."

"Where, exactly."

"On the old path back from the castle. Past a little stream, I think, on a rise."

"Which object. Say it again."

"A mirror. An old one, broken already, with a metal rim."

The one who asked nodded once. She had asked every question in the same careful voice, each word set down on its own, and nothing in her voice had moved. She did not say that it was terrible. She did not say that it was all right, either. She said, "Thank you. That's what I needed," and turned back to the bed, and her ears came slowly forward.

The one on the far side of the bed spoke then, and her voice was warmer, and quicker. "Were they hurt? The children."

Rarity had braced for a great many things, and not for that. "No," she said. "No. They ran. Nopony was hurt."

"Good," said that one. "Good." Her ears came forward too.

Then the one in the bed laughed.

She had not moved or opened her eyes any wider. She lay under the grey blanket with the bound arm on top of it, and laughed, weakly but for real, the way somepony laughs at a joke told to them from across a room. It went on. The hand on her chest pressed down, flat, and she laughed under it with her head turned into the big one's arm. None of the others said anything to her. Rarity looked from face to face for whatever she had missed, and found nothing she could read.

The big one had not spoken. She was looking past Rarity. She was looking at Sweetie, steadily, from under the lamp, and she went on looking, and Sweetie did not raise her eyes from the floor.

Rarity waited for her to say something. She was sure, standing there, that she would; that Sweetie would at least say *sorry*, or *it was me*, in her own small voice, and that she would be glad. Sweetie stood beside her and said nothing at all.

"Thank you for coming," said the warm-voiced one, after the pause. "It's late for you. Hir will sleep now, I think."

In the armchair Twilight had not stirred. The nurse held the door.

---

It was past half one when they got back. Sweetie went upstairs without being told. Rarity lit the second lamp and sat down to the sleeve.

She set it by three. It was not as good as the other one.

She could see it the moment she stood the form under the lamp. The cap sat a quarter of an inch forward of where it should, so that the ease bunched faintly at the front of the shoulder, and the seam, which on the left arm ran down as straight as a ruled line, wandered off true just above the elbow. The Countess wouldn't see it. It would still be there, turning with her, every time she turned.

Rarity looked at the clock and at the seam, and did the arithmetic. To unpick it and set it again, properly, was three hours at least. It was twenty past three.

She pressed it instead, as well as a seam that was wrong could be pressed. Then she took the gown off the form and folded it into the tissue, sleeve over sleeve so that the good one lay on top, and put the lid on the box, and tied it. Opal was asleep on the stairs and did not notice.

She sat down in the chair by the worktable to rest her eyes for ten minutes before going up to the farm.

---

The knock woke her at twenty to seven.

The box was on the counter where she had left it, tied, with the Countess's letter still curling under the cash box beside it. There was grey light in the window and the rain was coming down steadily on the square. Rarity went to the door with her mane flat on one side, thinking *Applejack, the farm*, with the speech half composed.

Applejack was on the step.

She was soaked to the skin, with her hat dark with rain and water running off the brim, and she had evidently walked straight from the market, because there was a crate of eggs strapped to her side with a sack over it. Apple Bloom stood a pace behind her in the wet, with her mane plastered flat and no bow in it, looking at the step.

"Applejack," said Rarity. "I was just coming to—"

"I know," Applejack said. She wasn't loud. She sounded as if she had not slept either. "Half the market had it before I'd got the stall up. The miller's been telling it in the bread queue since five. Clover Patch had it off the nurse. Three fillies and a mirror and a wagon, and you going round there in the middle of the night to tell them all about it." She looked past Rarity, at the stairs, where Sweetie had come down as far as the landing and stopped. Then she looked at Rarity again.

"My sister's been lying to my face for two weeks, and I had to hear it off Clover Patch," she said. "You told them before you told me."
