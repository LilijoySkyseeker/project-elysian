# X01 — Chapter 3
**POV:** Fluttershy · **Form:** Chapter

---

The lid of the feed bin had stuck since last spring, when the damp got into the hinge. Fluttershy had a way with it by now: a hoof on the front corner, her shoulder against the side, and a sharp knock underneath with the scoop. "Come on, you," she told it. She was on the knock when the hens came down the ramp.

She heard them before she looked. Marigold came first, as she always did, talking to nopony, with Bess close behind her, and after those two the rest came down in whatever order they liked, except that Pepper was always last and always cross about it. Fluttershy liked to hear them from the bin. It meant the hatch had held another night.

The lid came up. The talking on the ramp stopped.

It didn't trail off. It stopped between one cluck and the next, so that she turned round with the scoop still in her mouth. All eleven hens were standing on the ramp in a line, each with one foot up, staring at the stream.

There were five of them at the edge of the trees on the far bank, just past the other end of the plank bridge.

Her first thought was that they were grey, and that was wrong: the grey came off them when one of them moved, so it was ash, a whole coat of it, and under it they were darker. They were long and low, and they stood on four feet with two more held up off the ground in front of them. The two front ones had hands on the ends. Their ears were enormous. Their eyes were set in the fronts of their faces, and all ten of those eyes were looking the same way as the hens.

Fluttershy had spent her life with animals whose eyes were on the sides of their heads, and with the animals that ate them.

Then Marigold screamed, and the ramp came apart.

Hens went off it on both sides, flapping, half flying and half falling. Two went under the hen house. One went straight over the woodpile, which was the worst place, and one went up onto the cottage roof, where she had never been in her life and would not know how to get down. The rabbits on the lawn were gone before the hens had landed, every one of them, into the hedge and under the porch and down the holes by the rhubarb. Up in the birdhouses the starlings, who had eggs, came out in a rush and went round and round over the yard in a tight black knot, shrieking. Under all of it, from across the water, came a thin whine and then a huff, short and sharp, from more than one throat.

Fluttershy put the scoop down.

"Marigold," she said. "Bess. In." She said it in the voice she used for a hen that had got into the kitchen, which was quiet and flat and did not ask. She walked toward the hen house without hurrying. Hurrying was what a fox did. "In. Hattie. Pepper, under, good girl. In."

Four of them went in. That was four. Up on the roof, the hen who had never been there before put her head over the edge of the grass and looked down at her, and Fluttershy told her under her breath exactly what she thought of her.

When she looked up, the strangers at the bridge had gone still.

All of them had stopped moving at once, as if somepony she couldn't hear had spoken. Their heads came round together to look at her, not one after another but in the same instant, and they stayed turned. Her legs had gone thin and shaky under her. It made her angry. She had not been angry at dawn in a long time. It had been such a good spring: nests in every birdhouse and two in the hedge, and three weeks ago the vixen she had raised from a kit had come back and moved under the woodpile with two kits of her own, as if she had always meant to.

One of them came down to the far end of the bridge and out onto the first of the planks. It walked on its four back feet with its hands held up off the wood, slowly, rocking a little, as if the ground were further away than it expected. It stopped well short of the middle.

"What do you want," Fluttershy said. It came out small and hard, which surprised her.

It looked at her and said nothing.

It was only a moment, but it was a moment too long, and none of the others moved while it lasted. She had the feeling of being weighed, or of somepony checking a thing against a list in another room.

"Water," it said. Its voice was warm and low, with something thin and high running under it like a whistle behind a door. It put the words down one at a time, carefully. "And the way to the nearest town."

"The stream's right there."

It took a moment over that too. "Is it safe to drink?"

"The deer drink it," she said.

It seemed to think about that. Then it said, "I'm Madder," and waited, and when she did not give her own name back it did not ask for it. It turned its head, and the four at the tree line came down to the water on their own side together, as though it had called them. Madder went back off the planks to join them.

---

She had eleven hens to find and a jackdaw in a crate by the stove who had to be fed every two hours or he would stop trying. So she went on with the morning. There was nothing else to do with it.

She got Hattie out from under the hen house with a handful of oats and Dimity off the woodpile by lifting her bodily, while the vixen's eyes watched her from the dark inside the logs. The vixen did not come out. On any other morning she would have come to the end of the pile by now to see whether there was anything in it for her. Today she stayed where she was, her kits somewhere behind her, and only her eyes showed.

Fluttershy fed the jackdaw. He had started taking the grubs off the tweezers before she offered them, and in a week or so he would be ready to go. She would stand at the back door after he went, the way she always did, feeling foolish. From the kitchen window she could see the strangers on the far bank by the bridge. They drank lying down with their faces in the water, all in a row, pressed up against each other along their whole sides. When one got up, the one next to it put a hand on its back. She had seen puppies do that in a basket.

The sun came over the hill. She went out and filled the water trays under the birdhouses, keeping them at the edge of her eye, and she saw their ears change. As the light reached the far bank, the ears went out wide and stiff to either side of their heads, all five pairs together. She had seen a vixen's ears go like that when something came too near the den. She put the tray down and stood very still. "Don't," she said, very quietly, to none of them in particular. None of them came any nearer. They sat in the sun on their side of the water with their ears spread like that, looking at nothing, and after a while she went and did the rabbits' greens with her back to them. She did not turn round until she had finished.

The rabbits would not come out for the greens. She left the pile on the back step. Angel came, eventually, and ate as if nothing had happened. The others stayed in the hedge.

It was the one with the ribbon who came down next. She was on her way back from the hedge with the empty greens basket when it came to the far end of the bridge and sat down there, on its own bank, with the stream between them.

She noticed the ribbon before anything else about it. It was tied tight round the thing's wrist, in a loop: something red, or that had been red, gone brown and stiff along one edge where it had burned. It caught at her. She stood at the near end of the bridge and looked at it, and it caught at her the way a word does when it's nearly there. Then the one wearing it said, "Hello," and she lost it.

"Sorry about your birds," it said. "Is it birds? They're birds. I'm Sloe. That's Madder, you've met, hir's in charge, or hir thinks so. That's Haw, the one on the rail. Don't mind Haw. That's Yarrow, and the small one is Teasel. We came down at last light. We've been walking since—" It stopped. Its ears came forward, slowly, both at once, and stayed there a moment before it went on. "Since last light. It's a lot of walking, down here."

Yarrow was the big one, then: the biggest of them, and the only one that had not made a sound all morning. It had come down behind Sloe to the end of the bridge, and while Sloe talked it ran a hand along the ends of the planks, one after another, and then along the rope that held them, and gave the rope a slow pull, as if to find out what it was tied to.

"The one on the rail" was on the bridge, hanging head down off the side of it by its back feet and its tail, looking down into the water. The back feet were wrapped round the edge plank the way a hand holds a branch.

"What do you take for water?" Sloe said.

Fluttershy didn't understand.

"For the water. The stream. Who do we pay?" It held its wrist up, with the ribbon on it, and turned it this way and that. "We haven't got much. We haven't got anything, if I'm honest. We've got this. I can do you a very good price on this."

"It's a stream," she said. "Nopony owns it."

Sloe took a moment over that. "Then who decides who drinks from it?"

"Nopony decides. You just drink it."

Sloe looked at the stream, and then at her, and its ears went out wide again in the sun. "Right," it said. "That's either very good or very bad, and I can't tell which yet."

Fluttershy very nearly laughed, and was cross with herself for it.

"What's your name?" Sloe said.

She hadn't meant to tell it. It was only that it asked like a pony at the market asks, easy, as if it had a right to know and it would be strange not to answer. "Fluttershy."

"Fluttershy," Sloe said. Behind it, on the far bank, Teasel's ears came up, and Madder looked round.

After that they all used it. They used it every time, at the start of anything they said, as if they had been told that was polite and wanted to be sure. Madder came down to the end of the bridge after Sloe and said, "Fluttershy. How far to the town?" and when she told it, it said, "Fluttershy. Which way does the path go from your door?" and when she told it that, it said, "Fluttershy. Is there anywhere near here that is cool out of the sun?" Every time, it stopped first and looked at her, and then said the thing, and every word was put down separately.

The small one, Teasel, did not come down to the bridge. It stayed at the back of the others on the far bank with its tail wrapped round its own feet. When she went past with the grain, it watched the grain. When she tipped the rabbits' pellets out at the hedge, it watched the pellets. When Angel sat on the back step and ate a dandelion leaf from one end to the other, it watched that too, all the way to the end, and its ears came forward. She looked at it and it looked away.

---

Pepper was the last hen out. By the middle of the morning she was still out, on the near bank under the near end of the bridge, in a dip in the grass where she had decided she lived now. Fluttershy had been leaving her until the others were settled. Pepper could not be hurried; she had to be let come round to it.

Fluttershy was carrying eggs in a basket in her mouth when she saw that Haw had crossed. It had come off the bridge on her side, the only one of them that had, and it was lying in the grass very close to Pepper, with its face nearly touching her. It was watching her breathe. Each time her feathers rose and settled, Haw's ears went a little further forward. It had kept that up for a minute or so, which was the longest she had seen it keep still for anything. Fluttershy put the basket down on the step.

Haw moved.

It didn't move the way anything she knew moves toward a hen. It came up off the grass all at once and got Pepper round the middle and round the legs and by a wing, with more hands than she had counted. The feet it had been standing on all morning were hands as well. She saw the thumbs close on Pepper's legs. Pepper came up into the air in a flurry, upside down, screaming, while feathers went everywhere.

"Put her down," Fluttershy said.

It came out of her low and flat, and it scraped her throat on the way.

Haw had started to say something. It stopped on a small sound, half a squeak, down in its throat. All of them went still. Haw stood on the bank with Pepper in its hands and turned its head to look at her, and so did the other four, at the same instant, from across the water: Madder at the far end of the bridge, Sloe at the edge of the stream, the big quiet one against the willow on that bank, and Teasel at the back. For a moment nothing in the yard moved except Pepper.

Then Haw put her down. It put her down on her feet, carefully, and she went off across the grass in a straight line, flat out, with her neck stretched and her wings half open.

"She's very fast!" Haw said. It said it out loud, delighted, too loud, like a foal at a fair.

"She's frightened."

It looked at Pepper going, then at Fluttershy, and its ears went down a little. "Oh," it said.

Pepper went into the hedge and did not come out. The starlings, who had just begun to settle, went up again. Haw went back over the bridge to the others.

Fluttershy picked up the egg basket. She did not look at Haw again. She took the eggs in and fed the jackdaw, who ate, and then she stood at the kitchen counter for a little while with her eyes shut, and said a word the hens had never heard from her, quietly, to the counter.

---

When she came out again the smoke had got into the air.

She could smell it now in the yard, not strongly, only enough to tell it was there. The sky over the forest had a brown edge to it. She stood on the step and looked up at it. Across the stream, the strangers had their faces tipped up to it too.

Somewhere up in the trees, a long way off, something cracked.

It went on cracking, a long tearing sound, and ended in a thump she felt through her hooves. A tree had come down. On the far bank all of them dropped flat to the grass at once, and a noise came off them, several noises together, a yelp and a whine and something like a cough. Their tails had wound tight, round each other or round their own bodies. Only then did their heads come round toward the sound. The rabbits who had crept back to the greens at last went off in every direction.

One of them went the wrong way.

It was the smallest of the April litter, a grey doe with a nick out of one ear from the time the hedge wire caught her. She went across the lawn in three bounds and onto the bridge, where her feet drummed on the planks, and then she was over it and in under the trees, going up the slope, toward the brown in the sky.

On the far bank Madder came up off the grass and went a step after her, quick and low, toward the trees. Another hand shot out and caught its arm, and it stopped. Fluttershy saw that, and did not know what she had seen.

She went after the doe.

She went over the bridge at a run, past them on the bank without looking at them, and in under the first of the trees. Then she stopped.

It was darker under there than it ought to be at that time of day. The smoke lay low along the ground between the trunks, a thin grey haze, not moving much. It smelled of burned leaves and of something else, something like hot metal, that she didn't know. The Everfree went up the slope in front of her with no path in it, and somewhere in it there was fire, and she did not know where.

She called. Once, not loud.

Nothing came. A bird she didn't recognise called back from somewhere to the right, a long falling note.

She knew what smoke did to a chest as small as that. She knew it better than she wanted to, from the summer the hay barn at the Pinwheel place burned and she had sat up with what they brought her. She knew what else was in the Everfree, too, fire or no fire. She stood at the edge of the trees with her front hooves on the last of the grass and her hind ones on the leaf litter, and she did not go further, and she knew that she was not going to.

She waited as long as she could stand it. Then she came back out from under the trees.

They were in a knot on the bank by the bridge. They had drawn in close since she ran past; Sloe had one hand on Haw's back, and Teasel's tail was wound through Yarrow's. They were all looking at her. None of them said anything. She went past them and back over the bridge.

She went and moved the rest of the greens inside the hutch, where the others would have to come in for them.

---

Rainbow Dash came down at a quarter to twelve, by the kitchen clock, and landed by the hen house hard enough to make the hatch rattle.

"Fluttershy. Hey. Sorry. There's a fire in the Everfree, up above you, it's been going since last night. The team's being called in, so I need you to keep your animals shut in this afternoon, all of them, and—"

She saw them.

Fluttershy watched her see them. Dash did not say anything, which was not like her. Her wings came in tight against her sides, and she stood there on the grass with her eyes going from one to the next, the whole row of them on the far bank, and her mouth shut. There was a smear of soot along the top of her left wing, right across the blue, and she plainly didn't know it was there. Fluttershy wanted to brush it off for her, and stayed where she was.

Then she reached round into her saddlebag and took out a notebook and a pencil.

"Okay," she said. She opened the notebook against her foreleg and licked the end of the pencil. "Weather team. I'm going to ask you some questions. Were you in the forest last night?"

Across the stream nothing moved. Then Madder came down to the far end of the bridge on its four back feet with its hands up. Dash went to the near end, and they faced each other over the width of the water. There was the pause, and Fluttershy saw Dash notice it, saw her ears flick.

"Yes."

"Did you see the fire?"

"Yes."

"Where did it start?"

"On the old track that comes down from the high ground," Madder said. "On a rise, facing downhill. It burned in a ring round where we were, on both sides of the track, about twelve metres across. A strip ran off it to the left of the track, downhill, about a hundred metres long and five wide, into a clearing. It went out there. The rest was moving uphill, and to the right, with the air."

Dash was writing. She stopped writing. "Metres," she said.

"Yes."

"Okay." She wrote it. "When?"

"Sixteen hours and nine minutes ago."

Dash looked up.

"At last light," Madder said, as though that might help. "We last saw the glow of it seven hours and fifty minutes ago, from inside the trees, before first light. It was round the hill to the right of the way we came down. It had gone further round each time we looked. We looked eight times."

Fluttershy saw Dash's pencil hanging over the page. Dash had told her once, laughing, that you asked a farmer when a fire started and he said *round supper*. She was not laughing now. She wrote the numbers down very neatly, one under another, and went over the nine twice.

"What were you doing on the track?" she said.

The pause was longer this time. None of them moved.

"Waking up," Madder said.

Dash wrote that down too. Fluttershy watched her do it. Then Dash said, not looking up, "Did you start it?"

"We were there when it started."

Dash's pencil stopped. She stood a moment with it on the paper, then closed the notebook on it and put the whole thing back in her bag, and came back up the bank to where Fluttershy was standing by the feed bin, and stood close.

"Nopony talks like that," she said, low. "Nopony knows what time anything happened at a fire. You're lucky if they know what day." She looked back across the stream. Nothing over there had moved. "Are you okay? Have they done anything?"

"They caught a hen," Fluttershy said. "They let her go."

Dash looked at her for a moment as if she were waiting for more, and when there wasn't any, she let it go. "Okay. So. We're on this whole edge from about two. We can't go in, you know what the Everfree's like, it does its own weather and it won't take ours. So we soak the outside and hope the fire doesn't feel like coming out." She blew her fringe out of her eyes. "Half the team think it isn't even our job. The captain says it is. The captain's been off the ground about twice since winter, but sure. So it's our job. There's going to be twenty ponies coming in low over your yard with full clouds, and they're dropping them right along the stream. Both banks, from the trees all the way down to the Pinwheel fields. Nopony's going to be looking where it lands. Anything standing on either side of the water gets flattened."

"I know."

"So they can't be here." Dash rubbed her face with a hoof. "I've got to write it all up. I'm in the book already this month, Fluttershy, I can't have five of—" She stopped. "They can't be in the line. That's it."

Fluttershy looked past her at the far bank.

Sloe had its wrist held against its chest, with the ribbon on it, and was facing Haw, whose ears were down. Its mouth barely moved. What came out of it was thin and high, right at the edge of what Fluttershy could hear, and none of the other four turned toward it. Madder was standing a little in front of the others, where it always stood. Yarrow had a hand flat on the trunk of the willow on that bank; its other hand closed on nothing, and opened, and closed again. The little one, Teasel, was watching the hens, who had come back out at last and were picking at the grain in the yard; it watched their beaks go down and come up, and its ears were forward, and it didn't seem to know it was doing it. The vixen had not come out of the woodpile all morning. The starlings were still up. The grey doe with the nick in her ear was somewhere up in the smoke, and she had not come back over the bridge.

She had been up since before it was light. She still had the nest boxes to do, and the jackdaw at one, and the larks. All week she had meant to take her tea down to the south meadow one morning and sit in the long grass while the larks went up, and all week there had been something else.

She went down to the near end of the bridge. On the far bank, all five of them turned their heads to her at once.

"There's an old duck house at the bottom of the south meadow, past the willows, and nothing's nesting in it this year," she said. "Go round the edge of the grass, not across it. The larks are down in there. I'll bring what I can when I do them at four, and if I find one egg broken, you'll find somewhere else."
