# X01 — Chapter 2
**POV:** Madder-4 · **Form:** Chapter

---

The heap had to come apart from the outside in, the way a snarled line does, so Madder started with Haw.

Haw was on the outside because Haw always slept on the outside, with one leg slung over the rest of them like a guard rail, and hir was already awake. Hir was also already talking. "Madder, what's that smell? Is it us? It's in my mouth. Everything's gone orange, look, even Sloe's orange —"

"Haw. Leg off Sloe."

Haw lifted the leg. It came up slowly, the way a boom comes up when the winch is short of power, hung for a moment, then dropped back across Sloe's ribs so hard that Sloe swore. Haw laughed. Hir tried again with both hands under the knee, which got it clear, then rolled off the heap altogether and lay on hir back in a drift of dead leaves with hir arms out, still laughing.

Madder got hir own upper hands flat on the ground before hir tried anything else. Everything hir had was heavy: arms, head, the neck that held the head up. Hir tail lay out behind hir like a hawser somebody had forgotten to coil. Hir put all six limbs down, spread wide, and only when hir was braced did hir take hir weight off Teasel.

Teasel had been at the bottom, which was where the youngest always ended up when a web was left to settle, so hir came out last and flattest. Hir lay where hir was until Madder said, "Teasel. Up on your hands." Then hir did exactly that, one hand after another, and stayed there without being told anything more.

Yarrow was up already. Madder had felt hir come clear of the heap while hir was still dealing with Haw's leg, and now hir could see what had kept Yarrow busy. Yarrow's left lower hand was locked on the edge of a wooden cart, with the thumb hooked under the board and the knuckles tight. Hir must have taken hold of it in hir sleep. Yarrow looked at the hand as if it belonged to someone on another crew; then hir opened it. When the cart rocked back on its wheels hir watched it rock, and put the hand back on it, on purpose this time.

The fire was on both sides of the path. It was still low, working through dry fronds and dead sticks with a noise like a tray of loose fasteners being shaken, and it was near enough that Madder's ears had gone out wide from hir head before hir had thought about heat at all. Hir took stock the way hir always did, starting with the ways out. There was a path of sorts, a strip of beaten ground that ran uphill into the burning and downhill away from it. Everywhere else there were trunks. Because the light kept jumping, hir eyes were no good for any of it, but the near-field gave hir the trunks cleanly as columns, set at no spacing anybody had planned, with the ground between them tilting steeper to the left.

Downhill, then. Hir had the order ready. First, though, hir counted them.

Five. Haw on hir back; Sloe sitting up with a hand on hir ribs; Yarrow at the cart; Teasel on hir hands; and Madder. Hir had every one of them in the frame to the second, as plain and close as hir own pulse, so hir hadn't needed hir eyes for it. Hir did it with hir eyes anyway, once, looking at each of them in turn. Then hir looked past them, the way hir had looked past the crew at every waking of hir life, for the Hum of the ship behind them and around them and under everything.

It wasn't there. Hir asked the Core for the time, since the time was a thing hir could have, and the Core gave hir the seconds since hir had come up out of the lock: two hundred and nine. Hir noted it.

"Downslope," Madder said. "Yarrow, bring the cart. Haw ahead of me, not past me. Sloe and Teasel behind. We halt in ten minutes."

---

Before Yarrow could take the shafts hir had to empty the cart. There was a frame lying in the bed, dark metal with nothing in the middle of it but a few splinters of glass still stuck in the rim, and under it were two sacks burned down to black crusts. Yarrow lifted the frame out with both upper hands, weighed it once, and leaned it against a tree. Then hir took the shafts.

Sloe had gone round to the front to help, but stopped by the harness, which lay in the dirt in a tangle of straps. There was a red ribbon caught in the buckle, tied in a bow, with one loop scorched brown along its edge. Sloe worked it loose with hir fine fingers, taking hir time over it while the fire went on behind hir, then wound it twice round hir own left wrist and tucked the end in.

"Inventory," Sloe said. "One cart. One ribbon. No food. No water. No lights, no tools, no suits, no anything. Five of us." Hir held up the wrist. "I've done it twice, Madder. It doesn't get any longer, I did try."

Down the side of the cart, in red paint that the heat had lifted into blisters, somebody had written APPLE BLOOM in large careful letters. The B in BLOOM faced the wrong way. Madder read it, and hir felt the others read it through the frame, one after another. Nobody said anything about it.

"Ten minutes," Madder said. "Going."

---

The first stretch took longer than hir had allowed for the whole ten minutes, because nobody could walk.

They went on all six, with the upper hands down among the rest. On a hull you put a hand where the next hold was, and the hold stayed where you'd put it. Here the ground slid out from under a hand if the leaves on it were wet, and it tipped wherever it liked, so that a foot Madder set down on what hir near-field swore was level would land a hand's width lower than it should. Twice hir went down on a shoulder. The tail was the worst of it. It had always been a limb like the others, the fifth hand on a line; now it had to be held up off the ground at every step, or it pulled hir backward on the slope. Before long hir back ached all the way from the root of it.

Haw went down the path as if it were a game. Hir lost hir footing more than any of them, and every time hir got up faster. About halfway to the halt hir saw something ahead that hir wanted, a fallen trunk lying across the path at the height of hir chest, and ran at it. Haw got three strides into the sprint before the ground came up, because hir legs had been built to push off a wall and had no idea what to do with a floor that pushed back. Hir went into the trunk shoulder first.

*[Haw. Report ~ sharp]*

*[Fine! Fine fine ~ bruise, delight, more delight]*

"Did you see that?" Haw said aloud, sitting up. "How fast I went? I've never gone that fast without a line on."

"You weren't going fast. You were falling."

"I was falling fast," Haw said, pleased, and got up to look at the trunk hir had hit.

While they waited for hir, Yarrow reached out and set a hand flat against the nearest tree. The bark was cracked into ridges, grey-brown, with a green bloom on the side away from the fire. Yarrow pressed on it, took the hand away to look at the palm, then put it back and pressed again. Madder waited until Yarrow was done.

Teasel sneezed a little way past the trunk.

Madder felt it before hir heard it: a jolt through the frame, sharp and startled, as if Teasel had put a hand on a live contact. When hir turned round, Teasel had stopped dead on the path with hir head low and hir eyes streaming. There was a stand of tall weeds beside hir with heavy yellow heads, and the air round them was full of dust in the firelight.

"I'm fine," Teasel said, before anyone asked.

"Report."

"Something went off in my face." Teasel looked at the air in front of hir as if whatever it was might still be there. "It came out. I didn't do it. It just came out, all at once."

"Went off how?" said Sloe.

"I don't know. Out." Teasel wiped hir eyes with the back of an upper hand. "I'm fine. Is anyone else hungry?"

"Everyone's hungry," Sloe said. "I told you. I counted."

Madder watched Teasel for another ten seconds, until hir was sure Teasel wasn't going to do it again. When Madder listened past the five of them for the Hum, at the six hundredth second, it still wasn't there.

---

The hollow at the bottom of the slope had water in it, a thin run over stones that hir heard long before hir found it. The fire was behind them now, a glow up among the trunks, and the smoke had turned to go the other way. Madder called the halt at the twenty-sixth minute, sixteen minutes late.

They sat down where they stopped. For a while nobody did anything but breathe, and Madder let them. Hir went over them from the front. Sloe's ribs were bruised, not broken. Haw's shoulder hir couldn't check, because Haw wouldn't keep it still. Yarrow had nothing wrong with hir; Teasel had nothing but the wet eyes. Nobody was bleeding. Hir could feel each of them breathing at hir own pace, a little out of step, the way the crew always breathed after a long shift.

Then hir went looking for Clary, though hir hadn't meant to. Clary-6 had stopped at the twentieth minute of the second watch on a rest day, while Madder was standing in the galley queue with a tray. The recall came back whole as soon as hir reached for it. The presence table had held Clary's place for a tenth of a second, and then it hadn't, and everyone in the queue had put a hand on the counter at the same moment, as though the ship had lurched. The ship hadn't moved at all. Madder had put hir tray down so carefully that the soup in the bowl never stirred. That evening the whole rigging watch had come to the ready room to Read hir, with Clary laid out on the table under the good lamp, and Madder had stood at the head.

Hir came back out of it. The water went on running over the stones.

Sloe was looking at the ribbon on hir wrist. Without lifting hir head, in the voice hir used for stores, hir said, "Hundred and eighty-three. And the two in medical."

Nobody asked what the number was.

"Watch rota," Madder said. "Yarrow first, to the half hour. Haw second. Then Teasel, then Sloe. Me last. Everyone off watch sleeps."

Nobody lay down.

"Station?" Yarrow said.

Madder looked round the hollow for one. There was the water, a flat-topped stone standing up out of it, and the dark beyond. "There," hir said. "By the stone."

"Watching for what, though?" said Sloe.

"Changes."

"In what?"

"Anything."

Sloe looked at hir for a moment, then went back to the ribbon. Yarrow got up and went to stand by the stone.

---

Madder watched Yarrow. Yarrow stood square with hir lower hands planted and hir tail held off the mud, which was more than any of them had managed on the slope. After a while hir bent to look at the stone. Yarrow would be looking at how the stone was sitting in the stream, whether it was seated or only resting, which way it would go if it went.

There was a lump on top of the stone. Madder's near-field had given it to hir as part of the stone from the start, and hir only saw it properly when Yarrow's face was a hand's width from it. It was the size of a closed fist, wet all over, the colour of the stone except in patches where it was greener. Its legs were folded under it at angles no joint Madder knew of would fold. Its eyes were on top of its head. It didn't move at all, until Madder saw a flutter at its throat, quick as a gauge needle.

Yarrow went still. Through the frame the fear came up out of hir like a pressure spike, hotter and sharper than anything hir had sent while the fire was on them, so fast that all five of them felt it at once. Haw sat up. Teasel made a small noise.

The lump jumped.

It went straight up past Yarrow's face and away in one movement, with no warning, no crouch and no push that Madder could see. Yarrow went backward. Sixty kilos of rigger went over into the stream on hir back with a noise like a dropped pallet, and hir hands shut on a tree root at the edge of the water as soon as they found it, so that hir hung there half in and half out, holding on.

Haw was in the water before Madder could stop hir. "It's alive! Madder, it's alive, it's got a face, did you see it go? It went all the way over there without —" Hir plunged a hand in among the reeds. "Where is it? Where did it go?"

"Haw. Out of the water."

"It was looking at Yarrow —"

"Out. Now."

Haw came out, dripping and grinning, still craning back at the reeds. Yarrow hadn't moved. Hir lay in the stream with both lower hands locked on the root, looking at the place where the stone was, until hir lifted one upper hand clear of the water and held it up, flat, with the fingers spread. On the hull that meant *all stop*. Everyone stopped. For a count of thirty nobody moved, and the only sound in the hollow came from the water running round Yarrow's body.

Then Yarrow let go of the root and got up out of the stream, and went and stood by the stone again, a little further off than before.

Haw's watch would have come due at the half hour. Madder didn't call it. At the twenty-first minute of Yarrow's watch hir stood up and said, "Up. We go on," and nobody argued.

---

They followed the water downhill.

The going was easier by the stream, and before long Madder had hir upper hands off the ground for three and even five steps together before hir needed them again. Somewhere behind them a tree came down in the fire, a long cracking tear that ran through the ground into hir feet. Nobody turned round. Yarrow had the cart. Every so often hir let go of a shaft to put a hand on a trunk, then took the shaft up again.

They found the animal where the stream bent round a clearing.

The fire had been through the clearing, then gone out again for lack of anything to burn, so the ground there was black and soft and smelled like the back of a plasma housing after a flush. The thing lay at the edge of it, on its side. It was small, smaller than the lump on the stone, with fur that had gone to crisp black points along its back and was still grey on its belly. Its feet were curled up. It had a tail as long as its body, flattened and scorched.

Haw went toward it with a hand out.

"Haw," Madder said, and Haw stopped.

Madder went over and looked down at it. It didn't move. There was no flutter at the throat, which hir had learned to look for on the stone. Hir marked it the way hir had marked every one for forty years, before hir had thought whether to: casualty, one; cause, fire; fire, theirs. Hir had it ready.

There was no board to put it on. Hir stood with the number and nowhere to take it.

Hir left lower hand was full of burrs. Hir must have walked it through something on the slope, because the pads and the fur between the fingers were packed with small hooked seeds that pricked when hir closed the hand. Hir sat down beside the animal and began to pick them out with the hand above, one at a time, while the others waited. The hooks came out stuck to hir fingers, so hir had to scrape each one off on hir knee before hir could go for the next.

"Do we Read it?" said Teasel.

Madder took out another burr. "Unconfirmed," hir said.

Sloe came and crouched by hir, and held out hir hand for the burrs as Madder got them loose, and took each one as it came. After a while Sloe said, "It's got very small hands," and after another while Madder said, "Yes," and then there were no more burrs.

---

The trees thinned. It happened gradually, so that for a long time Madder only knew it from the near-field, which kept returning the columns further apart and the ground between them flatter, until hir looked up and could see a grey paleness between the trunks. The slope gave out. Under hir feet the leaves turned to something short and green and wet that bent without breaking. The air smelled different again, of the water, of something sweet as a ration-pack glaze, and of a sharp dry dustiness that made Teasel stop and hold hir breath and wait, eyes screwed shut, until whatever it was had passed without going off.

Madder called the halt at the edge of the trees, and took stock.

There was a stream, wider here, with a small bridge over it made of planks. There was grass running down to it, and on the far side of the grass there was a house. It was small and low, with a round roof grown over with something green, and it was built into the side of a hill. It had a door and two windows. Round it, on posts and on the walls and in the trees nearby, were a great many small wooden boxes, each with a round hole in the front. Hir could make no sense of them, so hir noted them and moved on. No light showed in the windows, and nothing moved at the door.

Beside the house stood a shed with a slanted roof and a wooden ramp leading up to a little hatch low in its side.

The five of them stood together in the edge of the trees. Yarrow set down the shafts of the cart. Sloe had one hand on the ribbon at hir wrist, and Haw was leaning out past Madder's shoulder, with hir whole body pointing at the house, only waiting to be let go.

The hatch in the shed swung open.

A bird came out onto the ramp: brown and round, a good deal bigger than the animal by the stream, walking on two thin legs with its head going forward and back at every step. Another came out behind it, then another, a line of them filing down the ramp one after the other into the grey light, red at the head and muttering.

The first one saw them, and stopped dead. So did the next, and the next, all the way back up the ramp to the hatch, until the whole line of them stood without a sound, every one exactly where it was.
