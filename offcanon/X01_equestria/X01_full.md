# X01 — (working title) *The Mirror*
*A non-canon crossover novelette: My Little Pony: Friendship is Magic × Project Elysian. Private reading.*


---

## 1. The Mirror

Scootaloo wouldn't push the back of the wagon because of the beetle.

"It's a beetle," Apple Bloom said. She had the traces over her chest and her hooves dug into the path. The wagon had stopped dead on a root, so the mirror had slid forward in the bed until it was leaning all its weight into the backs of her legs. "It's the size of an oat."

"It's looking at me."

"It ain't got a face to look at you with."

"Then what's that," Scootaloo said, and pointed with her chin.

Sweetie Belle leaned in to see. The beetle was sitting on the corner of the frame, where the dark metal had gone rough and pitted: a green one, the shiny kind, with its back end tipped up like it was thinking something over. Sweetie Belle said it probably didn't even know it was on a mirror. She said it probably thought it was on a rock, and it'd go home tonight and tell all the other beetles it rode a rock through the woods, and they'd say rocks don't move, and it'd say this one did, and they'd — "Anyway. It's not going to do anything."

"So you move it."

Sweetie Belle did not move it.

Apple Bloom ducked out of the traces, went round, and flicked the beetle off with the edge of her hoof. It dropped into the ferns without bothering to open its wings. Scootaloo put her shoulder to the tailboard as if none of that had happened.

"Push when I say," Apple Bloom said, and got back in the traces. "Not before. Last time you pushed before and it went over my hoof."

"It went over your hoof because you stopped."

"I stopped because you pushed before."

The wagon came off the root with a lurch and ran on a length until the mirror knocked the backboard. All three of them froze and listened for glass. Nothing. Apple Bloom let her breath out through her nose. She'd laid the thing face-down in the bed on top of her own two feed sacks, the empty ones she'd brought in case they found apples. They hadn't, because it was the Everfree and nothing in it grew the way it was supposed to, so the sacks were padding now.

It was heavy. She'd been working it out all the way down from the castle: it was heavier than a full water trough, which she couldn't lift. Because it was flat and tall, with all its weight in the frame (plain dark metal, with no pattern on it at all), it wanted to go over sideways every time the path tipped. Getting it up into the wagon had taken all three of them, a board and a lot of yelling. Getting it off again at the barn was something she'd have to think about, though Big Mac would help if she asked, and he wouldn't ask what it was.

She'd put it at the back of the barn, behind the hay rake, under the blue tarp with the hole in it that nobody used. It'd be hers. She'd seen it first, when a corner of it was sticking out of a bank of nettles in a room with no roof, and she'd said *that's mine* before either of them had even turned their heads, so that was that. Nobody'd given it to her, so nobody could say *give it here, that's for the farm* or *that's your cousin's turn*. It was hers the way the wagon was hers, only more, because she hadn't even had to have a birthday for it.

The wagon's side said APPLE BLOOM in red paint, in Big Mac's careful letters, with the B in BLOOM facing the wrong way. It had been like that since spring. Nobody had told him. Applejack said it was the principle of the thing, since he'd worked so hard; then she'd gone out behind the smokehouse and laughed until she had to sit down.

---

The light was going grey under the trees, the kind of grey that at home meant you ought to have started the milking already, before somebody said so. Apple Bloom kept her head down and pulled. The path out of the Everfree was longer than the path in. On the way in, when it had been a dare, they'd trotted it. On the way out it was her wagon and her mirror and her neck, and a lot of roots.

Her bow caught on a low branch and yanked her head back. She stopped, reached up, unhooked it, and went on. Three steps later it caught again on the same kind of branch, a grey springy one with no leaves, so she said a word she'd heard Granny Smith say to the stove.

"Ooh," said Sweetie Belle.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything. I said *ooh*."

Apple Bloom put her head down and pulled, and while she pulled she went back over the week, which had been a bad one. On Monday Applejack had snapped at Big Mac about a fence post that had been leaning since last year. On Tuesday she'd sat up at the kitchen table after supper with a stack of papers and a pencil she kept chewing. When Apple Bloom came down for a glass of water, Applejack had turned the top paper over. Just turned it over, face-down, and said *bed*. On Wednesday she'd said *not now* three times before lunch, one of those times before Apple Bloom had even asked her anything.

By Thursday Apple Bloom had worked it out. It was the hen house. Sunday night had been her night to latch it, but she'd been reading under the covers and forgot, so on Monday morning they'd been one hen short. Clover. The brown one that pecked. Nobody had said a word to her about it. Nobody had said a word about anything, all week, except the snapping, which was at everybody else.

She was still turning that over, head down, pulling.

"My legs hurt," Scootaloo said from the back.

"You ain't even pushing."

"I'm pushing with my mind."

"Push with your legs."

"*Your* legs," Scootaloo said, which didn't mean anything, but she said it with a lot of feeling. Then, "Rainbow Dash could pull this with one wing."

"So go get her."

Scootaloo didn't say anything to that.

---

They stopped at the bottom of a dip where a little brown stream ran across the path, because the wagon had to be walked through it one wheel at a time or it'd bog, and because Sweetie Belle had sat down.

"Just for a minute," Sweetie Belle said. "I'm not tired. I'm just sitting."

Apple Bloom looked at the light. She didn't like it, but her shoulders had been telling her things for a while now, so she stepped out of the traces and rolled them. The straps had rubbed two sore stripes in her coat.

Scootaloo walked round the wagon once. Then she climbed up on the wheel, leaned over into the bed, and knocked on the back of the mirror with her hoof.

It went *tunk*. Deep. Like knocking on a rain barrel that's half full.

"Hear that?" Scootaloo said.

"Get off my wheel."

"It's hollow. The back of it. There's a space in there, listen." *Tunk.* "That's why it's so heavy. There's something inside."

"Hollow's lighter," Sweetie Belle said. "Hollow things are lighter. That's what hollow is."

"Not if there's something *in* the hollow."

"Then it's not hollow, it's full."

"It's *half*-hollow," Scootaloo said, "shut up," and knocked again. "We could get the back off. There's a seam all down the side. I bet if we got a flat rock in it —"

"No."

"I'm not saying break it. I'm saying *look*."

"It's mine," said Apple Bloom, "and it's going in the barn in one piece, and you ain't getting a rock anywhere near it."

"We've been carrying it all afternoon! I want to know what's in it!"

"*I've* been carrying it. You've been pushing with your mind."

Sweetie Belle had pulled her knees up and wrapped her front legs round them. She said, "I don't like it."

They both looked at her.

"I don't. I didn't like it at the castle and I don't like it now." She was talking to the stream more than to them. "It's like when you're in bed and the closet door's open a little bit, and you know you shut it. And nothing's in there. You *know* nothing's in there. But you can't go to sleep until you get up and shut it, and you don't want to get up, because then you'd have to walk past it —" She stopped. Just stopped, with her mouth still a bit open.

"It's a mirror," Apple Bloom said.

"I know it's a mirror."

"So it ain't a closet."

"I *know*."

"We could leave it here," Sweetie Belle said. "Right here. By the water. And come back for it another day with a grown-up —"

"We ain't telling a grown-up we were in the Everfree."

"— or not a grown-up, just, not *tonight* —"

"It's *my wagon*," Apple Bloom said, "and it's going in the barn."

Scootaloo got down off the wheel, slow. When she was down she said, not loud, "You just want something to show Applejack so she'll talk to you."

The stream went on running over the stones while Apple Bloom felt her face go hot from the ears in.

"At least mine knows my name," she said.

It came out before she'd decided to say it. It came out in exactly the voice she'd meant, too, which was the worst part. She saw it go into Scootaloo like a splinter goes in, not all at once but all the way. Scootaloo didn't say anything back; she turned round and looked at the trees on the other side of the dip like there was something in them she was interested in.

Nobody said anything for a while.

Then Sweetie Belle said, in a small, ordinary voice, "Who put it face-down?"

"What?"

"In the room. With the nettles. It was lying on its face." Sweetie Belle was looking at the back of the mirror, the plain dark metal with the seam down the side. "There wasn't anything else in there. No chairs. No nothing. It wasn't on a wall that fell down, because the walls were all still up. It was just in the middle of the floor, on its face. Somepony put it like that."

Apple Bloom had seen it and gone straight past it to *that's mine* the way you go past a dog you don't know.

"It fell," she said.

"From what?"

"I don't know from what. It fell. Things fall." She picked the traces up off the ground. "Maybe a pony put it face-down so it wouldn't get scratched. That's what I did. That's just sense."

Sweetie Belle nodded. Then she opened her saddlebag and took out half a daisy sandwich wrapped in a napkin, the half she'd been saving since lunch. She unwrapped it and began to eat it, right there by the stream, while the light went and the question sat out in the air where she'd put it.

"*Now?*" Apple Bloom said.

"I'm hungry," Sweetie Belle said, with her mouth full.

"You ain't hungry. You're scared."

Sweetie Belle chewed and didn't answer that.

---

They walked the wagon through the stream a wheel at a time. The water came up to Apple Bloom's knees, cold enough to make her teeth hurt. When the mirror rocked in the bed they all held their breath again, but again nothing broke. On the far side the path climbed. As Apple Bloom leaned into the traces she thought about the barn, the blue tarp and the rake, the hay loft ladder, and Clover the hen, who had pecked, but who hadn't deserved a fox.

Scootaloo was at the back, not talking. Every so often Apple Bloom felt the wagon get a bit easier, so she was pushing, anyway. Sweetie Belle walked alongside, licking her hoof where the sandwich had been.

Apple Bloom did not hear the stick go into the seam. What she heard, afterward, was a little sound, *tick*, like a fingernail on a bottle. At the same time the front wheel went down off a stone and up again, so that the whole wagon jumped, and something in the bed went *tick* a second time, louder. Then there was a long thin sound like when you run a wet hoof round the rim of a glass.

She stopped. She turned her head.

Scootaloo was standing back from the tailboard with a stick in her mouth and her eyes huge. The stick had a bent end. Now there was a gap, a finger-width, between the frame and the backing, all down one side.

"I didn't," Scootaloo said round the stick. She spat it out. "I barely —"

The mirror turned over.

It didn't slide. It didn't fall. It went up on its long edge in the bed of the wagon, slowly, the way a pony rolls over in her sleep, until it settled on its back on the feed sacks with its face to the sky. Through the traces Apple Bloom felt the whole wagon go heavy and then light, like something had got in and then got out again.

The glass had a crack in it. She'd seen that at the castle: one long crack straight across from one corner nearly to the other, like the part in a mane. She'd thought it'd be easy enough to live with.

It wasn't showing the sky.

It was showing grass: tall pale grass, or reeds, close up, like the mirror was lying down in the middle of a field of them. They were all bent the same way, hard over, like in a gale, though nothing moved them. They were bent and still. Out of the mirror came a sound, slow, the same over and over: a long creak and a stop, a long creak and a stop, like the barn door when the latch is off and there's a wind. Except there wasn't any wind, there wasn't anything, just the reeds holding themselves over.

Sweetie Belle made a noise in her throat.

The reeds went. In their place was a floor, a tiled floor of red and yellow squares, with something dark spreading slowly across it toward the edge of the glass, thick and slow, like a spill nopony was going to mop. Every time Apple Bloom blinked, it was nearer.

Then that went too, and the glass went dark all over. Apple Bloom thought, *good*, *it's done*, and she had one foot out of the traces to go and look.

And the glass found something.

It was a light, behind some kind of bars or a grating, a long way down inside the mirror or a long way off, and it was steady. It didn't flicker the way a lamp does. It was steady and very bright, so bright she could feel it in her teeth, and under it was a sound that didn't change: the sound the cider press makes when Big Mac has it running just right, when it goes and goes and you could set a clock by it. She'd always liked that sound.

The mirror held on to it, like somepony getting a grip on a rope.

Heat came off the glass.

She felt it on her face, then more than that, while the air above the wagon bed went wavy. The feed sacks under the frame started to smoke. The red paint on the side of the wagon, APPLE BLOOM, the backwards B, came up in little bubbles.

"Get back," she said. Her voice came out thin. "Y'all get back —"

The crack in the glass opened without a sound. The two halves bowed out toward the sky like a sheet on the line when the wind gets under it, bellied out and out, soft, and the steady light was right there under the skin of it, and Apple Bloom thought, very clearly, *that's not how glass goes*, and then the light came through.

She didn't see it. She felt it on the front of her face and her chest and the insides of her ears. For a moment there was nothing but white, and a smell like hot iron, and then the ferns on both sides of the path were burning.

Ferns, dead leaves, the grey springy branches that had kept catching her bow: the fire came up out of them tall and fast and yellow and loud, a crackling, busy sort of loud, and the smoke went sideways across the path and into her eyes.

The mirror was gone. There was a frame on its back in the wagon, dark metal, with nothing in it, smoking. The feed sacks were on fire.

And the path behind the wagon was full.

It was big. That was the first thing: it was as big as a load of hay on the big wagon, dark, all one heap tangled into itself, and it was moving. Slow. Not coming at them. It moved the way the barn cats move in winter, when they're all asleep in one pile in the straw and one of them wakes up, so the whole pile has to shift round. Something in it lifted and flopped back down. Something else lifted. The firelight went over it without showing her anything, just dark and more dark and edges she couldn't hold on to with her eyes, while smoke rolled over the top.

Scootaloo was right next to it, because she'd been at the back of the wagon. She was standing there with her wings half open and not a sound coming out of her.

"Scoot," Apple Bloom said.

Then a hand came over the side of the wagon.

It came up out of the heap, over the tailboard and along the side where the letters were, and closed on the edge of the wagon bed. It was a hand. It had fingers, long ones, and it had a thumb, and the thumb went round underneath the wood the way nothing she knew could go round anything, and it *held on*. She saw the knuckles go tight. She saw it pull. When the whole wagon rocked back toward the heap, the traces went tight across her chest, and she was pulled back a step with it.

The traces were buckled, and the buckle was under her, where she couldn't get at it.

Scootaloo went. She didn't say anything. She just wasn't there: there was a shape going off into the smoke on the left with its wings clamped down tight, and then she was gone.

Sweetie Belle said, "Apple Bloom, Apple Bloom, Apple Bloom," like it was one long word.

The hand pulled again. Apple Bloom threw herself forward, and the wagon came a little way with her before it stopped, hard, so that she felt the straps bite into the sore stripes they'd already made. She got her head down and backed up, fast, into the harness, the wrong way, and dropped her shoulder and twisted, and the breast strap went up over her ears and took her bow with it and she was out. She fell on her face in the path. The traces fell on the ground in front of her in a tangle, with the red bow caught in the buckle.

The wagon stayed, with APPLE BLOOM on the side, bubbling. The hand was still on it.

She got up and ran.

---

There wasn't a path. There had been a path, and now there was smoke, and trees, and a light behind them that made every tree throw a shadow the wrong way, toward her, long, so she kept thinking the ground was a hole and jumping over nothing. Sweetie Belle was beside her and then ahead of her and then beside her again. She had her saddlebag still, flapping. Apple Bloom could hear her breathing and she could hear the fire, which was bigger now, busy and cracking, and somewhere in it a tree went over with a long tearing sound and the ground shook under her hooves.

"We have to find Scootaloo," Sweetie Belle said. "We have to — which way did she go, did you see which way she went, because I didn't, I think it was left, it was left, but it's all left now, everything's left, I can't —"

She stopped talking. She kept running.

"Left," Apple Bloom said.

She didn't know if it was left. She went left. Her mane was in her eyes without the bow and she kept shaking her head to clear it and it kept falling back. Brambles got her across the chest. She went through them. She thought, *the wagon*, and then she thought about the thumb, and she stopped thinking about the wagon.

They came out of the smoke into a bit of clear, and the air was cold, and it was nearly dark, real dark, the forest kind, and there on the far side of the clear, standing still under a tree with her back to them, was Scootaloo.

"Scoot!"

Scootaloo didn't turn round. She was shaking all over, Apple Bloom could see it from here, her whole back going. She was looking at something on the ground in front of her: small, with fur, not moving, and a thread of smoke coming up off it.

Apple Bloom looked once. Then she went across the clear at a gallop and hit Scootaloo in the side with her shoulder, not gentle, and Scootaloo staggered, and Apple Bloom got her teeth in the scruff of her neck like Applejack does with the barn kittens and pulled, and Scootaloo came, without a word. Her legs just started going. Sweetie Belle came past on the other side, and the three of them went into the trees together, downhill, because downhill was toward water and water was toward town and that was the only thing Apple Bloom knew for sure, and behind them the light was getting bigger, and Scootaloo's hooves were going wrong, tripping, catching, and Apple Bloom still had her by the scruff and wasn't letting go, and her jaw hurt, and she could taste smoke, and Sweetie Belle was saying "I can see — I think I can see —" and there was a bank ahead, a steep one, dark, and Apple Bloom didn't slow down for it.

## 2. Theta

*[What's burning]* went out from four of them at once, and Madder was one of the four.

Haw was the fifth, and was sending something else over the top of it.

*[I was falling fast ~ still falling, thrilled with it]*

*[Your leg's on my back]*

*[Whose leg, which one, I've got six—]*

*[Why is it heavy]*

*[It's hot on the left. It's hot on the right. Madder, it's hot on—]*

*[Sloe, that's my arm you're lying on]*

*[I'm not lying on anything, something's lying on me ~ muffled, indignant, and under that frightened]*

Madder got an eye open, and saw it.

Flame. Not a red mark on a deck plan, not a drill: the thing itself, standing up out of the ferns an arm's length away, yellow and tearing, with nothing between it and them but air. All of them went rigid in the same instant. Somebody whined. Madder's ears had fanned wide against the heat before hir knew hir had them.

*[FIRE ~ all of them, on top of each other]*

*[Where's suppression, where's the—]*

*[Seal it, seal the—]*

*[There's no hatch. There's no wall. There's nothing to seal]*

They had gone down at the end of second watch, in the web by the coolant bay. They had come up in a heap, and nothing else was right. A heap in the web drifted. This one had gone flat like a sack put down, so that every body pressed hard into the one below. Madder was near the bottom, where Sloe's weight lay across hir ribs, and the ground under all of them was not a deck.

Hir put the checklist up. *Heads. Hands. Air. Exits.* Hir got as far as hands before hir saw it. Right against the heap, touching it, stood a box on wheels. Yarrow's lower left hand was locked on its tailboard, closed there while hir slept. Then four other threads came in over the checklist, and hir lost the place.

*[There's a box. There's a box on wheels, Yarrow's holding a box on wheels]*

*[Something ran]*

That one was Teasel's, and it came clean, with nothing under it at all.

Madder swivelled both ears downhill. Something was running down there, small and fast and more than one. A smell came up the slope after it, sharp and sour like a pump seal going, until the fire covered it.

*[Where? What? How many? Is it coming back? Can I—]*

*[Two. Three? Small ~ counting, not sure of the count]*

*[Let it go,]* Madder sent, and Haw let it go, mostly.

Hir reached for the frame, which gave hir five before hir had finished asking. Sloe was hot and loud; Haw was louder; Yarrow was level as a strut; Teasel was held small and tight. And hirself. Five, all there.

Then, in the same instant, with nobody calling it, all five of them reached past each other for the ship: for fourteen hundred others, and for the Hum that had run under every hour of Madder's hundred and forty years. They found the same thing together. There was nothing in the sky.

The fright went out through the frame and came back off four other bodies, doubled and doubled, before Madder could have said whose it had been first. Every tail went tight at once, round a leg, round a wrist, round each other, until the heap was pulled in closer. Somebody made a noise through the nose, high and thin, like a line singing under load. It might have been hir.

*[Where's the bay]*

*[Where's anyone]*

*[If the Nest had five of us come off the table in one second—]*

*[Don't]*

*[I'm only saying what it looks like from there ~ and wishing hir wasn't]*

*[That's what it looks like when somebody—]* That was Teasel, clean, and it stopped halfway. Teasel's tail came round Madder's wrist and stayed.

*[Unconfirmed,]* Madder sent.

Nobody accepted it. Nobody argued with it either. The fright went round and round between them without settling; whenever it thinned in one body, it came back off another. Sloe was whining, very low, on every breath out. Madder counted hir own breaths until hir lost the count, then started again. When hir asked the Core for the time, it said ninety seconds. When hir asked again it said four minutes, though nothing had changed except the light on the underside of the leaves, which had gone from yellow to orange.

---

When Sloe rolled off hir, Madder's chest did not come up after the weight had gone, so hir had to lift it. Hir got hir four lower hands under hir before hir pushed. Hir tail lay out behind hir like somebody else's cargo. One of hir hind hands had found Teasel's foot, and it stayed there.

Hir ears came slowly forward. Hir asked the Core.

*One point zero two standard,* the Core said.

Hir asked it for the air.

*Breathable. Oxygen twenty-one percent. Water vapour high. Volatile organics: several hundred, unclassified.*

Hir asked it for the time.

*Six minutes forty seconds since theta release.*

Four other pairs of ears had come forward round hir while hir asked. The others had asked theirs too, so the channel filled with readings that all arrived at once, every one of them intact.

*[One g. One g! We're on a surface,]* Haw sent.

*[Air's richer than ours,]* from Sloe. *[Twenty-one percent, and we pay for nineteen. Taste it. You can taste it]*

*[It smells,]* Teasel sent. *[It smells of everything]*

*[It's a planet, it's a planet, which planet, it's too heavy for a moon, is it a moon, is it one of ours?]* That was Haw again, stacking them.

*[Lifeboat,]* Sloe sent. *[Somebody put us in a lifeboat asleep and—]*

*[No boat,]* Yarrow sent.

*[A sim, then. It's a sim. It's a training sim and Ilex is laughing at us from—]* Haw.

*[Sims don't smell,]* Teasel sent.

*[Or we're dead ~ Haw, delighted by the idea for a second, then not]*

*[If this is dead I want a word with somebody,]* Sloe sent.

*[Heads,]* Madder sent, flat, into the middle of it. *[Hands]*

It went under the noise. When hir sent it again, it went under again, like a rota posted on a hatch nobody used. And hir could feel the fire. It stood in a ring round the wagon and the heap, in the ferns on both sides of the old track where they had woken, pushing heat into hir face. There was no hatch to shut on it. It was out in the air, eating, while nothing came. Every ear in the heap had fanned wide against it. Haw let out a huff that shook.

Yarrow still had hir lower hand on the tailboard.

Smoke was coming off the bed behind that hand, low and yellow, where two sacks lay slumped on charred planks. While Madder watched, a red line crawled along the fold of one of them.

Nobody sent anything. Nobody had to. Sloe was up before the thought had finished, and had the near sack by a cool corner and over the side. Haw took the other. Teasel and Madder scraped up dirt in double handfuls and threw it where the sacks came down, while Yarrow knelt by the mirror and beat the glowing boards with the flats of hir upper hands until they were black. Somebody was counting the beat under hir breath. Twelve. Fourteen. Then the sacks were two dark humps under dirt, and the bed was only hot.

For one breath the frame went smooth, the way it did at the end of a drill before anybody called the time. They knew this. Out of everything here, they knew this. Haw's ears came up, and Sloe let a breath out through the nose that was nearly a chirr.

Then the ferns went on burning on both sides of the track, where no handful of dirt would reach, and the breath was over.

It was a wagon. They all knew the word once they saw it properly: a flat bed on four wheels, with two shafts out the front, and straps and a buckle. Something small and red was caught in the buckle. Down its side, big and a little uneven, somebody had painted letters, which the heat had blistered into bubbles.

Sloe read them first.

*[APPLE BLOOM]*

Then, while everyone was still getting it: *[That's our alphabet. That's our alphabet, we can read that]*

Somebody chirred, a small sound, surprised out of them.

*[Apple Bloom!]* Haw sent. *[Apple Bloom. It's a crate stencil. It's a produce brand. It's cider, I bet it's cider—]*

*[It's done by hand,]* Sloe sent. *[Look at the strokes. Somebody did this with a brush. The B's the wrong way round ~ fond of whoever it was, and surprised to be]*

*[So there are people,]* from Sloe, straight after. *[Somebody paints. Somebody sells paint]*

*[So it's Earth!]* Haw sent.

*[Earth's one g,]* Teasel sent.

*[So's half the colony list,]* Madder sent.

*[So is it humans, are there humans, where are they, did the humans put us—]* Haw, of course.

*[Unconfirmed]*

*[You always say that,]* Haw sent.

*[It's always true]*

Madder pulled hir tail round until it was under hir. Then hir stood up on hir four lower hands with hir arms free, as hir had ten thousand times on a hull when there was a hand on the rail. There was no rail. Hir swayed without going over, and felt four people watching hir through the frame while they decided not to say so.

"Rota," hir said aloud.

It came out rough, with dirt in hir mouth. The ferns gave nothing back. Hir said the rest slowly, putting every word down on its own.

"Haw, point, inside ten metres of me. Sloe, left flank. Yarrow, rear. Teasel, with me. Watches change on the hour by the Core. We go downhill, on the burnt ground." Hir stopped, then added, "Now."

For a moment the channel was empty, which never happened with this Cluster. Speech, out loud, among Kin, with no human anywhere: somebody had got up on a chair.

*[~ a laugh, from Haw, stopped halfway out]*

*[Logged ~ very clean for Sloe]*

Haw went to point, came back to ask where point was, and went again. Teasel did not move until Madder put a hand on hir shoulder. Sloe crouched at the front of the wagon with one hip against Yarrow's leg, while hir worked the red thing out of the buckle: a strip of something soft, tied in a loop.

*[One wagon,]* Sloe sent. *[Two sacks, burnt through, empty. One mirror, no glass in it. One ribbon, red. Call it nothing, call it a day's water if we find a buyer. That's stores]* Hir put the ribbon over hir wrist and pulled it tight. *[~ laughing, not much]*

The mirror lay on its back in the wagon bed, a rim of dark metal round nothing, still too hot to touch. Yarrow got it out anyway, with hir ears fanned wide and a burnt sack from the dirt wrapped round both upper hands. Hir stood it on its edge against the nearest trunk. When hir took hir hands off the metal rim, hir put one of them flat on the bark and left it there.

*[Yarrow]*

*[Holds itself up,]* Yarrow sent. *[No guys. Nothing on it]*

Madder ran hir RADAR up the trunk. It went up and split, then split again, with no line or strut anywhere, and still it stood.

*[Leave the wagon,]* hir sent.

Yarrow did not leave the wagon. Hir lower left hand had opened a finger at a time; now it closed again on the tailboard, on purpose. Then hir went round to the front and took up the shafts.

*[It rolls,]* Yarrow sent.

Madder let hir have it.

Downhill to the left, there was a strip where the fire had already been. Madder's eyes gave hir a black lane through the ferns, while the near-field gave hir the same lane, cooler than everything round it. It ran away from the fire, which was moving uphill and right with the air. Hir had marked it as the exit before hir had finished standing up.

As soon as they moved off, the channel went.

*[Is it always this heavy? Do you get used to it? Does anyone live like this?]* from Haw, already three metres past point.

*[Smoke, sap, something rotting, something sweet, and about twelve things I can't name,]* Sloe sent. *[Two of those I'd pay for. The rest I'd pay to have taken away]*

*[Did we do this,]* Teasel sent, clean, looking back up at the burning ring.

*[Came through,]* Yarrow sent, from between the shafts.

Nobody took that any further.

*[Where did the running ones go? Are they down there? Are we following them?]*

*[Point, Haw,]* Madder sent. *[Ten metres]*

They went down the strip.

---

The ash came up round them in a cloud wherever a foot came down. They went as a clump, close enough to knock shoulders: Teasel's flank against Madder's, Sloe's tail across the back of Madder's leg, Haw's point shrinking to three metres, then two. Inside ten paces Madder came down onto six, so that hir upper hands took weight they had never been asked for.

Teasel sneezed.

Hir whole body jerked with it before hir sat down in the ash. Every heart in the Cluster went up through the frame and came down again.

*[What was that, what was THAT, do it again—]*

*[No,]* Teasel sent.

*[Are you hurt?]*

*[I'm fine,]* Teasel sent. Hir got up and brushed hir front. Hir ears were flat. Madder could feel hir under the frame, working at something while hir kept it down.

The clearing at the bottom of the strip was small, with pale stems round the edge and thin grass in the middle. The fire had come this far before it went out, so the grass was only singed. In the middle, on its side, lay the animal.

None of them had seen one anywhere but in a record. This one was the size of a hand, grey and brown, burnt black along the back, with a long tail flattened like a paddle. Its eyes were shut. Its feet were curled under it, with something like fingers.

Haw got there first and stopped so hard that Sloe walked into hir. Sloe stayed there, chest to Haw's back. Somebody made a thin sound, a whine through the teeth.

Madder's ears came forward. Hir asked for small mammals. *Forty-one records,* the Core said. *No exact match.*

*[Is it one of them? The ones that ran?]*

*[Too small,]* Sloe sent.

*[Is it asleep?]*

Nobody answered that. Yarrow came up past the wagon and knelt, then turned the animal over with one finger. The underside of it was not burnt. It was pale and soft; when Yarrow touched it the fur moved, and nothing else did.

*[It's got a tail,]* Teasel sent.

*[It was here first,]* Sloe sent.

*[Did we kill it,]* Haw sent. There was nothing under it. Haw had never sent a clean line in hir life.

*[It was already—]* from Sloe, and it stopped.

*[Unconfirmed,]* Madder sent, and all of them felt it be the wrong word. Yarrow sent nothing at all.

Haw put out one hand and laid it on the soft pale fur beside Yarrow's. Hir rider went out for a second, not stripped, only gone, as if there was nothing left for it to ride on.

*[Moving,]* Madder sent.

Haw left the hand where it was a moment longer than that. Then hir took it back and went to point.

---

On the far side of the clearing the ground did not slope. It dropped straight down between the trunks, with no track on it and nothing burnt, as if it belonged to a different hill altogether. They came down it badly. Haw and Sloe were on the shafts, while Yarrow went behind with all four lower hands on the tailboard, holding the wagon back whenever it tried to run them over.

Halfway down, the front left wheel gave. It had burned through at the hub while they slept, and when it went it went all at once: a crack, a lurch, the spokes folding in. The wagon slewed. Haw and Sloe let go of the shafts before it could take them with it. It went over on its side, slid a body-length through the leaf litter, and stopped against a root.

APPLE BLOOM lay uppermost, blistered, facing the leaves.

Yarrow had not let go. The tailboard had pulled hir down onto hir knees, and hir stayed there with both lower hands on the wood. Through the frame hir went level and very still, the way a strut goes still just before it takes a load it was never rated for. Then hir hands opened, a finger at a time, until they were off. Nobody sent anything. Hir lower left hand closed once more, on nothing, before hir got up.

They left it there.

At the bottom was a hollow, cooler, full of a sound Madder had never heard: water running over stones, on and on, wider than any pipe. It came from up the slope to the right before it went away to the left. On its near bank there was a flat-topped stone the size of a table.

Haw was on the stone before anyone could send anything.

*[It's cold! It's wet on top. Everything here is wet. Sloe, come and—]*

Something on the stone moved.

It had been part of the stone. Madder's RADAR had it as a lump on the edge, and then it wasn't; it was a thing with legs that went off the stone in one long shove, past Haw's face, and hit the water with a loud slap.

The terror came out of Haw first and then out of everyone, and every one of them felt it off all the others, so there was no floor under it anywhere. Haw came off the stone backwards and landed on Sloe, and both their tails whipped round each other and locked. Teasel screamed, a thin note that climbed out of hearing and went on. Madder found hirself flat against the bank with every limb dug in and hir tail wrapped twice round hir own hind legs, and could not have said when. Only Yarrow had not moved. Yarrow had hir hand flat on a trunk and hir tail coiled round its root, and was pressed so close against it that bark came away under hir fingers.

The water went on running. Out in it, further off, something small went *slap* again.

Nobody sent anything for a long time. Their hearts went too fast and did not come down. Madder asked the Core for the time, and it told hir. It had been nine seconds. Hir asked it what the thing had been.

*Amphibian,* the Core said.

That helped nobody.

*[It was very small,]* Haw sent at last. It was meant for a joke and didn't make it.

Sloe got up from under Haw and went to Teasel, who was folded tight on the bank with hir tail wrapped round hirself and hir ears flat to hir skull. Sloe put hir head down by Teasel's head and said something on the whistle, too high and too near for the frame to carry. When Sloe lifted hir head, Teasel's ears had come up a little. That was all Madder got.

*[Downstream,]* hir sent. *[Stay off the stones]*

---

They went down the stream all night.

It did not get lighter. The crew walked in a knot, with somebody's hand always on somebody's back. The fire was behind them and above them. Whenever Madder looked back there was a smear of orange on the underside of the leaves, a little further round the hill to the right each time, while the smoke came down after them and lay along the water. On the hour hir asked the Core for the time and changed the watch. Sloe went to rear. Yarrow came up on the left, where hir lower left hand kept opening and closing on nothing.

The talking came back within the first hour and went on for a long while.

*[How long does night go, here? Hours? Days? Does it come back every time?]* from Haw.

*[Unconfirmed,]* Madder sent.

*[Do the lights come on? Is there a switch? Who runs it?]* Haw again.

*[Nine degrees down since the clearing,]* Teasel sent, and then, a second later, correcting it: *[Ten]*

The cold came up off the water until it got into the pads of their hands. Above them the whole roof of leaves moved at once, and kept moving, with a long rushing sound that came and went.

*[What's moving the air?]* Teasel sent.

*[No fans,]* Yarrow sent.

*[No coats, no light, no food, and one ribbon,]* Sloe sent. *[If somebody offered to buy us I'd want to know what they'd done wrong]*

Something called on the far bank, a long note that fell away at the end. Every ear swivelled to it.

*[Is that one of them? Is it talking? Is it talking to us? Should we answer?]*

*[No,]* from Madder and Yarrow at once.

*[Forty years of rotas,]* Madder sent, *[and I never once put a night on one]*

Somewhere in the second hour hir put a lower hand down on something that bit. When hir lifted the hand, there were three hooked burrs in the pad of it, brown, the size of peas. Hir asked while hir picked. *Seed heads,* the Core said. *Hooked, for carriage on fur.* Once they came off the pad they stuck to hir fingers. When hir got them off those, they stuck to the others.

*[It's shipping itself on you,]* Sloe sent. *[Freight, no manifest, and it isn't paying]*

In the end hir walked on with them in hir hand and stopped trying.

In the third hour the trees came apart over the water for a stretch, and there was the moon.

It stood in the gap, white and nearly round, marked with grey, and laid a road of light down the stream. Somebody drew a sharp breath. They all stopped. Their ears came slowly forward together while Madder asked, and the Core gave hir what it had: *Natural satellite. Reflected light.* Hir had a thousand moons in records, though hir had never seen one.

*[That's a moon,]* Haw sent. *[That's a moon, that's a real one, how far is it, is it always there, is it ours? Whose is it?]*

*[Something's lighting it,]* Sloe sent. *[So there's a sun. Round the far side of all this, there's a sun]*

*[It's bigger than the records,]* Teasel sent.

*[~ Yarrow, nothing but a long look]*

They stood in the cold and looked at it until the trees closed over it again.

In the fourth hour Haw sent, *[Is it second watch at home?]*

The frame went quiet. It had not occurred to Madder to wonder. When hir asked for the last watch, it came up word for word: the web by the coolant bay, the lights down, hir own voice on the channel. *[Web in ten. Haw, off the rail.]* And Haw's answer under it, *[~ grumbling, going]*. It was all there, down to the coolant trunk breathing along the wall.

Hir did not send any of it.

*[Unconfirmed,]* hir sent.

*[They'll be at the web,]* Sloe sent. *[Our end of it. Somebody'll be sleeping in our end]*

*[Somebody'll want our rota,]* Haw sent.

*[Nobody wants our rota,]* from Teasel, dry, and for a moment it was very like hir.

*[Dunlin would take the rigging,]* Sloe sent.

*[Dunlin can't rig,]* Yarrow sent.

*[Dunlin can rig!]* from Haw.

*[If the bay—]* Haw again.

*[If the ship—]* That was Sloe.

It came round to the reactor, as it had to, then went round it without anybody saying it. Madder felt the whole weight of it going through the frame between them, where it found nowhere to land, and went on.

The halts got longer after that. At each one Yarrow went to the nearest trunk and put a hand flat on it, and stood there until they moved on. Nobody asked hir what hir was checking.

In the sixth hour Teasel sent, *[Is anyone else hungry?]*

It came clean, as everything from Teasel had since they woke. Under it, through the frame, Teasel's hum held flat and tight as a cable at full load.

*[Stores are a ribbon,]* Sloe sent. *[I'm not giving you the ribbon]*

*[I'm fine. I only wondered]*

*[It all smells like food,]* Haw sent. *[And none of it is]*

As the stream widened it got louder, while the talk thinned to single threads until it stopped.

The eighth hour had no talk in it. There was the stream. There were the burrs in Madder's hand. Every few minutes Haw came back from point to put a hand on Sloe's shoulder before going away again, and each time Sloe's shoulder leaned into it. Teasel walked with hir tail hooked through Yarrow's. Behind them the orange had gone further round the hill.

---

The trees stopped.

First they thinned, until there was only one; after it, open ground went down to the water in a long green slope. Grey light was coming up ahead of them, low and wide, on the side away from the fire.

Haw made a small chirr and did not seem to hear it.

A small bridge of planks went over the stream. On the far side the hill had a door in it, a real door, with windows beside it and grass on the roof, as if the hill had only let a house in partway. Little wooden boxes stood on poles and walls and up in the trees round it, dozens of them, each with a round hole in the front. Off to one side stood a shed on legs, where a plank ran up to it like a ramp.

Madder counted exits: the bridge, the path going away from the door, the stream, which hir did not want. Hir sent nothing. The Cluster stood at the edge of the trees in the ash they had carried down on their fur, pressed shoulder to flank. Sloe had the ribbon on hir wrist. Yarrow's hand, with nothing to hold, opened and closed at hir side.

At the top of the ramp, a small hatch opened.

A bird came out. It was brown, with a red comb. Madder's ears came forward while hir asked. *Poultry,* the Core said. After the bird came another, then another, until there was a line of them coming down the ramp in the grey light, one behind the other, talking to themselves.

Then, one after another, every one of them stopped dead.

## 3. The Yard

The lid of the feed bin had stuck since last spring, when the damp got into the hinge. Fluttershy had a way with it by now: a hoof on the front corner, her shoulder against the side, and a sharp knock underneath with the scoop. "Come on, you," she told it. She was on the knock when the hens came down the ramp.

She heard them before she looked. Marigold came first, as she always did, talking to nopony, with Bess close behind her, and after those two the rest came down in whatever order they liked, except that Pepper was always last and always cross about it. Fluttershy liked to hear them from the bin. It meant the hatch had held another night.

The lid came up. The talking on the ramp stopped.

It didn't trail off. It stopped between one cluck and the next, so that she turned round with the scoop still in her mouth. All eleven hens were standing on the ramp in a line, each with one foot up, staring at the stream.

There were five of them at the edge of the trees on the far bank, just past the other end of the plank bridge.

Her first thought was that they were grey, and that was wrong: the grey came off them when one of them moved, so it was ash, a whole coat of it, and under it they were darker. They were long and low, and they stood on four feet with two more held up off the ground in front of them. The two front ones had hands on the ends. Their ears were enormous. Their eyes were set in the fronts of their faces, and all ten of those eyes were looking the same way as the hens.

Fluttershy had spent her life with animals whose eyes were on the sides of their heads, and with the animals that ate them.

Then Marigold screamed, and the ramp came apart.

Hens went off it on both sides, flapping, half flying and half falling. Two went under the hen house. One went straight over the woodpile, which was the worst place, and one went up onto the cottage roof, where she had never been in her life and would not know how to get down. The rabbits on the lawn were gone before the hens had landed, every one of them, into the hedge and under the porch and down the holes by the rhubarb. Up in the birdhouses the starlings, who had eggs, came out in a rush and went round and round over the yard in a tight black knot, shrieking. Under all of it, from across the water, came a thin whine and then a huff, short and sharp, from more than one throat.

Fluttershy put the scoop down.

"Marigold," she said. "Bess. In." She said it in the voice she used for a hen that had got into the kitchen, which was quiet and flat and did not ask. She walked toward the hen house without hurrying. Hurrying was what a fox did. "In. Hattie. Pepper, under, good girl. In."

Four of them went in. That was four. Up on the roof, the hen who had never been there before put her head over the edge of the grass and looked down at her, and Fluttershy told her under her breath exactly what she thought of her.

When she looked up, the strangers at the bridge had gone still.

All of them had stopped moving at once, as if somepony she couldn't hear had spoken. Their heads came round together to look at her, not one after another but in the same instant, and they stayed turned. Her legs had gone thin and shaky under her. It made her angry. She had not been angry at dawn in a long time. It had been such a good spring: nests in every birdhouse and two in the hedge, and three weeks ago the vixen she had raised from a kit had come back and moved under the woodpile with two kits of her own, as if she had always meant to.

One of them came down to the far end of the bridge and out onto the first of the planks. It walked on its four back feet with its hands held up off the wood, slowly, rocking a little, as if the ground were further away than it expected. It stopped well short of the middle.

"What do you want," Fluttershy said. It came out small and hard, which surprised her.

It looked at her and said nothing.

It was only a moment, but it was a moment too long, and none of the others moved while it lasted. She had the feeling of being weighed, or of somepony checking a thing against a list in another room.

"Water," it said. Its voice was warm and low, with something thin and high running under it like a whistle behind a door. It put the words down one at a time, carefully. "And the way to the nearest town."

"The stream's right there."

It took a moment over that too. "Is it safe to drink?"

"The deer drink it," she said.

It seemed to think about that. Then it said, "I'm Madder," and waited, and when she did not give her own name back it did not ask for it. It turned its head, and the four at the tree line came down to the water on their own side together, as though it had called them. Madder went back off the planks to join them.

---

She had eleven hens to find and a jackdaw in a crate by the stove who had to be fed every two hours or he would stop trying. So she went on with the morning. There was nothing else to do with it.

She got Hattie out from under the hen house with a handful of oats and Dimity off the woodpile by lifting her bodily, while the vixen's eyes watched her from the dark inside the logs. The vixen did not come out. On any other morning she would have come to the end of the pile by now to see whether there was anything in it for her. Today she stayed where she was, her kits somewhere behind her, and only her eyes showed.

Fluttershy fed the jackdaw. He had started taking the grubs off the tweezers before she offered them, and in a week or so he would be ready to go. She would stand at the back door after he went, the way she always did, feeling foolish. From the kitchen window she could see the strangers on the far bank by the bridge. They drank lying down with their faces in the water, all in a row, pressed up against each other along their whole sides. When one got up, the one next to it put a hand on its back. She had seen puppies do that in a basket.

The sun came over the hill. She went out and filled the water trays under the birdhouses, keeping them at the edge of her eye, and she saw their ears change. As the light reached the far bank, the ears went out wide and stiff to either side of their heads, all five pairs together. She had seen a vixen's ears go like that when something came too near the den. She put the tray down and stood very still. "Don't," she said, very quietly, to none of them in particular. None of them came any nearer. They sat in the sun on their side of the water with their ears spread like that, looking at nothing, and after a while she went and did the rabbits' greens with her back to them. She did not turn round until she had finished.

The rabbits would not come out for the greens. She left the pile on the back step. Angel came, eventually, and ate as if nothing had happened. The others stayed in the hedge.

It was the one with the ribbon who came down next. She was on her way back from the hedge with the empty greens basket when it came to the far end of the bridge and sat down there, on its own bank, with the stream between them.

She noticed the ribbon before anything else about it. It was tied tight round the thing's wrist, in a loop: something red, or that had been red, gone brown and stiff along one edge where it had burned. It caught at her. She stood at the near end of the bridge and looked at it, and it caught at her the way a word does when it's nearly there. Then the one wearing it said, "Hello," and she lost it.

"Sorry about your birds," it said. "Is it birds? They're birds. I'm Sloe. That's Madder, you've met, hir's in charge, or hir thinks so. That's Haw, the one on the rail. Don't mind Haw. That's Yarrow, and the small one is Teasel. We came down at last light. We've been walking since—" It stopped. Its ears came forward, slowly, both at once, and stayed there a moment before it went on. "Since last light. It's a lot of walking, down here."

Yarrow was the big one, then: the biggest of them, and the only one that had not made a sound all morning. It had come down behind Sloe to the end of the bridge, and while Sloe talked it ran a hand along the ends of the planks, one after another, and then along the rope that held them, and gave the rope a slow pull, as if to find out what it was tied to.

"The one on the rail" was on the bridge, hanging head down off the side of it by its back feet and its tail, looking down into the water. The back feet were wrapped round the edge plank the way a hand holds a branch.

"What do you take for water?" Sloe said.

Fluttershy didn't understand.

"For the water. The stream. Who do we pay?" It held its wrist up, with the ribbon on it, and turned it this way and that. "We haven't got much. We haven't got anything, if I'm honest. We've got this. I can do you a very good price on this."

"It's a stream," she said. "Nopony owns it."

Sloe took a moment over that. "Then who decides who drinks from it?"

"Nopony decides. You just drink it."

Sloe looked at the stream, and then at her, and its ears went out wide again in the sun. "Right," it said. "That's either very good or very bad, and I can't tell which yet."

Fluttershy very nearly laughed, and was cross with herself for it.

"What's your name?" Sloe said.

She hadn't meant to tell it. It was only that it asked like a pony at the market asks, easy, as if it had a right to know and it would be strange not to answer. "Fluttershy."

"Fluttershy," Sloe said. Behind it, on the far bank, Teasel's ears came up, and Madder looked round.

After that they all used it. They used it every time, at the start of anything they said, as if they had been told that was polite and wanted to be sure. Madder came down to the end of the bridge after Sloe and said, "Fluttershy. How far to the town?" and when she told it, it said, "Fluttershy. Which way does the path go from your door?" and when she told it that, it said, "Fluttershy. Is there anywhere near here that is cool out of the sun?" Every time, it stopped first and looked at her, and then said the thing, and every word was put down separately.

The small one, Teasel, did not come down to the bridge. It stayed at the back of the others on the far bank with its tail wrapped round its own feet. When she went past with the grain, it watched the grain. When she tipped the rabbits' pellets out at the hedge, it watched the pellets. When Angel sat on the back step and ate a dandelion leaf from one end to the other, it watched that too, all the way to the end, and its ears came forward. She looked at it and it looked away.

---

Pepper was the last hen out. By the middle of the morning she was still out, on the near bank under the near end of the bridge, in a dip in the grass where she had decided she lived now. Fluttershy had been leaving her until the others were settled. Pepper could not be hurried; she had to be let come round to it.

Fluttershy was carrying eggs in a basket in her mouth when she saw that Haw had crossed. It had come off the bridge on her side, the only one of them that had, and it was lying in the grass very close to Pepper, with its face nearly touching her. It was watching her breathe. Each time her feathers rose and settled, Haw's ears went a little further forward. It had kept that up for a minute or so, which was the longest she had seen it keep still for anything. Fluttershy put the basket down on the step.

Haw moved.

It didn't move the way anything she knew moves toward a hen. It came up off the grass all at once and got Pepper round the middle and round the legs and by a wing, with more hands than she had counted. The feet it had been standing on all morning were hands as well. She saw the thumbs close on Pepper's legs. Pepper came up into the air in a flurry, upside down, screaming, while feathers went everywhere.

"Put her down," Fluttershy said.

It came out of her low and flat, and it scraped her throat on the way.

Haw had started to say something. It stopped on a small sound, half a squeak, down in its throat. All of them went still. Haw stood on the bank with Pepper in its hands and turned its head to look at her, and so did the other four, at the same instant, from across the water: Madder at the far end of the bridge, Sloe at the edge of the stream, the big quiet one against the willow on that bank, and Teasel at the back. For a moment nothing in the yard moved except Pepper.

Then Haw put her down. It put her down on her feet, carefully, and she went off across the grass in a straight line, flat out, with her neck stretched and her wings half open.

"She's very fast!" Haw said. It said it out loud, delighted, too loud, like a foal at a fair.

"She's frightened."

It looked at Pepper going, then at Fluttershy, and its ears went down a little. "Oh," it said.

Pepper went into the hedge and did not come out. The starlings, who had just begun to settle, went up again. Haw went back over the bridge to the others.

Fluttershy picked up the egg basket. She did not look at Haw again. She took the eggs in and fed the jackdaw, who ate, and then she stood at the kitchen counter for a little while with her eyes shut, and said a word the hens had never heard from her, quietly, to the counter.

---

When she came out again the smoke had got into the air.

She could smell it now in the yard, not strongly, only enough to tell it was there. The sky over the forest had a brown edge to it. She stood on the step and looked up at it. Across the stream, the strangers had their faces tipped up to it too.

Somewhere up in the trees, a long way off, something cracked.

It went on cracking, a long tearing sound, and ended in a thump she felt through her hooves. A tree had come down. On the far bank all of them dropped flat to the grass at once, and a noise came off them, several noises together, a yelp and a whine and something like a cough. Their tails had wound tight, round each other or round their own bodies. Only then did their heads come round toward the sound. The rabbits who had crept back to the greens at last went off in every direction.

One of them went the wrong way.

It was the smallest of the April litter, a grey doe with a nick out of one ear from the time the hedge wire caught her. She went across the lawn in three bounds and onto the bridge, where her feet drummed on the planks, and then she was over it and in under the trees, going up the slope, toward the brown in the sky.

On the far bank Madder came up off the grass and went a step after her, quick and low, toward the trees. Another hand shot out and caught its arm, and it stopped. Fluttershy saw that, and did not know what she had seen.

She went after the doe.

She went over the bridge at a run, past them on the bank without looking at them, and in under the first of the trees. Then she stopped.

It was darker under there than it ought to be at that time of day. The smoke lay low along the ground between the trunks, a thin grey haze, not moving much. It smelled of burned leaves and of something else, something like hot metal, that she didn't know. The Everfree went up the slope in front of her with no path in it, and somewhere in it there was fire, and she did not know where.

She called. Once, not loud.

Nothing came. A bird she didn't recognise called back from somewhere to the right, a long falling note.

She knew what smoke did to a chest as small as that. She knew it better than she wanted to, from the summer the hay barn at the Pinwheel place burned and she had sat up with what they brought her. She knew what else was in the Everfree, too, fire or no fire. She stood at the edge of the trees with her front hooves on the last of the grass and her hind ones on the leaf litter, and she did not go further, and she knew that she was not going to.

She waited as long as she could stand it. Then she came back out from under the trees.

They were in a knot on the bank by the bridge. They had drawn in close since she ran past; Sloe had one hand on Haw's back, and Teasel's tail was wound through Yarrow's. They were all looking at her. None of them said anything. She went past them and back over the bridge.

She went and moved the rest of the greens inside the hutch, where the others would have to come in for them.

---

Rainbow Dash came down at a quarter to twelve, by the kitchen clock, and landed by the hen house hard enough to make the hatch rattle.

"Fluttershy. Hey. Sorry. There's a fire in the Everfree, up above you, it's been going since last night. The team's being called in, so I need you to keep your animals shut in this afternoon, all of them, and—"

She saw them.

Fluttershy watched her see them. Dash did not say anything, which was not like her. Her wings came in tight against her sides, and she stood there on the grass with her eyes going from one to the next, the whole row of them on the far bank, and her mouth shut. There was a smear of soot along the top of her left wing, right across the blue, and she plainly didn't know it was there. Fluttershy wanted to brush it off for her, and stayed where she was.

Then she reached round into her saddlebag and took out a notebook and a pencil.

"Okay," she said. She opened the notebook against her foreleg and licked the end of the pencil. "Weather team. I'm going to ask you some questions. Were you in the forest last night?"

Across the stream nothing moved. Then Madder came down to the far end of the bridge on its four back feet with its hands up. Dash went to the near end, and they faced each other over the width of the water. There was the pause, and Fluttershy saw Dash notice it, saw her ears flick.

"Yes."

"Did you see the fire?"

"Yes."

"Where did it start?"

"On the old track that comes down from the high ground," Madder said. "On a rise, facing downhill. It burned in a ring round where we were, on both sides of the track, about twelve metres across. A strip ran off it to the left of the track, downhill, about a hundred metres long and five wide, into a clearing. It went out there. The rest was moving uphill, and to the right, with the air."

Dash was writing. She stopped writing. "Metres," she said.

"Yes."

"Okay." She wrote it. "When?"

"Sixteen hours and nine minutes ago."

Dash looked up.

"At last light," Madder said, as though that might help. "We last saw the glow of it seven hours and fifty minutes ago, from inside the trees, before first light. It was round the hill to the right of the way we came down. It had gone further round each time we looked. We looked eight times."

Fluttershy saw Dash's pencil hanging over the page. Dash had told her once, laughing, that you asked a farmer when a fire started and he said *round supper*. She was not laughing now. She wrote the numbers down very neatly, one under another, and went over the nine twice.

"What were you doing on the track?" she said.

The pause was longer this time. None of them moved.

"Waking up," Madder said.

Dash wrote that down too. Fluttershy watched her do it. Then Dash said, not looking up, "Did you start it?"

"We were there when it started."

Dash's pencil stopped. She stood a moment with it on the paper, then closed the notebook on it and put the whole thing back in her bag, and came back up the bank to where Fluttershy was standing by the feed bin, and stood close.

"Nopony talks like that," she said, low. "Nopony knows what time anything happened at a fire. You're lucky if they know what day." She looked back across the stream. Nothing over there had moved. "Are you okay? Have they done anything?"

"They caught a hen," Fluttershy said. "They let her go."

Dash looked at her for a moment as if she were waiting for more, and when there wasn't any, she let it go. "Okay. So. We're on this whole edge from about two. We can't go in, you know what the Everfree's like, it does its own weather and it won't take ours. So we soak the outside and hope the fire doesn't feel like coming out." She blew her fringe out of her eyes. "Half the team think it isn't even our job. The captain says it is. The captain's been off the ground about twice since winter, but sure. So it's our job. There's going to be twenty ponies coming in low over your yard with full clouds, and they're dropping them right along the stream. Both banks, from the trees all the way down to the Pinwheel fields. Nopony's going to be looking where it lands. Anything standing on either side of the water gets flattened."

"I know."

"So they can't be here." Dash rubbed her face with a hoof. "I've got to write it all up. I'm in the book already this month, Fluttershy, I can't have five of—" She stopped. "They can't be in the line. That's it."

Fluttershy looked past her at the far bank.

Sloe had its wrist held against its chest, with the ribbon on it, and was facing Haw, whose ears were down. Its mouth barely moved. What came out of it was thin and high, right at the edge of what Fluttershy could hear, and none of the other four turned toward it. Madder was standing a little in front of the others, where it always stood. Yarrow had a hand flat on the trunk of the willow on that bank; its other hand closed on nothing, and opened, and closed again. The little one, Teasel, was watching the hens, who had come back out at last and were picking at the grain in the yard; it watched their beaks go down and come up, and its ears were forward, and it didn't seem to know it was doing it. The vixen had not come out of the woodpile all morning. The starlings were still up. The grey doe with the nick in her ear was somewhere up in the smoke, and she had not come back over the bridge.

She had been up since before it was light. She still had the nest boxes to do, and the jackdaw at one, and the larks. All week she had meant to take her tea down to the south meadow one morning and sit in the long grass while the larks went up, and all week there had been something else.

She went down to the near end of the bridge. On the far bank, all five of them turned their heads to her at once.

"There's an old duck house at the bottom of the south meadow, past the willows, and nothing's nesting in it this year," she said. "Go round the edge of the grass, not across it. The larks are down in there. I'll bring what I can when I do them at four, and if I find one egg broken, you'll find somewhere else."

---

## 4. The Tab

Fluttershy had brought nineteen eggs at four o'clock, under a cloth in a basket, and set them down at the door of the duck house without coming in. With them came a sack of sunflower seed that had a painted finch on the front. Sloe had watched her decide about the sack. She had stood at the edge of the long grass with it on her back, looking from the finch to the five of them, before she put it down and went.

That was yesterday. This morning Sloe had the eggs out on the boards in rows of five, which was how hir had laid out stores for fifty years: the gap at the end of a row told you what you were short. Three rows, and four over. Haw's tail lay across hir feet while hir counted, and hir didn't move it. Hir had weighed the seed by lifting it, and called it a little over a kilo.

Hir ears came forward, and hir asked the Core what the eggs and the seed came to, as food.

*Seven thousand two hundred, approximately,* the Core said.

Hir asked what five of them burned in a day, on this ground.

*Ten thousand, approximately.*

So it was most of one day, and nothing at all for the day after.

*[Is it all eggs? Are they all eggs? Can I have the cracked one?]* Haw had hir head in through the door, which was the only part of hir that fitted. The tail on Sloe's feet belonged to the rest of hir, outside.

*[Nobody has anything till I've counted,]* Sloe sent.

*[You've counted. I felt you count. You did it twice]*

*[Three times ~ not sorry at all]*

The duck house had been built for ducks. The five of them had slept in it the way cargo goes into a hold that is one pallet too small: Yarrow's hindquarters out of the door, Madder wedged in the corner, and the rest of them fitted between. It smelled of old feathers, damp straw and something sweet that the Core could not name.

Hir gave out three eggs each and a handful of seed each. The spare eggs and the rest of the seed hir kept back for the evening. They ate the eggs whole, shells and all. Haw ate hirs in one go and then looked at the spare ones for so long that Sloe moved them behind hir tail.

Through the frame the hunger came in from the others, a dull pull under the ribs, and went round and back to hir so that hir could not have said how much of it was hir own. Under that, in Teasel, there was something else. It was low and steady, like a pump running in a compartment nobody used. Sloe put a hand flat on Teasel's back and went looking for what it was attached to, and found nothing.

*[I'm fine,]* Teasel sent, clean. *[Is anyone else hungry?]*

*[Everybody is,]* Sloe sent. *[You've eaten yours. I watched you]*

*[I know. I only wondered]*

Hir left the hand where it was. Teasel leaned back into it a little, and did not seem to know hir had.

Madder was sitting in the doorway with hir back to them, watching the forest edge.

The weather team was still at it. From the duck house door they were small shapes in the air over the far bank, going along the tree line in ones and twos. Each one pushed a cloud in front of it, low over the trees, as a rigger pushes a pallet down a corridor. Grey rain fell out of the clouds onto the smoke. They had been at it since two o'clock yesterday, when the whole team came in low over Fluttershy's yard with the clouds full and put them down on both banks, where the five of them had stood that morning.

*[That one went up backwards!]* Haw sent. *[Did you see? Backwards, with a cloud! Madder, can I go up the big tree and look? ~ already halfway to the tree in hir head]*

*[Seventeen,]* Sloe sent. *[Eighteen. One's behind the smoke]*

*[What's holding it?]* Yarrow sent.

Sloe's ears came forward. Hir asked the Core what one of them would weigh.

*Between ninety and one hundred and forty kilograms, approximately.*

Hir asked what the wings came to, both together, as far as it could see them.

*Point seven square metres, approximately.*

Hir asked what it would take to lift the lightest of them on that.

*Wing area insufficient by a factor of eight, approximately.*

*[Mine says eight,]* hir sent.

*[Mine says nine,]* from Madder. *[And the wing's the wrong shape to hover]*

*[Mine says the cloud,]* Haw sent, over both of them. *[I asked about the cloud. Forty metres across, forty kilos of water in it, and at that much rain it's empty in ten seconds. It's been raining for ages. Why isn't it empty? Sloe, why isn't it empty?]*

*[Eleven minutes,]* Sloe sent. *[That one. I've been counting ~ couldn't not]*

*[Pushed,]* Yarrow sent. *[Not towed]*

Through the frame Haw's delight came in so bright that Sloe's own tail lifted with it. Under that, from Yarrow, there was a narrow working attention, the kind hir had felt for fifty years coming off a rigger on a bad line. Madder's was flat and wide, and pointed at the edge.

*[The blue one came down in the yard yesterday like it was nothing,]* Haw sent. *[Straight down, and walked. It didn't even flap much at the bottom. I want to go up. I want one of them to take me up]*

*[Their wings are wet,]* Teasel sent.

Teasel didn't send anything after that. Sloe asked the Core what water would add to a wing that size, and then wished hir hadn't.

*[Unconfirmed,]* Madder sent. *[Nobody goes near the edge while they work. Not the bank, not the stream. Haw, that includes the tree]*

*[None of it closes,]* Haw sent, and it came through clean, with nothing under it at all, which Haw could never do. *[But what brought us here didn't close either]*

Nobody answered that. Out over the trees another cloud went along, low and grey, while the rain came out of it onto the smoke.


*[Sloe. Food status, by dusk,]* hir sent. Then, after a moment: *[And a roof. This one's a duck's. I have been in charge of a duck house for fourteen hours, and I would like to hand it back]*

*[Logged ~ laughing]*

Madder's ears moved very slightly.

---

*Three point four kilometres,* the Core said, when hir asked it at the end of Fluttershy's path how far it was to the town.

On the ship, that was a spine corridor and back. You pulled along the rail with one hand and held a bulb of tea in another. Here every step meant lifting sixty kilos off the ground. Then you put it down somewhere else, and lifted it again. Hir went on four with hir arms up, as Madder did, until the first bend took hir out of sight of the cottage. After that hir came down onto six and stayed there. The sun came up on the left, over a field of young cabbages in long rows. Hir ears went out wide and stiff in the heat, and stayed out. Twice hir sat down in the verge and let hir tail lie in the road behind hir.

*[How far are you? What can you see? Is it a town yet? Are there lots of them?]* Haw sent, from the meadow.

*[Grass,]* hir sent. *[More grass. A post with a number on it ~ tired, and hiding it badly]*

Four people felt hir sit down in the verge the second time, and nobody said so.

The town began with a fence and then a house, and then houses on both sides. They were round-shouldered and bright, with thatch on them like the grass on Fluttershy's roof, and window boxes full of red and yellow flowers that nobody seemed to be eating. There was a mare out front of the third house, sweeping her step. When she saw hir she stopped sweeping, went in with the broom and shut the door. Across the road a shutter came to. Further up, somepony put her head out of a door and said a name sharply, and a foal Sloe had not even seen went in past her legs.

Hir walked up the middle of the road on four, with hir arms up and hir ears as close to hir head as the sun would let them go.

Nopony said anything to hir. What hir got instead came through the walls. Hir heard: *—out of the fire, the girls said—*, and *—more legs than a—*, and *—teeth, all teeth, Scootaloo says—*, and a door slammed on the rest. The fear-smell came out of the houses, sour and sharp. Hir had smelled it two nights ago, off the small running things in the forest.

It went out of hir before hir could stop it. Hir heard hirself make a thin sound through the nose, and hir tail came round and coiled tight about hir own hind leg, so that hir walked the next few steps badly.

The frame answered all at once.

*[What's wrong? Sloe? What is it? Is it them? Is it a pony? Did one do something?]* That was Haw.

*[Sloe. Position and threat,]* Madder sent, over the top of it.

From Teasel there was nothing in words, only a quiet that had turned all the way towards hir.

*[Sloe,]* Yarrow sent. Just that.

*[Nothing! Just a street. They're shy ~ fine, fine,]* hir sent. Hir felt all of them feel exactly how fine it was.

Then, flat, with nothing under it: *[Six. If anybody's asking]*

Sloe put hir hand over hir own wrist, where the ribbon was, and held it there until the street opened out.

The town hall stood in the square, taller than the rest, with a clock in its gable. The clock said four minutes to eleven. Hir ears came forward.

*Ten fifty-two,* the Core said.

---

The Mayor's office had a desk with a blotter on it, a window, and more paper than Sloe had seen in one place in hir life. The Mayor sat behind the desk with both her forehooves flat on the blotter. She was a tidy mare with her mane done up high and a pair of spectacles on a cord, and she smelled of the same sour thing as the street, held down under something like soap.

She did not offer to shake. Sloe, who had seen two ponies do it on the steps outside, did not either. Hir tail had found hir leg again under the chair, and hir let it stay.

"Mayor," hir said. "I'm Sloe. There are five of us, everypony at Fluttershy's, in a building for ducks. We'll need somewhere to live and something to eat, and I don't have any money. I'd like to talk about the second one first, if that's all right. The first one can wait a day."

The Mayor took a moment over that, as ponies did with everything hir said.

"I have had," she said, "a great many visitors this morning. The weather team captain has been in twice about overtime. I have had three families about their children." She squared a pile of letters against the edge of the desk. "I did not expect to have you."

"No," Sloe said. "Well."

The Mayor looked at hir for a while over the spectacles. Then she took a sheet of paper off the nearest stack and turned it round.

There was an old seed barn on the river road, she said. It had been empty since the co-op moved to the new building by the mill. It had a roof and walls, and she could not promise more than that. It was, she said, a long way from anything. For food there was a town chit. She wrote it out while she talked: *reasonable costs, food only, bearer to present to any trader in the town, not to exceed twenty-five bits in any one week, to be redeemed at quarter-day.* She signed it and blotted it, and slid it across without her hoof leaving the paper until the last moment.

Sloe read it twice.

*[What's a bit?]* *[Sloe, what's quarter-day?]* *[Can you eat paper? Is it that kind?]* *[Unconfirmed]* from Madder, and Haw again, *[Can you though?]*, all in the same second, and hir let it go by.

"When's quarter-day?" hir said.

"Midsummer."

Hir asked. *Nine weeks and four days,* the Core said.

So anyone who took it would be lending the town the money till midsummer.

"Thank you," hir said, and meant it.

The Mayor looked as if she didn't know what to do with that. She rang a small bell, and a stallion came in from the outer office.

"Would you show it out, please," she said to him. "And find it the key for the seed barn."

Crossing the square, hir looked up at the clock in the gable, and sent nothing about it.

On the way out of town, with the key in hir hand, hir caught up with the egg cart. The stallion driving it said he did the bakery first, at five, and he said it while he was already shaking the reins.

---

*[Roof,]* hir sent, from the bottom of the town, in the afternoon. *[A barn, on the river road. Four kilometres out of town and three from the meadow, so everybody walks. I've got the key. And paper that says the town will pay for food, in nine weeks ~ pleased with hirself for as long as that took, then less]*

*[A barn! How high? Can we climb it?]* Haw sent.

*[Moving at fifteen hundred,]* Madder sent. *[Yarrow, rear. Haw, you are not climbing anything until I've seen it]*

A little later Madder sent again: *[Told Fluttershy at her door, aloud, so the four o'clock doesn't go to an empty duck house ~ she said "oh", twice]*

The river road ran along the bottom of the fields. Across the water, the forest edge still had smoke along it, and the weather team's clouds lay low on the trees, grey on grey, the whole length of it. Hir asked. *Smoke at two point one kilometres. No flame visible.* Hir sat down in the verge more times than hir sent about.

*[It's locked,]* Haw sent, from somewhere ahead of hir. *[Sloe, it's locked. You've got the key. Where are you?]*

*[Door,]* Yarrow sent. It was all hir sent, and it was not a question.

Sloe came in last, at dusk. The barn was long and grey, and stood alone in a field of old stubble with the river going by at the bottom. The big door hung square in its frame. The hinge pins were scraped bright where the rust had been knocked off, and there were fresh splinters on the sill, and two new holes beside two old ones where the straps had been moved up. Hir stood there with the key.

*[Yarrow took it off!]* Haw sent. *[The whole door! Off, and we came in, and then hir put it back on, better. Hir put it back better, Sloe]*

Yarrow sent nothing. From the back of the barn hir tail lifted once and went down.

Inside, the air was cool and smelled of chaff and mice. The roof went up into the dark, where things moved along the beams and made small sounds.

*[Swallows!]* Haw sent. *[I asked. The Core says swallows. There are nine. There were eleven, two went out. It's eleven metres to the ridge. I haven't climbed it. Madder, I haven't. Tell hir I haven't]*

*[Hir hasn't ~ as if it had cost hir a great deal,]* Madder sent.

The others were at the back of the barn, in the straw, already a heap. Sloe did not send anything. Hir went across the floor on six and got in. Haw's arm came over hir and Teasel's tail came round hir leg, and somebody's hind hand found hir hip and stayed there. Hir had not been touched since the duck house door, before the sun was up.

Hir lay there doing the chit. Twenty-five bits a week. Nine weeks. Hir did it again, and again, until Madder's hand came down flat on hir back without Madder waking, and hir stopped.

---

A little after three, in the dark, Sloe got out of the heap. Hir untied the ribbon from hir own wrist with a thumb and hir teeth, and tied it round Teasel's, and Teasel did not wake.

*[Teasel. Keep that on for me today,]* hir sent, and went.

Hir was at the back of the bakery at a quarter to five, in an alley that smelled so strongly of bread that hir stomach hurt. It was dark, and it was cold, the first cold of hir life. Hir had walked the road without trouble, since the dark was only geometry, but on the way hir ears had folded flat and would not come up, while hir hands went numb and stayed numb.

*[Sloe. Position,]* Madder sent, from the heap.

*[Four forty-six. Bakery. Cold]*

The back door was propped open with a sack. Heat and light came out of it with a noise of metal. Then a pony came out backwards, bumping the door wider with her rump, with a sieve in her mouth. She was pink under the flour. The flour went up to her elbows and over one ear. She banged the sieve twice on the step, turned round, and saw hir.

She stopped. Then she took the sieve out of her mouth.

"You're in the way of the flour," she said.

Sloe moved. The pony banged the sieve twice more where hir had been, and a cloud went up and settled on both of them.

"Sorry," Sloe said. "I'm Sloe. I'm here about the eggs."

"Pinkie." She looked hir up and down, all of hir, the way a stallholder looks at a load before she prices it. "The eggs aren't here yet. The eggs aren't anybody's except Mrs. Cake's, anyway. She has a contract. It's a whole thing."

"Who's the farmer?"

"Clover Patch. He comes Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays, and he won't sell at the door. Contract. Don't ask him. He'll just go red." She pushed the sack back against the door with a hind hoof. The doorpost had a hoofprint in flour on it, at the height of her shoulder, and under the paint, if hir looked, there was another one just like it. "The ponies with too many hens are down by the fountain. Get there early. Everybody's got too many eggs in spring. Nobody knows what to do with them."

"I know what to do with them," Sloe said.

Pinkie looked at hir again. Her eyes went to Sloe's flank, where the fur lay closer over the ribs than it had on the ship, and stayed there a moment. She didn't say anything about it.

"Hey," she said instead. "Have you guys even had a—"

She looked back through the door. There was a slate on the wall inside with chalk on it: *2 x wedding (Sat). School, 90 buns. Filly Delight, sponge, NO nuts.* The word *Saturday* was underlined twice.

"Nope," Pinkie said. "Never mind. Door."

She went in. The door swung to on the sack. Sloe stood in the alley a little longer in the bread smell, with flour on hir ears, and then went to find the fountain.

---

The market was a square of stalls round the fountain, with doors behind it into the streets. Hir did it the way hir had always done a market: once through without buying, to learn where the prices sat, and then back.

Eggs were everywhere, as Pinkie had said. A mare at a blue door took the chit for a dozen at three bits and seemed glad to see them go. Two doors on, a grey mare wouldn't take the chit at all. A colt at the fountain would have, and hir already had the eggs, so hir marked him for tomorrow. Cheese hir found once, at the dairy cart. The dairy mare looked at the chit for a long time before she cut hir a wedge for a bit, and said it was no skin off her hooves either way. Every pony hir bought from took a long look at hir first, then a longer one at the paper, and nopony touched hir hand.

A stallion at the end of the second row sold only radishes. He had them in heaps, red and white, very clean. Hir couldn't eat a radish and told him so, and he didn't seem to mind. He told hir about his knee instead. He'd done it on the ice in the winter of the big freeze and it had never been right since. It was worse in the damp, so it told him when rain was coming better than the weather team did, no offence to them. Sloe asked him how long before rain. He considered it and said Thursday. Hir liked him.

By eight hir had used four bits of the chit, and hir arms were full.

*[A dozen eggs, and a wedge of cheese. The cheese is the best thing I've seen on this planet,]* hir sent. Then: *[And rain on Thursday. A stallion's knee says so]*

*[Rain?]* Yarrow sent.

*[Is it cold? Does it hurt? Is it like standing under the hoses? Do we go in? Do we stay out in it? Sloe, do we go in?]* That was Haw, and under it hir could feel hir wanting to be out in it.

*[Unconfirmed,]* Madder sent, and nobody had anything better.

*[Is everypony fed?]* Sloe sent. *[I mean us]*

*[Bring the cheese!]* Haw sent. *[Is it far? Can I come?]*

*[Is the cheese for everyone?]* Teasel sent. *[I'm fine. I had mine]*

Under the jokes the frame held the hunger in all of them, and the low, steady thing in Teasel, which had not moved since the morning before. Sloe went looking for its cause again, and again there was nothing to find.

The general store would not take the chit at all. The storekeeper was a brown mare with a pencil behind each ear, and she had a list on the counter headed STOCK, with nothing written under it. She said so frankly: the town had never paid her on time in her life, and she wasn't starting with strangers.

Sloe looked at the list, then at the shelves.

"I can count that," hir said. "Your stock. All of it, today, written up. For bits, in the hand, at closing."

The storekeeper looked at hir for a while. "How much?"

Hir had been walking since three. Hir asked the Core what ten bits would buy at the blue door, and it told hir, and that looked like a lot.

"Ten," hir said.

"Done," said the storekeeper, very fast.

Hir counted until the store closed at six. It was not quick. Hir had to be shown what half the things were, and the Core had never heard of the other half. At two hir was up the ladder, with a tin of something called saddle soap in each hand. Below hir, the storekeeper said to nopony, "I gave that Dawdle colt thirty last spring, and he missed a whole crate of nails." Hir took the thought to the end, and then put the tins back.

Thirty. Hir laughed, up the ladder, a short chirr through the nose, and the storekeeper looked up.

"Nothing," Sloe said. "Tins."

---

At dusk the last stall still packing up was at the far corner. It had sacks of dried beans, a crate of walnuts and another of hazelnuts from last autumn's store, eggs in straw, and a cart behind it with a yoke and no pony in it yet. The mare behind the stall wore a hat. She was folding a cloth over the eggs when hir came up, and she didn't stop folding it.

"We're shutting," she said.

"I've got cash," Sloe said.

The mare stopped folding. She looked at hir, at the paper in hir hand and at the ten bits in the other. It was a flat look with nothing in it, and Sloe liked it at once.

"Applejack," she said. "What do you want?"

"Sloe. Eggs, three dozen. Walnuts, as many as this buys. Beans."

Applejack weighed the walnuts on a pan scale hung from the stall's crossbar. When the needle went past the line she took one nut off, then a second, until it sat on the line and stayed there. She tipped them into a sack and wrote the weight in a ledger that lay open on the board. The pencil was a stub tied to the corner post with a length of twine, and the twine was too short for her to reach the far column. She wrote the last figure with the ledger tipped up on its edge to meet it.

"Walnuts is five the pound," she said. "Eggs is four the dozen. Beans is two. You want to see me weigh the beans?"

"If it's no skin off your hooves," Sloe said.

Applejack gave hir a look for that, and weighed the beans.

It came to thirty-one, and a bit for the sack. The chit said food only, and a sack wasn't food. Sloe put down seven of the ten bits. "I'm keeping three back for the morning," hir said, and held the chit out. Applejack read it, without taking it, all the way to the bottom.

"Quarter-day," she said.

"Midsummer. Nine weeks."

"Town paid the last one of these in August." She took it anyway. She wrote *21 on chit* in the ledger, stood a moment looking at the paper, and put it under the cash box rather than in it. Then she counted the seven bits. She counted them again. She put them in the box, shut it, and had it under the board before Sloe had taken hir hand back. Sloe noticed that.

"That leaves you four short," Applejack said. "Call it three and the sack."

"Put me in the book," Sloe said. "I'll be back tomorrow."

"I don't doubt it." Applejack tipped the ledger up on its edge again to reach the column. "Name?"

"Sloe."

She wrote. Sloe read it upside down: *the strange one (Sloe). Seed barn. It says the town's good for it.*

Hir hand went to hir wrist. It was bare.

*[Walnuts ~ very pleased, and very, very tired,]* hir sent, on the river road, with the sack on hir back and the eggs in both arms. *[Three of their pounds, and beans, and three dozen eggs. Ask me what it comes to. Somebody ask me]*

*[What does it come to?]* they all sent at once, Yarrow included, which Yarrow never did.

Hir asked the Core anyway. *Fourteen thousand six hundred, approximately.*

*[A day and a half,]* hir sent. *[That's one stall, tonight, at closing. A day and a half off one stall. It can work]*

---

On the fourth morning, the morning after the market, the mare at the blue door wanted five bits for a dozen, and she did not look glad.

The grey mare who had refused the chit said six. The colt at the fountain said he'd sold all his to a pony from the other side of town who'd heard eggs were going, and didn't have any. The radish stallion's radishes were the same price as the day before, since nopony wanted radishes any more than they had yesterday. His knee was a little better. He said he'd put Thursday back to Friday.

Sloe walked the rows once, and the prices were all in the wrong places. Hir reached for why, and it came back exact, in the voice of the quartermaster who had trained hir in hir first year on the stores deck: *Small market, never your own face, never all of one thing in a day.* Hir had known it for eighty years. It had not come to hir once until now.

Applejack's eggs were six the dozen, and her walnuts were six the pound.

"You put it up," Sloe said.

"I did." Applejack didn't look away. "You cleaned out every egg on this row yesterday, and the whole town's heard. Mae's asking five, Granite's asking six. I'm not going under the row for anypony."

"The chit's gone," Sloe said. "It's twenty-five a week. You've got twenty-one of it and the rest went at the doors."

"Then it's cash."

Hir had three bits.

"Half a dozen," hir said.

Applejack counted six eggs out of the straw, one at a time, and put them in hir hands. She took hir bits and counted them, twice. She wrote in the ledger, with it tipped up on its edge.

"That's you square for today," she said. "Still four from yesterday. I'd want that before there's any more in the book."

"Yes," Sloe said, and stood a moment longer with the eggs, and went.

Hir asked the Core on the road home, with the six eggs held against hir chest, what the week came to, at today's prices, with what hir had left. It told hir. Hir asked again, a different way, and it gave hir the same answer.

*[Radish stallion's put Thursday back to Friday,]* hir sent. *[His knee, no offence to it]*

## 5. Liaison

Spike was halfway through the business of Mrs. Hay Bale's brooch when he held out the letter, and he didn't stop talking while he did it.

"—so I told her it's not a garnet, it's glass, and she said her mother paid four bits for it at the Cloudsdale fair, so I said it's very nice glass. That came up at six. It's got the seal on. I didn't charge her in the end. For the brooch, I mean. You can't charge somepony for glass."

"No," Twilight said. She had the letter open before he'd finished. It was short, as the Princess's letters were when she was busy, and most of it was about the weather in Canterlot. One line in the middle was not.

*The Mayor tells me you have visitors, and that she is at her wits' end; would you look after them for me?*

She read it twice. Then she put it down on the desk beside the shoebox, and her eye went to the shoebox without being asked.

It had been a boot box once, for a pair of Rarity's winter boots, and now it held the index, which was to have one card for every manuscript in the royal library's catalogue of pre-Nightmare holdings, and there were about three thousand manuscripts. The cards went in the Princess's own preferred order, which was not alphabetical and took a page of notes to explain. A length of string held the lid on. When it frayed through in the second week of March, Twilight had knotted it, and the knot sat on top of the lid like a small hard bead. Inside there were four hundred and twelve cards. She had counted them on Sunday, twice.

Spike thought it was nearly finished. She had told him so in February.

"Is it the index?" he said. "Does she want it early?"

"No. It's the visitors." She took a fresh sheet and wrote *Visitors* at the top of it, and under that, in a column: *Food. Water. Warm enough at night? Anyone ill.* She looked at it. Under that she wrote *Index, two hours after*, and underlined it. Then she turned her notebook over and opened it from the back, and wrote *Questions (not to ask)* across the top of the last page. Under that she wrote *Where are they from?* The quill was already on the next line. She lifted it off, shut the notebook, and went downstairs and made a cup of tea, which she left on the windowsill when Spike called up to ask where the good polishing cloth had gone.

---

The river road went out of town past the mill, and after the mill there was nothing on it but fields.

She heard the market bell go for eight behind her as she passed the last fence. It was a flat, cracked bell on a post by the fountain, and she had stopped wincing at it some time in her second month. Across the river the Everfree stood up dark, with a brownish haze still hanging in front of it, thin as weak tea. The weather team had set a line of clouds low over the treetops to keep them damp. Somepony had laid them out ruler-straight, which she approved of. One pegasus was out, very small, nudging a cloud back into line. Twilight watched it until the road bent.

She ate half her sandwich as she walked. It was cress and hay on brown bread. She had made it at six, and it had gone a little soft.

The seed barn stood by itself in a stubble field, with a willow at the far end where the field met the water. She had passed it a hundred times without looking at it. Now there was somepony on the ridge of the roof, or something, very high up, and as she came off the road into the stubble she saw that it was hanging off the ridge by its back feet, head down, looking at the river.

Then it saw her, and it went still.

So did the others. She had not seen them until they stopped moving: one at the big door with its hand on the doorpost, one sitting in the sun against the wall, two at the corner of the barn by a heap of walnut shells. Every head came round to her together. It was so quick and so level that she stopped in the stubble with one hoof up.

"Hello," she said. "I'm Twilight Sparkle. From the library. The Princess asked me to—" She had rehearsed this on the road and it had gone. "To see how you're getting on."

The one by the wall got up. Its ears stood straight out to either side, wide and stiff, and Twilight's heart sank a little. On a pony that meant somepony was put out, and she hadn't said anything yet. It walked toward her on its back four legs with its front hands held up off the ground. It was thinner than she'd expected from the stories, and dustier, and its eyes were tired.

"Twilight," it said. There was a pause before it, but not much of one. "I'm Sloe. Come in, come in. We don't bite the hoof that feeds us." It seemed pleased with that. "Hir on the roof is Haw, the big one at the door is Yarrow, that's Madder, and Teasel. Haw, come down."

Teasel was the smaller of the two at the corner. It ducked its head when its name was said.

"I'm fine up here!" called the one on the roof. Its voice was very loud and delighted.

"Come down anyway."

It came down. It came down the corner of the barn face first, hand over hand over hand, dropped the last six feet or so onto the stubble in a heap, and got up laughing. Then it walked straight up to Twilight and looked at her from very close. Before she could step back it had put out a hand and touched her mane, once, lightly.

Twilight jerked her head away. She took a step back into the stubble without meaning to.

"It's a different colour at the ends," it said. "Why is it a different colour at the ends?"

Haw's hand came back to its chest. Nobody had said anything. It came back all at once, the way a pony's hoof does when somepony has told her to put that down. Then Madder said "Haw," from the corner, not loudly. It came a beat late. Madder was looking at Twilight while it said it.

Twilight didn't know what to make of the order of that. Haw didn't look sorry. It was still looking at her mane.

---

She asked her questions in order.

Haw had sat down against Sloe's side and was leaning on it with its whole weight. Sloe didn't seem to notice. Teasel sat hunched at the edge of the others with its tail pulled in close underneath it. Madder had one hind hand resting on its back, flat, the way you'd rest a hoof on a stack of books to keep it from sliding.

Food first. Sloe answered it, and did it well, without being asked twice: eggs, walnuts, beans at a push, anything that had been an animal if they could get it. Not greens. They had tried greens on the second day, from Fluttershy's garden.

"It went badly," Sloe said. "Very. Haw was— no. Ask Haw. Don't ask Haw."

"How much, in a day?"

Sloe's ears came forward, both of them, slowly, and stayed there a moment. "At yesterday's prices, for all five of us, about forty-five bits a day. That's if we can get walnuts for half of it. Walnuts are cheap and they keep. If it's all eggs it's seventy. Seventy-two if the egg mare's in a mood, and she's been in a mood. And there aren't enough walnuts. I think we've had all of Ponyville's, apart from the ones in cakes."

Twilight did the arithmetic before she could stop herself. The chit was twenty-five a week. She said nothing about it, and neither did Sloe, and she saw that Sloe had seen her do it.

Water was the river. Before she could move on, Madder asked her how far upstream the mill was. Then it asked whether the mill put anything into the river. When she said she didn't think so, it asked who would know. She told it the miller. It asked the miller's name.

"Does the wheel go all the way r—" Haw began, and stopped dead with its mouth open. Nobody had said a word to it. It shut its mouth.

Madder asked what hour the mill started. Somewhere around there Twilight realised she was being interviewed back. She didn't mind. It was like talking to the senior archivists in Canterlot, who took notes while you talked and asked where you'd got it.

Cold at night: "Yes," Sloe said. "It's very cold. We've got each other. It's fine." Then, as if that had been too short: "It's the first cold any of us have had. The ears are the worst. They go—" It made a flat shape with both hands against its own head, and let the rest go.

Anyone ill: there was the pause, a proper one this time. "No," Madder said.

Teasel had come a little closer while they talked, and Madder's hand had come with it. It was watching the sandwich. Twilight had taken it out of her saddlebag again without thinking, and was holding the second half in her mouth by the crust. Teasel watched it go up to her mouth and come down. It watched her chew. When she looked straight at it, it looked away at the river.

She took the sandwich out of her mouth. "Would you like—"

"We can't," Sloe said, gently. "Thank you. It's the hay. Teasel knows."

"I'm fine," Teasel said. Its voice was quiet and careful. "Thank you."

Its ribs showed. The others were lean, but on Teasel she could have counted them, down one side, where the fur lay close. She put the sandwich away. She lifted her notebook and her quill out of the saddlebag with her magic, the way she had done every morning of her life, and let them hang in the air in front of her while she found the page. She wanted the ribs written down before she forgot them, along with the forty-five bits and the miller.

All five of them went still.

Their ears went flat against their heads, all ten of them, the way hers went in a gale, as if something were blowing straight at them. Then, slowly, the ears went out wide to either side and stayed there, stiff. The big one at the door took its hand off the doorpost and stepped back, one step, off the sill and into the shadow. Teasel made a sound, high and thin, through its nose. Madder huffed once, short and hard. Haw had been leaning on Sloe; its tail came round Sloe's hind leg and wound there, twice, tight. Twilight could hear the river, and a swallow somewhere inside the barn.

Nobody said anything. The notebook turned a page by itself in the air. She had asked it to before she'd noticed. All five pairs of eyes went to the page.

*Oh*, she thought. *Of course.*

The story in the market that week had a horn in it now. She had heard it that morning from two different ponies, neither of whom had been there: green fire, a light from a horn, a crack like a tree coming down. Perhaps whatever had happened to them in the forest had looked like this. Or perhaps they had never seen magic at all.

It hurt anyway, small and sharp under her ribs. Foals fell asleep on her at story hour.

She let the quill down into her saddlebag, and the notebook after it, slowly, so as not to make it worse. Then she dug one-hoofed in the bottom of the bag until she found the stub of pencil that lived there, and put it in her mouth, and took the notebook out with her hoof and balanced it open on her foreleg.

"Forty-five," she said round the pencil. "Bits. A day. And the miller."

The ears came in slowly, one pair at a time. Sloe's came first. Yarrow's came last, back in the shadow of the door. In between, the five of them looked at each other, one to the next and not at her, as if something were being passed along. Then Madder said, "Yes. Go on." The big one came back onto the sill and put its hand on the doorpost again, where it had been.

Haw's tail stayed where it was. Haw leaned toward her saddlebag as far as the tail would let it, with one hand out. "Can it do that with anything? Could it do a—"

Sloe put a hand flat on Haw's chest without looking round. Haw stopped.

She wrote the rest of the visit like that. Pencil had never been her best, and pencil held in the teeth was worse, so that her sixes came out like her zeros and the page got damp at one corner. She didn't mention it. None of them did either. Once, while all their eyes were on Madder, she turned to the back and wrote *the pause before they answer*, and under it *five heads at once*, and turned back before anybody looked round.

She asked the names again, for the form she didn't have yet. Madder gave its own as *Madder-four*, very clearly, and waited for her to write the four. She wrote it. The next word was already in her mouth behind the pencil, and it was *why*. She took the pencil out.

"With a hyphen?"

"Yes," Madder said.

*Look after*, the Princess had written. Not *look into*. Nobody else gave a number. She wrote down what she was given.

Sloe walked her back across the stubble to the road. At the edge of the field it stopped, and she stopped, and it looked back at the barn, where the others were watching them go.

"Twilight," it said. "The pen."

"I'm sorry about the pen."

"No. It was—" A pause, longer than any of the others. "It was like weather. It was like weather, up close. It's—" Its ears went forward and stayed forward a while. They came back with nothing. "No. Like weather."

She didn't understand it. She wrote it down anyway, at the bottom of the damp page, *like weather*, with the pencil. Sloe watched her do it and said nothing. Then it went back across the field to the others.

On the road, out of sight of the barn, she turned to the back and added *the four*, then copied *like weather* under it, then *the ears*. By the mill she had added *Teasel's ribs*, which was not a question.

"Not to ask," she said round the pencil, to nopony, and put the book away.

---

The Mayor already knew.

"Forty-five," she said, when Twilight told her, and took her spectacles off and rubbed the bridge of her nose. "I had it at thirty. I'd hoped thirty." She had a ledger open on the blotter with a column in it headed *Visitors*. The column had four entries and a great deal of empty space. "Twilight, the chit is twenty-five because the poor fund is a hundred and ten bits for the whole spring. For the whole town. There are two widows on it and the Dawdle family and the Pinwheel colt who broke his leg."

"I'll write to Canterlot."

"Please do."

She wrote that afternoon, a proper letter, to the Royal Treasury rather than to the Princess. She set the figures out in a table, with a note on the prices, and sent it by Spike. Then she did six index cards before supper, which made four hundred and eighteen.

---

After supper she meant to do twenty. She did one, and then went for *Hoofwright's Bestiary*, as a break between cards.

Hoofwright was a book for fillies, and had nothing. After it she took down the older bestiaries from the top shelf, then the travellers' accounts, then the zoology shelf. She was looking for six limbs, for hands where the hooves should be, and for anything that paused before it answered. Some time after midnight she unrolled the old atlas of the lands past the Everfree and went along its edges, where the cartographer had drawn mountains and then stopped drawing.

There was nothing. There was not a footnote. The travellers had been everywhere and eaten everything, and not one of them had seen a creature with hands on its feet.

She found that her forehooves were pressed flat on the atlas, holding it down. She lifted them. The edge curled a little, and she put them back.

She opened her notebook from the back and read what she had.

*Where are they from?*  
*the pause before they answer*  
*five heads at once*  
*the four*  
*like weather*  
*the ears*  
*Teasel's ribs*

The clock on the landing struck three. The count stood at four hundred and nineteen.

In the morning Spike put the books back an armful at a time, with a small noise in his throat at every shelf.

"Leave the ones on the desk."

He looked at the desk, and left them. "They're library books," he told the shelf. "They live on shelves."

---

The miller came into the library on the sixth morning with his hat on.

It was a flat grey felt hat with a band that had once been green, and he kept it on the whole time he stood at her desk. Twice, when he got to the part he minded most, he took it off and hit his own leg with it and then put it back on.

"On the roof," he said. "At dawn. I come round the corner with the barrow and there it is, sat up on the ridge over the wheel, looking down at the race like it's thinking of going in. Two and a half mile it's come, Miss Sparkle. From that barn, in the pitch dark, all along the river road, to sit on my roof. There's roofs out there. And my shingles are older than you are. It goes through them, it goes in the race, and then what? Then I've got a—" He took the hat off. He hit his leg with it. "—one of those, in my wheel."

"I'll speak to them."

"I'd be obliged."

She walked out that afternoon and spoke to them. Madder listened, and said "Understood," once. While Madder said it she could see Haw on the barn roof behind it, lying flat along the ridge with her chin on her hands, listening too. When Madder turned round, Haw came down. Twilight didn't know whether that was the end of it.

On the way back she passed a mare she knew from the Tuesday reading group. The mare was walking her colt the long way round by the top field to the school. The long way was a mile further. Her ears went back a little when she saw Twilight coming from the barn.

"I'm sure they're very nice," the mare said, and kept walking.

The colt looked back over his shoulder at the barn roof all the way up the field. Twilight stood in the road until they were over the top.

---

She got home a little after four. Spike had chalked his slate again. It hung on a nail by the library door where customers could see it, and it said, in his best capitals: *POLISHING — rubys, sapphires, anything shiny. NO opals (to fiddly).* Underneath, smaller, there was a price list. Under that he had rubbed something out and written it again.

He was at the back table with a sapphire the size of a hazelnut and a square of chamois. He was working at the same facet, with the same small circular movement, that he had been using when she went out.

"Is that Rarity's?" she said.

"Mm. Thirty of those. For the big Canterlot thing." He held it up to the lamp and looked through it, and put it down and went on. "She says she'll settle up when Canterlot settles up with her. She said that last week too."

"That's good," Twilight said. She was looking for the Treasury's reply in the post, which had not come. "That's a big order for you."

"Yeah," Spike said. He went on with the facet.

She took a cup of tea upstairs and left it on the stairs when she stopped to find a pencil.

---

The reply from Canterlot came on the seventh day. It was a printed form with a covering note. The note said that the matter was *being considered*, and that the enclosed form should be completed in full and returned to expedite it. The form wanted the number of persons, the daily requirement per person in bits, and the period for which relief was sought. In a box at the bottom marked *Names of persons (with numbers)*, it wanted their names.

She did the rest of it at the kitchen table after supper and stopped at the box.

*With numbers.* It might mean how many there were. It might mean an age, or a registry number, which was what Canterlot usually meant. It might mean Madder-four. She had Madder-four, and she had four other names with nothing after them. She did not know whether the others had numbers they hadn't given her. She did not know whether the four was the kind of thing you didn't ask about, or whether putting it on a Treasury form would be the wrong thing to have done with it.

It was nearly eleven. The index was upstairs. She had done no cards that day.

The form was a perfectly good reason to walk out to the barn, and she let it be the reason. She put the form in her saddlebag, and her scarf round her neck, and went out.

---

It was two and a half miles, and she had never walked the river road at night. There was a moon, and the fields were grey and silver under it. The smoke over the forest edge had thinned to almost nothing. Somewhere across the water an owl was working the tree line. She thought about the form, and then, since it was dark and nopony could see her do it, about the chapter in *The Winter Orchard* where the sisters find the letters in the wall, which she had been meaning to reread since Christmas. Then she thought about the form again.

The barn was dark. The big door stood open a crack, a hand's width, as if it had swung a little on its own after somepony pulled it to. She stood outside it and knocked on the doorpost, very softly. Nothing answered.

She lit her horn, low, and put her head round the door.

They were in the far corner, in a hollow they had trodden into the old straw. At first she couldn't tell where one of them stopped. They lay in one heap, curled in toward the middle with their foreheads together, almost touching. Their tails had come round the outside and been wound through each other, over and under. Sloe was on the near side. Twilight could see her face. Her eyes were shut, and her mouth was a little open.

None of them was moving.

She waited for the sides to rise. She was looking for the slow lift of a barrel, the way Spike's went in his basket, and the longer fall. She looked at Sloe's flank. She looked at Haw's, where it showed over the top of the heap. She looked at the big one's back, which was the broadest thing there and should have been the easiest to see. Nothing lifted. Nothing fell. The straw under them did not stir.

She counted them. Five. She counted again, as though a sixth might be breathing.

The light from her horn lay across them, very steady. Up in the dark the swallows shifted on the beam and were quiet. She thought, quite clearly: *the doctor on Mulberry Lane*. It was two and a half miles back to his door. Then it was two and a half miles out again, with him, and he was sixty and did not like being woken. After that there would be a letter. She began it in her head, and found she could not get past the part where she said how many.

Five, in her town. All at once.

She went in two steps and stopped. She didn't know what she would do when she got to them. Her horn was still lit.

The heap breathed.

All five of them breathed in at once, one long slow breath, so that the whole heap swelled and the tails drew in tight round each other. It went on longer than any breath she had ever taken. Then it went out of them, all together, just as slowly, and they were as still as before, and nothing rose or fell.

Twilight stood where she was.

She counted, without meaning to, the way she counted anything. She had got to ninety-one when the next one came, all five at once again, as long and as slow as the first. Some part of her had been taking notes the whole time: the foreheads together, the tails wound over and under, and now ninety-one. Her face went hot, and she hated that part of her a little. She did not wait for a third. She put her light out. In the dark she could hear the river and the swallows, and nothing else.

She backed out of the door. She pulled it to, carefully, until it was open about as far as she had found it. Then she walked back along the river road to the town. She passed the mill, where a lamp was on in the house, and the fountain, where the bell hung dark on its post. She let herself into the library, and did not go to Mulberry Lane.

---

Spike was asleep in his basket at the foot of the stairs, curled round the chamois, with the sapphire in his fist.

The index was open on the desk where she had left it that morning. The card on top was half written: *Herbal (partial). Forty-one leaves, vellum, hoof unknown; binding later, calf over boards; three leaves*, and then nothing, where she had stopped to go down for the post. The string lay beside the box with its knot turned up. The books were still piled at the other end of the desk, open at the pages she had stopped on, and one of the bestiaries had slid off the pile onto the card. There was a cup of tea by her elbow, with a skin on it.

## 6. Rain

A little after one in the morning the big door began to knock against its stop, a small dull knock every few seconds, so Yarrow got out of the heap to see to it.

The straps were where hir had moved them, and the pins were tight. It was only the wind working the door, and a wedge would cure it. While hir was feeling along the sill for where the wedge should go, the sound on the roof changed. For an hour it had been a dry scratching, which the Core had called leaves. Now it was a close, fine hiss down the whole length of the barn, as if every shingle had been set working at once.

Hir opened the door a body's width, and went out.

Out in the stubble it came down on hir everywhere. A sound came out of hir that hir had not meant, a short high chirr at the back of the throat. Hir ears came forward, and hir asked the Core what was falling.

*Water.*

Hir asked the drop size, and how fast they came, and how cold.

*Two millimetres, approximately. Six metres per second. Nine degrees.*

For a few breaths the fur along hir back held it off, in beads that hir could feel sitting on the guard hairs. When the fur gave, the water reached the skin along hir spine and went down both flanks in thin cold lines. Hir ears folded flat without being asked. Hir tail took it last, from the root down, until hir felt the weight of it in the muscle over hir hips. Across the river the smell of the forest had changed. For ten days it had been smoke. Now the smoke was under something darker, wet ash and wet leaf and a third thing hir had no record of, coming over the water on a wind that did not stop.

The chirr had gone through the frame as it left hir, and the heap woke to it.

*[Yarrow. What's on you? Is it safe on the skin?]* Madder sent.

*[The forest's different. It smells like the hold after a wash-down,]* Teasel sent, clean, over the top of it.

*[Is that water? All of it, off the sky, just like that? ~ half asleep and delighted]* That was Sloe, and then Sloe again: *[Don't drink it till the Core's had a look. Yarrow, don't drink it]*

Yarrow sent the numbers, and nothing with them.

*[Yarrow. In,]* Madder sent.

Hir went in and lay down at the edge of the heap, where Haw's shoulder found hirs and jerked away.

*[You're wet! Why are you wet? Did you go in the river? Is it the river? Is it on the roof, is that what that is?]*

Yarrow sent nothing. Haw's shoulder came back, slowly, and stayed.

At two hir went out again, and Madder sent hir in. The third time hir stood in it until hir could not feel the pads of hir lower hands, and Madder's *[In]* had a rider on it, very small, that hir did not answer. The fourth time Madder sent nothing at all.

Hir could not strip what hir felt, so the others had it through the frame the whole night. It stayed high while hir was out and did not come down when hir lay in the heap, and every time hir went to the door it rose again before hir reached it. Haw could not leave it alone.

*[What's it like? Is it cold? Does it go in your ears? Madder, can I go and—]*

*[No,]* Madder sent.

*[Yarrow. Yarrow. What's it LIKE ~ wide awake now, and furious to be in here]*

*[Wet,]* Yarrow sent.

*[Fifty years on the outside of a hull, and hir says wet ~ laughing into the straw, trying not to wake Teasel]* Sloe sent.

Hir came in the last time with the light grey in the door, and lay down against Teasel's back, where hir lower hand went round onto Teasel's side as it always did in a heap. There were more ribs under it than there had been a week ago. Teasel was asleep, or doing it well, with the ribbon tight on hir wrist. After a while the hand stopped counting.

*[Radish stallion's knee,]* Sloe sent, to nobody. *[Five days late. I'll tell him]*

---

The crew's line ran along the foot of the trees on the far side of the river, from the mill down as far as the Pinwheel fields. Yarrow set out on four in the rain with hir upper hands carried. The road was mud now, though, and it took each lower hand to the second knuckle and let it go with a pull, so that by the mill bridge hir was on six.

At the end of the first week Twilight had asked, and the crew had taken hir on at six bits the day. After that Sloe had gone to see Applejack. Now half of it went straight to Applejack's stall, against the tab and next week's walnuts, before Yarrow ever saw it.

Ruddock was a heavy bay stallion with a coil of line over his withers. On the first morning he had looked at Yarrow for a long time.

"It drives stakes," he had said, to the captain, not to hir. "It hauls. It don't touch a knot."

So hir drove stakes.

They were ash, split out of the round and pointed with an axe, a metre long and as thick as hir wrist, stacked under a tarpaulin at the head of the line. There was a snail on the top one this morning, on the flat of the head, with its body out and its horns up in the rain. Yarrow lifted it off by the shell and set it on the next stake down, and took the first.

The maul was pony-made, with a short handle meant for a mouth, and hir choked it right up in both upper hands. Hir gave the first blow softly, to learn the ground. Yesterday the ground had been soft on top and firm under. Today the maul came down and the stake went in a hand's breadth at a stroke, easy, into something that closed round the wood like a mouth.

Since the clouds were up and the pull would come up, hir drove it plumb, so that the load would run along its length. The earth ponies set theirs with the heads leaning toward the trees, at a slant hir had watched for three days and taken for habit. Hir drove eleven that morning, all of them plumb. Each time hir took a stake off the stack, hir moved the snail to the one below.

On the ninth of them the maul came down on a knot in the grain. There was a short dry tick under the head, hardly a sound at all, and then a long tearing as the stake split down half its length and folded open like a hand. Hir stood with the maul up and hir ears half-lifted in spite of the wet.

Hir pulled the two halves out, threw them on the pile for burning, and took another.

*[What was that?]* Haw sent, from somewhere in town. *[You went all—what was that? Yarrow?]*

*[Stake,]* hir sent.

---

The stakes driven on the days before already had their clouds. They lay along the edge at the height of the lowest branches, one to each stake, with a new hemp line off each underside down to Ruddock's knot. They were dark grey in the rain and rained steadily into the trees, where the ground under the burn steamed. Past the seventh, the eleven hir had driven that morning ran on down the bank toward the fields, bare, waiting for Dash to bring clouds down to them.

At the seventh stake the cloud had come down so low that its underside was level with Yarrow's head. The line ran down past hir shoulder to the stake, and when hir put a hand on it, it was hard as a bar and sang very faintly in the wind.

Hir ears came forward, and hir asked the Core what the line was carrying, from its stretch and its lay.

*Eight hundred newtons, approximately.*

Hir ears stayed forward while hir asked what the cloud held, from its size and how dark it was.

*Water content twelve kilograms, approximately.*

And the pull on the line was upward, away from the ground.

Hir put hir hand up into it.

It was cold and wet, colder than the rain, and there was nothing there. Hir fingers shut on air that clung to them, then opened, then shut again, and came away with water running off them to the elbow. The line under hir other hand stayed hard the whole time. It did not flicker. Hir went in again, up to the shoulder, and felt about for a skin or anything with an edge to it, while the line went on pulling at the stake beside hir.

"Leave it be," said the dun mare, going by with a stake on her back. She didn't stop. She was humming, as she had hummed every day on the line, the same four bars that went up and came round and started again at the top without ever reaching a fifth.

Yarrow took hir arm out.

---

Rainbow Dash came on the line at nine.

She came down out of the rain over the trees with a cloud in front of her, pushing it with her forehooves and her chest and a short hard stroke of the wings at the end of each push. She had soot on one wing still, or again. She brought it down to the eighth stake, the first of Yarrow's, where hir was standing, and the line trailed off the bottom of it and came swinging down past hir face.

Hir took it. Hir walked it in to the stake, keeping the weight on it so it would not foul, and stood there holding it while Ruddock came up and made it fast. Dash went up for the next one.

When she came down with that one, Yarrow was already at the ninth stake with both upper hands up, and the line came down into them. For an hour it went like that, down the line. She would drop a cloud to within a hand's breadth of where it needed to be, and hir would take the line on the first swing. Before the cloud could start to walk, hir had the weight off it. By the time Ruddock got there with his teeth and his hooves, there was nothing left for him to do but the knot.

Ruddock tied a thing like a round turn and two half hitches, with the tail cut short and nothing to seize it. Hir would have left the tail longer, and stopped it. Every time, while hir held the line, hir thumbs went looking for the end hir would have stopped.

Dash said, "Left of that one," once, when the wind moved a cloud. She said, "Heavy," before the twelfth, and it was, and hir had the load before it pulled hir off hir feet in the mud. At the fourteenth, when a line came down short and hir went up the bank on six to reach it, she said, "Got it?" and hir said, "Yes," aloud.

At the top of the hour Dash landed by the stack to drink. She opened her saddlebag for a flask, and on the inside of the flap, where it would show only when the bag was open, a badge was sewn in, blue and gold, three wings on it, with the stitching done very small and even. She shut the flap on it.

"She's quick," she said to Ruddock, with the flask still in her teeth, and tipped her head at Yarrow. "Leave her on the lines with me this morning."

Ruddock looked at Yarrow, and then at Dash.

"It can have the lines, if it wants them," he said, and went back to his knots.

*[You're happy,]* Haw sent, from town. *[You're happy! You're doing a happy. What is it? Is it the water? Is it still doing it? Is it a pony?]*

Yarrow went up the bank for the next one.

---

At half past eleven a young pegasus with a notched ear sat down on the fourth cloud from the end to eat her lunch.

Its underside was a metre off the grass. She had come down on top of it and folded her wings, and then she was sitting there, on nothing, with a bag of oats between her forehooves and her tail hanging over the edge. The cloud gave a little under her, the way a web gives, and came back.

Yarrow stood under it.

Hir ears came forward, and hir asked the Core what she weighed, and it told hir. Hir put a hand on the edge of the cloud, and it went in. Hir put the other hand beside it, and that went in too. Hir got a lower hand up onto the bank for purchase, reached high, and put hir weight on hir arms.

Hir went through it into the wet grass.

It came up round hir, cold and blind, and a huff went out of hir as hir back met the ground. Then the grass was under hir, with the cloud raining into hir face and the pegasus looking over the edge at hir with an oat on her lip. The crew laughed. Ruddock laughed down his nose. The dun mare stopped humming to laugh, and then started the four bars again from the top.

Hir lay there a moment, looking up through the grey at the shape of the pegasus sitting where hir hands had gone through.

*[Did you fall?]* Madder sent. *[Yarrow. Position]*

*[Under it,]* hir sent.

---

They had it out at noon, while the crew ate under the tarpaulin and Yarrow sat on the stack of stakes in the rain with a cold egg Sloe had put in hir hand at the barn door.

*[Through it? All the way through?]* That was Haw, and it had been Haw for some time.

*[Eight hundred newtons off twelve kilos,]* Madder sent. *[Say that again. Slowly]*

*[Eight hundred,]* Yarrow sent. *[Twelve]*

Then it came from four of them in the same second, each over the others.

*[Then the Core's wrong, it's guessing at the water, it can't see into it—]* from Sloe, at the market.

*[So it's buoyant! Warm air goes up, so if it's warm inside—]* from Haw.

*[Not at that load. You'd want a cloud the size of the barn,]* from Madder, across both of them.

*[It's cold inside. Colder than the rain,]* from Yarrow.

*[Then the pony holds it up,]* Sloe sent, when the rest had cleared. *[Not the cloud. The pony's doing it. The one that brings it down]*

*[There's no pony on it, and the line's pulling,]* Madder sent. *[It pulls up. The pony's gone and it still pulls up]*

*[There was a pony on the one that went through,]* Haw sent. *[Sitting on it! Eating on it! Why can she sit on it and Yarrow can't? What's she doing that Yarrow isn't? Is it her feet? Is it the wings? She had them shut, Yarrow said she had them shut—]*

*[Folded,]* Yarrow sent.

*[So it's her,]* Sloe sent. *[Not the cloud. It knows her]*

*[A cloud doesn't know anything. The cloud's numbers are wrong ~ sharper than hir meant,]* Madder sent.

*[Or ours are,]* Teasel sent, clean, and sent nothing after it.

That stopped Haw for nearly a second.

*[Which ones?]* Haw sent. *[The gravity reading? The air? The Core says one g, is it one g?]*

*[Everything we've weighed since we came,]* Sloe sent, slower. *[Eggs. Walnuts. A sack of seed I lifted in the duck house. I lifted it and I knew what it was. I always know what it is ~ and hearing hirself, and not liking it]*

Out on the line the pegasus's cloud had gone up again on its tether, and Yarrow sat and looked at it with the egg in hir hand. Through the frame hir felt fear come up in Sloe at the market, and in Teasel at the barn, in the same second. Hir own tail was round the bottom stake of the stack. Hir had not put it there.

Madder had not sent anything.

The other four waited on Madder, and nothing came. Yarrow had never known Madder go silent that long on a working day. Hir ears came forward, and hir asked the Core how long. *Forty-one seconds.*

When it came, it was the rota.

*[Sloe, food status at dusk. Teasel, in. Haw, nowhere near the edge, not the bank, not the water. Not the trees]*

*[I wasn't going to—]* Haw sent.

*[Not the trees,]* Madder sent.

Yarrow ate the egg, shell and all, and went back to the stakes.

---

The wind got up a little after two. It came down off the forest through the wet trees, so that the rain came at the line sideways and every cloud leaned out on its tether toward the river. Yarrow, at the stack, heard it come on down the bank: a long low creak, stake after stake taking the load.

The eighth stake came out of the ground.

It came out whole, with a short wet suck, and lay a moment in the mud with Ruddock's knot still on it. Then it was going along the bank at the end of its line, bouncing, while the cloud swung broadside to the wind and went off loose over the water toward the fields.

"Three!" the captain said, from his map board. "Three on it, now—Skylark, Gust, you—"

Dash was already gone.

She went up past the cloud in a climb so steep that for a moment she was standing on her tail in the air. At the top of it she turned over on her back, so close that a streamer of grey came away round her, and dropped down the far side in a tight curl that Yarrow's eyes could not follow and hir RADAR only just kept. A sharp breath went in through hir teeth, and hir fright went with it into the frame. Dash came out of the bottom of the curl with the line in her teeth. Then she was in front of the cloud, with both forehooves in it and her wings going short and hard, until it turned. It came back over the line and over the bank, to where it had been, with the stake swinging below it at the end of the rope.

*[What was that? What's happening? Is it the stake again? Yarrow?]* Haw sent, from town.

*[Pony,]* hir sent.

Hir ears came forward. *Ten point four seconds.*

The three the captain had called had got as far as the air above the stack.

Ruddock got the stake, and Dash came down beside him and stood with her sides going, wiping the line-grease off her mouth on her foreleg.

The captain had come down the bank, an old grey stallion with a white muzzle and the map board under his wing. There was a brown ring on its corner, over the forest, where somebody had once set down a mug of tea.

"Dash," he said.

She looked up.

"What's the procedure for a loose cloud?"

It was a quiet voice. All down the bank the crew had stopped.

"Three flyers, sir."

"Three flyers. Ten minutes, and nobody in a hurry." He did not raise it at all. "And if you put a turn like that through a wet cloud that size, over the Pinwheels, and tear it open, where does it come down?"

Dash said nothing.

"On somebody's roof. All at once. I've had three houses in my time, Dash, and I'll not have a fourth for the sake of a turn." He took the board out from under his wing and looked at it, and not at her. "You're in the book already. You're off the line for the rest of the day. Go home."

She went. She did not go up. She walked off along the bank toward the mill bridge with her wings shut so tight to her sides that the feathers at the shoulder stood up. Nopony watched her go. Ruddock was looking at the knot on the stake, and the dun mare at the river. One of the three said something to the others, low, and the others didn't answer.

Nobody looked at the hole in the bank where the stake had been.

Yarrow stood by the stack with a stake across hir upper hands and waited for somebody to say it. It was hir stake. Hir had driven it plumb, and everybody on the bank had seen it come out whole. Hir waited while the captain went back up to his board and Ruddock stood the stake in its hole, and while the crew went back to their lines, and nobody said it.

Nobody had gone after Dash, either. On the ship somebody would have, and hir reached for how it went. When a rigger got back a load that had gone, somebody put a bulb in hir hand at the end of the watch. Dunlin always did, and made a noise about it on the channel. Twice in fifty years it had been Yarrow's load, and hir had been given the bulb. Three times it had been somebody else's, and hir had bought it for them.

Hir went down to the eighth stake. Ruddock stepped back from it without a word and went up the line.

Hir set the stake again a metre up the bank, on firmer ground, with its head leaning toward the trees as the ponies set theirs, and drove it until the head was a hand's breadth off the mud. When it was in, hir put hir weight on the line with both upper hands and all hir legs set, and the stake did not move.

The captain was bent over his board. The dun mare was humming.

---

The wagon came out of the burn at half past three, from up the slope behind the trees, above the mill end of the line.

Two pegasi brought it low over the trees, slung on ropes between them, swinging so that they had to come slowly. It was a child's wagon, four-wheeled. They set it down on the bank by the captain's board on its side, left side up, the way they had found it. The front wheel on that side was gone, and the spokes left on its hub were black stubs. The back one was still on, above the grass. The bed was charred through along one edge, and the paint on the side that faced up had gone to bubbles, red ones, in letters a child had painted with a brush: APPLE BLOOM, with the B the wrong way round.

The captain looked at it for a moment over his board.

"That's the Apple kid's," he said, without much in it. "Name's on it. Somebody run it up to the farm when the cart goes."

Yarrow was halfway down the line with a stake on hir back. Hir stopped. Hir lower left hand closed, in the mud, on nothing. A thin whine came out through hir nose before hir could stop it.

Through the frame it went out of hir too: one short hard spike, like a line parting.

*[Yarrow?]* Madder sent.

*[Nothing,]* Yarrow sent. It went out clean, with nothing under it.

Madder did not send again.

Haw sent, *[What? What was it? Was it the wind? Did something go?]*, and got no answer from anyone.

Hir went on down the line and set the stake. From there hir could see the tailboard, end-on to hir, against the grass. When the cart came at four, with a heavy mare in the shafts, two of the crew lifted the wagon onto it and roped it down. It went off over the mill bridge and up the far road into the rain, with the wheel that was left turning slowly in the air.

Yarrow went back up to the stack for the next one, and moved the snail.

---

All that while Haw had been on the road. Hir had come out at three, as far as the mill bridge, with hir tail held straight up behind hir to keep it out of the mud.

*[I'm not near the edge,]* hir had sent. *[I'm near the bridge. The bridge isn't the edge. I only want to see one close]*

*[Haw,]* Madder had sent.

*[I'm on the road, I'm on the road!]*

Hir had stayed on the road for most of two hours, sitting in the mud, sending everything hir saw: the pegasi, the heavy mare with the cart, a heron at the bend of the river, which the Core would only call a bird. Yarrow felt hir through the frame all afternoon, bright and restless.

At a little before five hir came off the bridge and went along the bank, toward the tree where the bank turned. It was quiet. Hir sent nothing about it.

The wind had dropped a little by then. Yarrow was at the head of the line by the bridge with a stake in hir hands when hir heard Haw send.

*[~ nothing in it but delight, rising]*

Hir looked up.

Just below the bridge, where the bank turned, there was a tree taller than the rest, an ash, old, with a straight grey trunk and its first branches very high. The crew called it the corner tree. The last cloud on the line was moored under its crown, low over the water, at the height of its upper limbs. Haw was in the tree. Hir was a long way up, out along a limb that went toward the cloud, on all six, with hir tail wrapped round the limb behind hir. Hir fur was dark with the wet and hir ears were flat, and hir had one upper hand out in front of hir, open, going toward the grey.

*[It's right there. It's right THERE. I can feel it cold on my face. I'm nearly in it. Yarrow, I'm nearly in it, I'm going to put my hand in—]*

*[Haw. Down,]* Madder sent, from the barn, and under it the frame went tight all at once in four bodies.

*[Haw, come in to the trunk, come in to the trunk ~ trying to sound easy, and not]* from Sloe.

*[Haw, the limb's wet—]* from Teasel.

*[Haw. Down. Now,]* from Madder again, over both.

*[It's inside! It's all wet inside, it's like being in the tank, it's like the tank but it MOVES ~ laughing]* Haw sent, to all of them at once and to none. *[One more. One more and I'm in—]*

Yarrow stood on the bank with the stake across hir upper hands. Hir did not send anything. Hir had hir eyes on the limb, where it went out from the trunk, and on the way it bent under hir, which was more than a limb that thick should bend.

Up in the ash, the wood gave a short, dry tick.

## 7. The Commission

The Countess wanted the bodice to catch the light when she turned, and not before, so Rarity had spent the afternoon setting the sapphires a hair's breadth deeper than was sensible. The thirtieth went in at half past six. She was still looking at it from the side, a quarter of an hour later, when Sweetie Belle said from the window seat, "What's that?"

Sweetie had been doing her reading on the window seat since four. She had turned one page.

"What's what?"

"In the street."

Rarity came round the worktable with a needle still in her mouth. Up the street from the mill end, where the lamps weren't lit yet, something was coming fast and low through the mud. For a moment she took it for a cart that had lost its pony. Then the miller came out in front of it at a canter, with a lantern swinging from his teeth and his hat jammed down over his ears, and the thing behind him came apart into bodies.

There were five of them. Everypony said there were five: the five from the forest, the ones in the seed barn. The biggest was in the middle. It walked on four legs, with its front two held up against its chest, and across them it carried another. Three more were packed round it so close that they kept knocking into each other. Each of those went on six, hands and feet both, in a lurching scramble that looked like falling and never quite was. One of them kept a hand flat on the carried one's back. Another had hold of the big one's flank. The smallest came last, with its hand on the one in front; when it slipped, it did not let go.

They passed under her window close enough that she could have tapped on the glass. The carried one's head was tipped back over the big one's arm, with its eyes open. One of its forelegs, or arms, hung loose from the elbow, and below the elbow it was bent in two places. Through the glass Rarity heard a thin high whine from one of them, higher than any pony could make, until it cut off. Blood ran down the big one's chest and along the street after them. It was very dark, almost black under the miller's lantern, and it did not thin out in the puddles as she would have expected.

Then they were past. When they turned at the corner toward Mulberry Lane, the street was empty except for the rain.

Rarity took the needle out of her mouth and put it in the pincushion. Her hoof was quite steady.

"Upstairs, Sweetie."

She was braced for *why*. Sweetie closed her book and went. Her hooves on the stairs were quick and light, and they did not stop until the door at the top shut.

Later Rarity went up and knocked on Sweetie's door, and Sweetie said through it that she was doing her reading.

---

The Countess's letter came with the morning post, and would not fold flat.

It was on a cream paper so heavy that it had been folded once and then had simply refused. It lay open on the counter, curling at both ends like a pastry, while Rarity read it for the third time. The Countess was *so looking forward*. The Countess wondered whether a little reticule might be managed, in the same silk, by Thursday. There was no word anywhere in the letter, on either side of the paper, about the last gown, or the forty bits still owing on it, or the hundred and forty for this one.

"A reticule," Rarity said to nopony. "By Thursday. Certainly. Out of the air."

She put the letter under the cash box, where it went on curling.

Sweetie had gone to school while she read it. Only when Rarity cleared the breakfast things did she see the bowl: the oats pushed from one side to the other and back again, and none of them eaten.

She lifted Opal out of the gown box for the first time that day. Opal went limp in the air, which was her protest, and was set down on the stairs, and walked back.

The customers began at nine, and every one of them had news.

Mrs. Tansy came for a hem. While the pins went in round her, she said they had taken the creature's whole arm off at the shoulder, and it had screamed the hospital down. When Lemon Drop came in for her collar, she said no, it was only the hand, and it hadn't made a sound, not one, which was worse, if you asked her. It had come down out of the corner tree by the mill bridge, she said, the big ash, straight down onto the stones at the edge of the water. A mare Rarity didn't know put her head round the door only to ask whether it was true that the thing's blood was black. Rarity said she had no idea, which was a lie.

"It's the big one I'm sorry for," said Mrs. Tansy, turning on the stool. "Carried it all the way up from the mill, the miller said. Wouldn't let anypony else take it."

Rarity turned her a quarter-turn and did not answer.

---

Twilight came in a little before eleven, looking grey.

She had a stub of pencil in her mouth, crossways, like a dog with a stick, and did not seem to know it was there until she tried to say hello round it. She took it out and held it in her hoof instead.

"I'm not staying," she said. "I only wanted to sit down somewhere that wasn't there."

Rarity cleared the fitting stool of Mrs. Tansy's offcuts. Twilight sat on it and looked at the thirty sapphires down the bodice. When she said nothing about them, Rarity understood that Spike had told her.

"They took her hand," Twilight said. "In the night. The hand, and the arm up to the elbow. The bone had gone in four places and the doctor couldn't— he said he couldn't tell what was hers and what wasn't. What was meant to be there." She turned the pencil over. "I held the lamp. He didn't know how much to give her, for the pain. Madder told him her weight to the pound, straight off, as if she'd looked it up somewhere, and he didn't trust it. He halved it to be safe. It wasn't enough, and after that he was frightened to give her any more."

"Oh, Twilight."

"I'm fine." She put the pencil back in her mouth and took it out again. "They said it'll grow back."

Rarity waited.

"Madder said. The one who does the talking, when it's serious. She said it would grow back, and I said how long, and she said six years." Twilight looked at the pencil. "Six. Years. I asked her to say it again, in case I had it wrong, and she said it exactly the same. She said it the way you'd tell somepony the time."

"Six years."

"Six years," Twilight said, and it came out of her like a question the second time too. She shook her head. "I keep saying it. It won't— it doesn't sound like anything. All morning."

Rarity had nothing to say to that, so she made tea. Twilight drank half of it before she went back to the hospital, and left the other half on the counter by the Countess's letter.

---

At noon Rarity went over Spike's sapphires one at a time with the burnisher, easing each claw down a last hair onto its stone until none of them would catch a thread.

When she brought him thirty rough stones in a twist of paper, he had brought her back thirty that looked as if they had been poured. Every facet was crisp. There was not a scratch on the tables. She had said *two bits apiece, Spike, and worth every copper*, and he had gone pink to the ends of his spines. Sixty bits. There were eleven in the cash box, and the Countess's forty had been *outstanding* since February.

She lifted Opal out of the gown box for the second time. Opal had taken the tissue paper for a bed and made it into one. She was put on the stairs with a lecture about Canterlot, which she did not attend to.

---

Sweetie came home at four and stood in the workroom door with her satchel still on.

"Apple Bloom told a lie last night," she said.

Rarity had eleven pins in her mouth and a sleeve over one foreleg. "Mm?"

"About her wagon. It came home on a cart. From the weather ponies. It was all burned, the wheel was gone, and Applejack wanted to know how it got up there, and Apple Bloom said we'd left it down by the creek and somepony must've taken it, and Applejack just looked at her, and Big Mac—" She stopped. She stopped the way she had stopped as a very small filly when she ran into the edge of a table: all at once, with her mouth still open.

Rarity took the pins out. "And Big Mac what?"

"Nothing," said Sweetie. "It doesn't matter. I've got reading."

"Sweetie, darling, has something happened at—"

The clock on the landing struck the quarter. Rarity looked at the sleeve over her foreleg, and put the pins back in her mouth. Sweetie went upstairs.

---

The first sleeve was set by ten, and it was the best she had ever done.

The cap sat on the shoulder without a pucker. The seam ran down the back of the arm straight as a ruled line. As she always did when something came out right, she thought of the christening gown. She had made it for Mrs. Cobble next door, the summer she was eight, out of a lawn so thin it tore if you looked at it. The hem dipped at the back by the width of a finger. The foal was carried all day, and nopony at the christening ever saw the back of it. Mrs. Cobble cried when she paid her, a bit and a boiled sweet. Rarity still saw the hem.

She cut the second sleeve at eleven, and had it pinned by half past, when Sweetie came down in the dark.

She came down without a light. She stood at the bottom of the stairs in her nightgown with one hoof on the last step, and Rarity turned the lamp up a little so that she could see her face. Sweetie had not been crying.

"We took a mirror," Sweetie said.

Rarity put the sleeve down.

"From the castle. The old one in the forest, the sisters' one. It was a dare, Scootaloo said Rainbow Dash went right inside once and it wasn't even scary, and I said it was stupid, I *said*, and then I went anyway, since they were going. And there was a room with no roof, and it was on its face in the nettles, and Apple Bloom saw it first and said *that's mine*, she wanted it for her barn, and Scootaloo wanted to show Rainbow Dash, and it was so heavy we had to use a board." She took a breath. "That Friday. Before last. On the way home, after the little stream, it just turned over—" Her eyes went sideways to the dark window, and came back. "By itself. And there were places in it. Grass all bent over, and a floor with something spilling on it, only slow, really slow, and it made a noise like the barn door. Then there was a light and it got hot and the glass went soft, and then there was fire everywhere. And there was a heap. On the path, behind the wagon. It moved. A hand came over the side and got the wagon, and Apple Bloom was still in the traces, she couldn't get the buckle, and I just kept saying her name—" She stopped dead.

Rarity waited. The lamp hissed. Somewhere back by the stream something had gone past her, the way a dropped stitch goes past under the needle, and she could not find where.

"She got out," Sweetie said. "We ran. Scootaloo was already gone, and when we found her she was looking at something dead, and Apple Bloom got her by the scruff. We came out by the river. We said we'd been at the clubhouse, and we never told anypony, and Apple Bloom lied about the wagon and Applejack knew and didn't say, and now the one that fell out of the tree by the bridge—" She stopped again. "It's our fault."

Rarity was on her feet. She did not go to her. Sweetie stood very straight, with her hoof still on the last step.

"Who else knows?"

"Nobody. Just us."

The clock on the landing said twenty to twelve, and the train was at eight. The second sleeve lay pinned on the table. Rarity thought first of Applejack, who had asked her sister a question and been lied to. Then she thought of whoever was minding Scootaloo these days. Last of all, while the clock went on, she thought of the hospital on Mulberry Lane with its lamps still lit, and the creature in it that had been carried past this window with its arm hanging wrong.

She thought: Sweetie is not going to stand in a room full of grown ponies and strangers and say that. Not while I'm alive. I'll tell them tonight, quietly, before the market has it. First thing tomorrow I'll go to Applejack myself, and to Scootaloo's, so that nopony hears it from anypony but me. The sleeve can be set by dawn. I have set sleeves in less.

"Get your coat," she said.

Sweetie looked up.

"We're going to the hospital. You needn't say a word."

Sweetie opened her mouth, and shut it, and went for her coat.

---

The hospital on Mulberry Lane was a doctor's house with a ward built on the back. The night nurse who let them in wore a cap that had been starched by somepony who hated her, and she took them down the passage without a word.

The ward had four beds and one lamp. Three of the beds were empty, with the blankets folded square at the foot: grey wool in a loose cheap twill that would be bald at every fold within the year. The fourth bed was by the far wall. The lamp stood beside it on a stool, turned low, next to a cup of tea that had gone cold with a skin on it.

The miller was asleep on a hard chair by the door, with his head against the wall. His hat lay on the chair beside him, the flat grey felt with the faded band. It had stopped dripping some time ago, though a dark patch still showed on the floorboards under the brim. Beyond him, Twilight was curled in the armchair at the end of the bed, with her notebook open on her chest and the pencil still in her mouth. She had held the lamp all last night. Rarity let her sleep.

Then she looked at the bed.

The one in it lay on its back with its eyes half open. The four others were round it so closely that at first they were one shape: a single knot of dark fur, with the lamp on it. The big one sat on the floor at the head of the bed, with one arm laid along the pillow and a hand on the sleeper's head. On the far side one stood with a hand flat on the sleeper's chest. On the near side another crouched by the bed, with a hand on the big one's back. The smallest had got up onto the foot of the bed somehow and folded itself small there, holding the good hand in both of its own. Round one of its wrists was a length of ribbon, knotted twice: red grosgrain, a good inch wide, scorched brown and stiff along one edge. It was decent ribbon, with the firm rib that holds a bow, and it was ruined. Rarity noted it as she would have noted a hem. Their tails lay on the floor in one heap, wound through each other. When she looked for the rise and fall in them, it came very slowly, all at once.

Sweetie's eyes had gone to the ribbon. They stayed on it, and then went to the floor. Rarity saw her sister looking, and could not read that either.

The arm on the near side, nearest Rarity at the door, ended at the elbow. It was bound in clean linen, wound in a figure of eight, firm and very even; somepony who knew what they were doing had wound it. Near the end the linen had gone dark, the same dark as the street.

They had all turned to look at her. They had done it together, heads coming round in one movement, and none of them had got up.

Rarity had prepared a speech on the walk over, and it went. She said, "Forgive me. My name is Rarity. I have the boutique on the square, and this is my sister, Sweetie Belle. I'm sorry to come at this hour, with her so ill. I have to tell you something, and I thought you ought to hear it from me and not from the market."

She had said *her*. She let it stand.

There was a pause. It was not long, but it was there, as if each of them were listening for a moment to somepony in the next room. Then the one with her hand on the sleeper's chest said, "Yes. Go on."

So Rarity told them. She told it plainly, as long as she kept her eyes off the bed: three fillies and a dare, a castle, an old broken mirror in a wagon.

At *wagon*, two of them turned their heads to the big one, together, and back again. Rarity did not know what she had said. She went on: the mirror waking on the path home and breaking, and a fire, and shapes in the fire, and the fillies running. She said that nopony had known until tonight. She said she was not asking them for anything.

When she had finished, all four of them went still.

The big one's hand lay on the sleeper's head without a tremor. Nothing moved in any of them, not an ear, not a finger, not the heap of tails on the floor. Two of them had their ears out wide to either side, stiff. The small one's ears had gone flat. The rain went on at the window while Rarity counted four breaths of her own.

Then the one with her hand on the chest said, "Which day?"

"The Friday before last."

"The hour."

"Evening. I believe." Rarity looked round for Sweetie, who knew. Sweetie was standing half behind her with her eyes on the floorboards, on the damp patch under the miller's hat. "About sunset."

"Where, exactly."

"On the old path back from the castle. Past a little stream, I think, on a rise."

"Which object. Say it again."

"A mirror. An old one, broken already, with a metal rim."

The one who asked nodded once. She had asked every question in the same careful voice, each word set down on its own, and nothing in her voice had moved.

"Is the mirror still where it was left?" she said. "Or did the children keep any piece of it?"

"I— they ran," said Rarity. "I don't believe they kept anything."

"Thank you." She turned back to the bed, and her ears came slowly forward.

The one on the near side spoke then, from where she crouched, and her voice was warmer, and quicker. Under it, very faint, Rarity caught a thin high sound, like a whistle behind a door.

"The children, though. Three, you said, three of them. Were they hurt? Burned at all, or cut, or knocked about? Did they all get home in one bit?"

Rarity had an answer ready for a different question, and had to put it down. "No," she said. "No. They ran. Nopony was hurt."

"Good," said that one. "Good. All three." Her ears came forward too.

Then the one in the bed laughed.

She had not moved or opened her eyes any wider. She lay under the grey blanket with the bound arm on top of it and laughed, weakly but for real, the way somepony laughs at a joke told to them from across a room. It went on. The hand on her chest pressed down, flat, and she laughed under it with her head turned into the big one's arm. None of the others said anything to her. Rarity looked from face to face for whatever she had missed, and could read nothing in any of them.

The big one had not spoken. She was looking past Rarity at Sweetie, steadily, from under the lamp. She went on looking, and Sweetie did not raise her eyes from the floor.

Rarity waited for her to say something. She was sure, standing there, that Sweetie would at least say *sorry*, or *it was me*, in her own small voice, and that she would be glad when she did. Sweetie's eyes went once more to the ribbon on the small one's wrist, and back to the floor. She said nothing at all.

"Thank you for coming," said the warm-voiced one, after the pause. "Two of you, out in the wet, and past midnight by your clocks. You'll be wanting your beds. Hir will sleep now, I think."

In the armchair Twilight had not stirred. The nurse held the door.

---

It was past half one when they got back. Sweetie went upstairs without being told. Rarity lit the second lamp and sat down to the sleeve.

She set it by three. It was not as good as the other one.

She could see it the moment she stood the form under the lamp. The cap sat a quarter of an inch forward of where it should, so that the ease bunched faintly at the front of the shoulder. The seam, which ran true all the way down the left arm, wandered off just above the elbow on this one. The Countess wouldn't see it. It would still be there, turning with her, every time she turned.

Rarity looked at the clock and at the seam, and did the arithmetic. To unpick it and set it again, properly, was three hours at least. It was twenty past three.

She pressed it instead, as well as a seam that was wrong could be pressed. Then she took the gown off the form and folded it into the tissue. She put the lid on the box and tied it. Opal was asleep on the stairs and did not notice.

She sat down in the chair by the worktable to rest her eyes for ten minutes before going up to the farm.

---

The knock woke her at twenty to seven.

The box was on the counter where she had left it, tied, with the Countess's letter still curling under the cash box beside it. There was grey light in the window, and the rain was coming down steadily on the square. Rarity went to the door with her mane flat on one side, thinking *Applejack, the farm*, with the speech half composed.

Applejack was on the step.

Her hat was dark with rain, with water running off the brim. A crate of eggs was strapped to her side under a sack, so she had come straight from the market. Apple Bloom stood a pace behind her in the wet, with her mane plastered flat and no bow in it, looking at the step.

"Applejack," said Rarity. "I was just coming to—"

"I know," Applejack said. She wasn't loud. She sounded as if she had not slept either. "The miller's been telling it in the bread queue since five. Clover Patch had it off the nurse." She looked past Rarity at the stairs, where Sweetie had come down as far as the landing and stopped. Then she looked at Rarity again.

"My sister's been lying to my face for two weeks, and I had to hear it off Clover Patch," she said. "You told them before you told me."

## 8. What There Is

Sloe did the count at dusk on the lid of the seed bin, where the last of the light came flat across the stubble and in at the big door.

Hir laid it out as hir always did: walnuts in fives, eggs in a row, and the bean sack at the end with its neck rolled down so that hir could see how far it had gone. Yarrow earned six bits a day on the crew line. Three of them went to Applejack's stall at noon against the tab, as they did every day, before Yarrow had carried them further than the end of the line, and the other three lay on the lid beside the eggs.

*[Walnuts, thirty-one. Eggs, nine ~ counting the eggs a second time, and liking the second count less]*

Teasel sat on the sill of the big door with hir back against the post. Hir had the number before Sloe did. All week hir had watched the pile go down a walnut at a time, and could have said who had taken which, how fast they had eaten it, and whether they had looked round first.

Haw never looked round. Hir sat in the straw with the bound arm across hir chest, in the sling the night nurse had knotted for hir out of a flour sack, and took an egg off hir own pile and cracked it against hir knee. Hir did it one-handed, with a short hard knock, then tipped the egg into hir mouth and the shell in after it.

*[Did you see that? Two halves. CLEAN ~ enormously pleased with hirself]*

*[Seen,]* Sloe sent. *[That's your fourth today]*

*[Is it? The arm's hungry. Madder said it would be. Is there a fifth?]*

There was not a fifth, and Sloe sent nothing more. Teasel had hir own egg under a lower hand on the sill. It had been warm from Sloe's basket that morning, and it was cold now. When Sloe turned to roll the bean sack shut, Teasel got up and crossed the barn toward the trough, and on the way the egg went into the straw beside Haw's head, into the hollow Haw rolled into at night. Haw found an egg there most mornings.

At the trough hir drank for a long time. Hir had asked the Core once what water was worth. *Zero kilocalories.* It sat in the stomach, though, and made a weight there.

*[Teasel. Status,]* Madder sent from the back of the barn, where hir was going over the door straps with Yarrow.

*[Fine,]* Teasel sent.

Hir felt it go out flat and smooth, with nothing under it, and felt what it cost to send it that way. Sloe's ears moved. Yarrow's did not. Nobody sent anything after it, and underneath all five of them the low, steady wrongness went on in the frame, as it had for two weeks.

That night hir lay on the outside of the heap, where hir tended to end up, and where Haw, reaching about in hir sleep some time after midnight, went over hir to get to Yarrow. At first grey the swallows' second brood started up in the rafters. Teasel lay still and counted the feeds. Before the sun reached the ridge there had been fifty-three, and every one was the same: a shape in the gap under the eaves, a shriek from the nest, and a beak being filled. When the sun was up hir went into town with Sloe for the eggs. On the river road hir fell behind at the mill, and then further, and Sloe waited at the far end of the bridge without sending why.

---

The fillies came on the fifteenth day, in the afternoon, after the rain had stopped.

Teasel smelled them from the sill before hir heard them. There were three small ponies and a larger one, and with them came apples, wet wool, something sweet gone stale in a bag, and over all of it a thin sharp smell that hir had last had on the rise with the ferns burning. Hir ears went round to the road. Hir tail came in against hir flank and wound itself tight.

They came down the track from the river road with Applejack behind them. At the field gate Applejack stopped and said something low to Apple Bloom, who nodded without looking at her. Then Applejack stood by the gatepost and let them go on alone. Teasel watched her pull a stem of grass from the verge and put it in her mouth, and stand there chewing it without swallowing.

The three of them stopped just outside the big door, in a row on the threshold stones. Apple Bloom was in the middle, with a saddlebag on and no bow in her mane. Sweetie Belle had her eyes on the ground already. Scootaloo stood half a step behind the other two with her wings clamped shut.

"We came ourselves," Apple Bloom said. "Nobody sent us. Rarity told you already, I know that. But." She stopped. "So we came."

Nobody in the barn moved. Sloe had a walnut in hir hand, halfway to the bin. Haw was in the straw with hir back against a post, Madder by the door straps with Yarrow beside hir. Teasel was nearest the fillies of all five, close enough on the sill to have touched Scootaloo's tail.

*[Facts are logged,]* Madder sent. Hir ears had gone flat. *[We have the day, the hour, the place and the object. Nothing they say changes the rota]*

*[Madder, say something to them, say something kind, they're children, look at the little one's knees ~ all of Sloe's warmth at once, far too much of it]*

*[Three of them! Three, with a wagon, and they pushed it ~ laughing]* That was Haw, and it came out aloud as well, a high breathy chirr of a laugh that made Sweetie Belle flinch. *[All that, and it was them—]*

Then Yarrow sent, and nothing rode under it at all.

*[I knew. The wagon. The day it came out of the burn]*

It reached the four of them in the same instant. Teasel felt Sloe take it, and Haw, whose laugh went out. Hir felt it in hirself, low, where the hunger lived. In Madder it did not land so much as set: hir whole weight in the frame went hard and still, like a line when the load comes on.

*[Four days?]* Sloe sent. *[Yarrow. Four days? ~ hir had turned to Yarrow in the ward at the word, the same as Madder, and had hoped every day since to be wrong]*

Yarrow sent nothing. Hir tail had come round and wound once about hir own ankle, and it stayed there.

All of it had happened while Apple Bloom drew one breath. She let it out, looking from one of them to the next at five strangers gone still.

Then Madder spoke aloud. Hir voice was careful and slow, with each word set down on its own.

"What was it like?"

Apple Bloom's ears went back. "What?"

"The night," Madder said. "Rarity told us what happened. She could not tell us what it was like. What was it like, for you?"

Teasel smelled it go into them cold. Sweetie Belle's smell sharpened. Apple Bloom's jaw set, and she said, "It was bad, what do you think it was like," and would have gone on, except that Scootaloo came forward past her into the light.

"Loud," Scootaloo said.

They waited for her.

"It was loud, and then it went all quiet in my ears, like after a firecracker. And there was a heap on the path. You. Right next to me, moving, and I couldn't move. I wanted to fly and my wings wouldn't." She stopped, and started again. "Then I ran. I left them. Apple Bloom was stuck in the harness and I just went." Her voice had gone flat and small. "That's not the bit, though. Everybody thinks it turned over by itself. It didn't. There was a crack down the side, like a seam, and I had a stick with a bent end, and I put it in the crack because I wanted to see what was inside. Apple Bloom said no. She said it twice. I did it anyway, while she was pulling and couldn't see. And it went *tick*." She was looking at the floor between Teasel's lower hands. "Me."

In the frame Haw started to send something, and didn't. Teasel could smell the cost on Scootaloo: salt, and the sourness of a filly who had left her breakfast in the bowl.

"I didn't tell either," Apple Bloom said quickly, as if it were a race. "Sweetie told Rarity everything but that. We said we wouldn't. And I lied to my sister about the wagon, and she knew, and she never said nothing." She swallowed. "And there was a hand. On my wagon. It came over the side and grabbed on, and it wouldn't let go, and I was stuck in the traces and it kept pulling me backwards, and I thought—"

Across the barn, Yarrow's lower left hand lay open in the straw. As Teasel watched, it turned over, palm down, and stayed like that.

"—and I got out, but my bow was caught in the buckle, and I left it. It was my good one." Apple Bloom stopped. She was looking at Teasel's wrist.

The ribbon had been there since the third morning, when Sloe had tied it on in the dark and said to keep it on. Teasel had stopped feeling it days ago. The burned edge had gone hard as bark, and the rain had pulled the knot so tight that hir was not sure it would open at all.

Hir put the wrist to hir mouth and worked at the knot with hir teeth, and then got a thumb of the other hand under it. It took longer than it should have. Hir could feel all four of them watching through the frame. The three fillies watched with their eyes, and Applejack watched from the gate, too far off to see anything. The knot gave. Teasel held the ribbon out across the sill on hir open hand, where it lay in a curl, red on one side and brown on the other.

Apple Bloom looked at it and did not take it. Then she reached out and took it in her teeth by the middle, carefully, so that her lips did not touch hir palm. She turned it over and looked at the burned edge for a moment. Then she put it in her saddlebag and did the buckle up.

Somewhere at the back of the frame Sloe felt it go into a filly's bag, and sent nothing about it.

Sweetie Belle had not said a word yet. Now she said, all at once, "The fire smelled like hot pennies, and the ferns made a noise like screaming, they did, and there was a little animal dead in the clearing, all curled up with smoke coming off it, and Scootaloo was just standing there looking at it, and I thought if we stayed one more second we'd—" She stopped dead, with her mouth still a little open. Then she lifted her eyes off the ground for the first time, and looked straight at Madder.

"What was it like for you?"

*[That isn't theirs to have,]* Madder sent.

*[She asked you, Madder, a child asked you straight out, you can't just— ~ hot all through, and ashamed for the lot of them]* from Sloe.

*[Tell her about the frog! Tell her the frog ~ laughing again, and hurting with it now]* from Haw.

Yarrow sent nothing.

"Your account is received," Madder said aloud, in the same careful voice. "The Cluster thanks you for it. It will be kept."

The three fillies stared at hir. Sweetie Belle's ears went slowly down.

"Oh, it was a night, wasn't it," Sloe said aloud, quickly, warmly, with the words coming over each other. "It was a night for everyone. We had it rough too, you know, we'd no idea where we were, and none of us is saying you— well. It's all water under the mill now, as they say here. And you came all the way out in the wet, the three of you, with your sister waiting up at the gate, would you take a walnut, any of you? We've got—" Hir stopped, with the walnut still in hir hand. Teasel, who knew the count, watched hir fail to hold it out.

Haw was still laughing, softer now, into hir good shoulder, with hir eyes shut. Yarrow's hand stayed palm down in the straw.

Teasel heard hirself begin before hir had decided to.

"I smelled you," hir said.

All three fillies turned to hir. To get the rest, hir had to go back to the rise, at the speed it had happened. Hir went. Hir had the heat on hir back again, and the crack and spit of the ferns, and the ground pulling at a body that did not know yet how to hold itself up. There was Yarrow's grip locked on something hir could not see. Under the smoke there were three small sharp smells going away downhill, fast. Hir felt the other four feel hir go. Nobody stopped hir.

"Before I could see," hir said. "Before I knew what anything here was, I knew you. Three of you, going away downhill. You smell the same now."

Scootaloo looked at hir. Sweetie Belle shut her mouth. Apple Bloom said, "Okay," and nodded, and went on nodding a little after she had stopped meaning it. Then she said, "We gotta go. My sister's waiting."

They went up the track in a row. At the gate Applejack took the grass stem out of her mouth and dropped it, and they went off up the river road with Applejack half a step behind Apple Bloom, close to her, not touching.

---

They were barely past the gate when Madder sent.

*[Four days]*

*[Yes,]* Yarrow sent.

*[You stood on that bank and sent me Nothing. Clean. I felt it, and I let it go]*

*[The small one's knees]*

*[On a working day. With the rota out. And you decided, on your own, that—]*

The send stopped in the middle. Teasel had never felt Madder do that. When hir went out by the small back door and stood in the stubble with hir back to the barn, Yarrow sat down where hir was, and nobody went after either of them. After a while Sloe put the walnut back on the pile and began the count again from the start.

When Madder came in after dark, hir lay down in the heap a body's width from Yarrow. The straw between them stayed cold all night, and all five of them could have said exactly how wide it was.

---

In the fourth week Teasel went to the river more and more.

Hir went to drink, and to sit somewhere with nobody counting anything. The frame came with hir, as it came everywhere, but the noise of the weir filled hir ears. Hir was slow on the road now. Hir left the barn on four with hir upper hands carried, and by the mill hir was on six.

The weir crossed the river below the mill in a long slope of stone, with the water going over it in one smooth sheet and breaking white at the foot. Beside it, on the town side, there was a low cottage with a tarred roof, and for a gatepost it had the tiller off a barge, painted green so long ago that the green was mostly in the cracks of the grain.

It was there, on the third evening, that Teasel smelled the fish.

Under the smells of weed and wet stone there was a thicker one that came and went with the current, oily and rich. It had not occurred to hir to ask what it was. Haw had put a hand in the river in the first week and sent *[There are things in it! Moving things!]* to everyone, and nobody had asked anything further. Hir ears came forward now, and hir asked the Core what a fish would be, as food.

*Protein, high. Fat, moderate. Zinc and copper present. Suitable.*

Hir asked how much fish would make a day.

*One point three kilograms, approximately.*

Hir sat on the bank with hir hands in hir lap and looked up the frog. It came back at once, as facts: the flat stone in the stream hollow, the gold eye, the throat going. Hir looked up the grey doe at Fluttershy's, which had gone over the plank bridge toward the smoke and not come back. Below the weir, Fluttershy's stream came out of the forest by her cottage and ran into the river, and her south meadow ran down to meet it. Whatever went into this water went past the place where her animals came down to drink.

Teasel had never killed anything in hir life.

The old mare from the cottage had seen hir the first evening, from her step, where she sat eating fried potatoes out of a paper. They were cold, by the smell, with vinegar on them. She had not said anything. On the second evening she nodded. On the fourth she came down to the gate with the tiller in it and leaned there, looking at the river, not at Teasel.

"Slack water, under the apron," she said, and tipped her head downstream, to where the white water ran out and went dark and slow. "Deep hole there. Big 'uns lie up in the bottom of it while the river's cold. They come up at dusk."

Then she went back indoors and shut the door.

---

Teasel went down to the pool the next evening, at dusk.

Below the weir the white water spread and slowed and went black, and the bank dropped away under it so that hir could not see bottom. Hir lay flat on the stones at the edge, on the town side below the cottage, and put both upper hands in to the elbow.

The cold went in to the bone at once. Hir ears came forward, and hir asked the Core. *Ten degrees.* Hir kept the hands still. Kin hands were fast, faster than anything hir had seen in this place, and when they closed they locked. Hir kept them still while the light went off the water and a bat came out over the weir, and went on keeping them still after hir fingers had stopped telling hir anything.

Hir felt the fish before hir saw it. The water moved against the backs of hir fingers, and then something long and slow slid along the inside of hir wrist, touching it, as if the wrist were a root.

Hir closed.

It was all muscle. The whole length of it bucked and threw water in hir face, but the lock held, so that hir came up off the stones onto hir knees with it, a fish as long as hir forearm, cold and fighting, its tail slapping hir chest. Hir did not know what to do next. Hir hit it on the stones and it slid; hir hit it again, wrong, across the tail end, and it went on fighting, and a thin high whine came out of hir that hir could not stop. Then hir got both thumbs in behind its head and bent it back until something went. It shook along its whole length and stopped.

The spike went out of hir into the frame before hir could do anything about it.

*[Teasel?]* That was three of them at once.

Hir ate it there on the bank, sitting back on hir heels, raw. The skin was tough and the flesh under it was pale and cold and dense, and hir stomach took it as if it had been waiting. Hir spat out the larger bones and ate the rest, the head last. It took a while. Hir did not look at anything but the fish.

*[Teasel. Status,]* Madder sent.

Hir tried to strip it and could not. It went out with everything riding under it that hir had held down for three weeks: *[At the pool ~ full, and shaking with it]*

*[FULL? Full of what? What have you got? Is it a thing from the river? Teasel, is it a fish, did you get one, can I—]* from Haw, and then Haw's question stopped.

They came in over each other, all in the same second. *[Nine eggs, and Haw's four, and the one in the straw every morning ~ Sloe going back through three weeks of eggs, morning by morning, and every morning coming out short by one]* was on top of Madder's *[Teasel—]*, and under both of them the cold place in the frame, that all four had had a hand on for three weeks, turned over and showed what it was. Then all of it cut off at once, and nobody finished.

There were hooves on the towpath. A mare was coming along it from the mill end in the last of the light, with two foals. The smaller foal was carrying a jam jar with a stickleback in it, holding it up to see the fish turn. They stopped. The mare looked at a Kin on the bank with a fish's tail in hir hand and blood on hir mouth and down to hir chin. Without a word she put a hoof to each foal and turned them round, then walked them back the way they had come, not fast. The older foal looked back once. "Gryphon," he said, in the voice for a word you don't say in front of your mother.

"Walk," the mare said.

The jam jar went away up the towpath with the stickleback turning in it. Teasel sat on the bank and rinsed hir hands in the river. Hir thumbs still had the neck in them, the small give and then nothing, and when hir took them out of the water they would not keep still. By the bread queue at five, hir thought, the miller would have it.

---

Pinkie Pie was at the bakery's back door when Teasel and Sloe went by in the dark, with flour to her elbows and the oven open behind her, eating a heel of bread standing up. She stopped chewing when she saw them. Then she started again, and said "Morning," round the bread, a beat later than she used to. As they passed she said to Sloe, low, "Is she all right?"

"Right as rain," Sloe said.

Twilight sat on the library steps at noon with a sandwich in wax paper going soft in the drizzle, which she had forgotten about, and a new card box under her wing, tied with string. She said hello and asked nothing. Her eyes went once to Teasel's mouth, and away. On the step below her Spike was polishing a stone with a cloth. When he was done he looked at it against the light, and put a chip of something green in his mouth from a twist of paper at his side, and crunched it.

At the boutique window Rarity stood with a letter in her hoof and a slice of toast in the glow of her horn, eating it over the letter as she read. Crumbs went down onto the paper. She tipped them off without looking up and took another bite.

Applejack ate an apple at her stall, core and pips and all, while she crossed a line out in her book. Sloe came back up the road with the empty basket and sent *[Tab's under two, with this week's walnuts on it]* to all of them, and nobody answered.

It rained on the Tuesday, and again on the Thursday. The stallion with the bad knee said he had known it would. Yarrow sent from the crew line that Dash was on the line with hir again, and that Ruddock had given hir a knot to tie.

Madder sent the rota every dusk, and nobody kept it. Haw went to the mill bridge anyway and sat on the road, and Sloe went to market at the wrong hour and came back with nothing, and Teasel went to the weir. Madder knew where each of them was, and did not send the rota a second time. At the fountain a mare called her colt in from the rim when Teasel came by, and he went, looking back. The egg mare at the market put her price up for the Kin by a bit a dozen, and said it was the rain.

Once, far off, on the path along the far side of the river road, Fluttershy went by with a basket on her back. She stopped and looked toward the barn for a while. Then she went on.

---

That night Teasel came in late from the pool, smelling of the river, and lay down on the outside of the heap. Before hir lay down, hir put the egg in the straw by Haw's head, where it always went.

Some time after midnight Haw's lower hands came looking for warmth in hir sleep, as they did every night, and found Teasel's flank, and stayed there.
