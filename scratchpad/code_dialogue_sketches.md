# Sketches — rendering Kin-code banter in S017

Test lines (same two exchanges in every option):
 A. Vega taunting Sorrel after a bad throw.
 B. Sorrel and Sedge, idle, mid-game.

Note: ⟨ ⟩ is taken (whistle, DOC-00 §6). Kin-code needs its own mark.

---

## 1 — Strict canon. The floor, for reference.

*[Lovely]*
*[Tired / you try]*
*[Never]*

*[Not moving]*
*[Thinking / moving]*
*[Not same]*
*[Adjacent]*

Authentic, unreadable as banter. Kills the joke in B entirely.

---

## 2 — Words inside the canon bracket, affect riding after a dash.

*[Oh, that was lovely — glee, unkind, small]*
*[I'd like to see you do it tired — sour]*
*[I've never been tired — and means it]*

*[You're not moving — flat]*
*[I'm thinking about moving]*
*[That's not the same]*
*[It's adjacent — pleased with hirself]*

Reads fast. The rider does the work tone-of-voice does in speech.
Risk: every line wants a rider, and the page starts to look annotated.

---

## 3 — Affect first, as a key signature the line is played in.

*[warm]* Oh, that was lovely.
*[sour]* I'd like to see you do it tired.
*[flat, true]* I've never been tired.

*[fond]* You're not moving.
*[fond]* I'm thinking about moving.
*[fond]* That's not the same.
*[smug]* It's adjacent.

The repeated *[fond]* through B is the point — in code you can't drop it,
so a whole run of banter is stamped with the same warmth whether you like it or not.
Cheap to read. Slightly script-like.

---

## 4 — The compression shown: symbol count as the texture.

Two symbols: *lovely*, and the one that means it wasn't.
*I'd like to see you do it tired.*
One symbol. *Never.* With a shrug in it.

*Not moving,* Sorrel said.
*Thinking about moving.*
*Not the same thing.*
*Adjacent* — one symbol, and Sedge had invented it, and had been
trying to get it into general use for about a decade.

Best "there is more here than words." The invented-symbol gag is only
possible in this option. Cost: needs narrator space, slows the game down.

---

## 5 — Prose carries it. No new punctuation at all.

Oh, that was lovely, Vega said, in the two symbols that manage it,
the second one doing all the damage.

I'd like to see you do it tired.

I've never been tired — flat, no colour on it, and the horrible part
was that it came through true.

Not moving, Sorrel said.

Thinking about moving.

Not the same.

Adjacent, said Sedge, and let it sit there warm for a second longer than
a word would have, because code doesn't stop when the sentence does.

Most human on the page. Italics + no quote marks marks it as not-speech.
"Code doesn't stop when the sentence does" is the whole idea in one line.
Cost: dialogue-heavy passages get loose; hard to keep straight who's talking.

---

## 6 — Hybrid: mark the channel with a light bracket, let affect surface only when it matters.

‹Oh, that was lovely.›
‹I'd like to see you do it tired.›
‹I've never been tired.› — and no colour on it at all, which was the insult.

‹You're not moving.›
‹I'm thinking about moving.›
‹That's not the same.›
‹It's adjacent.›

Then, once, when it counts — the joke landing:

Ilex sent four symbols. Sorrel got the first three as words and the fourth
one wasn't a word, it was eleven years arriving at once.

Cheapest to run across a whole scene. Banter stays quick and clean,
and the extra channel is spent only where the scene needs it.
Cost: you have to earn "there is more to it" with narration, not typography.

---

## Cross-cutting bits worth stealing whichever you pick

- **The un-droppable channel.** Speech lets you hide tone. Code doesn't.
  Somebody says a mild thing and everyone gets exactly how much they meant it,
  and that's a running joke in a bay full of people trying to trash-talk.
- **Overlap.** Code has no turn-taking. Four people can answer at once and all
  four arrive. Worth one moment where the replies land on top of each other.
- **Speed.** A whole exchange fits inside a throw. "They had the entire argument
  between the bag leaving Sable's tail and hitting the wall."
- **Human speech as the marked case.** Ilex shouting the record out loud in
  human speech is now a *gesture* — the noisy, slow, deliberately wasteful way
  to say a thing. That beat gets better the more the rest is in code.

# Round 2 — two fresh lines through each option

X. The pile-on (comic, overlapping): "Leave hir alone." / "Hir was revolving."
Y. The last beat (warm, quiet): "You're old, Sorrel." / "I'm hot. And old."

## 1 — Strict canon

X  *[Alone / leave]*
   *[Revolving]*

Y  *[Old]*
   *[Hot]* … *[Old / also]*

Y collapses to nothing. The joke in Y is the delay, and symbols have no delay.

---

## 2 — Words in the bracket, rider after the dash

X  *[Leave hir alone — no weight on it, means none of it]*
   *[Hir was revolving — glee, four of them at once]*

Y  *[You're old, Sorrel — fond, uncushioned]*
   *[I'm hot]* … *[And old — unhurried]*

Rider handles the pile-on well (one tag covers four senders). In Y the
ellipsis does the work and the riders start to feel like stage notes.

---

## 3 — Affect first, as key signature

X  *[flat]* Leave hir alone.
   *[delight]* Hir was revolving.

Y  *[fond]* You're old, Sorrel.
   *[warm]* I'm hot. *[warm]* And old.

The doubled *[warm]* in Y is decent — hir can't drop it even to be dry.
But two tags in one short line is where this option starts to clutter.

---

## 4 — Symbol count as texture

X  Leave hir alone — one symbol, and not a committed one.
   Revolving, said Sable, and it was a new symbol, and it was going to be
   in general circulation by breakfast.

Y  You're old, Sorrel. Two symbols, neither of them softened.
   Hot, Sorrel sent. And then, a beat later, because hir could not be
   bothered to send them together: old.

Strongest of the six on both. X gets a real gag out of the symbol spreading;
Y gets the delay for free, because sending two symbols separately is a
visible choice. Costs a clause of narration each time.

---

## 5 — Prose carries it, italics, no quotes

X  *Leave hir alone,* Sorrel sent from the webbing, with no weight behind it,
   meaning none of it.
   *Hir was revolving,* said Sable, and three other people sent the same
   thing underneath, and they all arrived.

Y  *You're old, Sorrel.*
   *I'm hot,* Sorrel sent, ears out flat — and let the rest of it come a
   second later, unhurried. *And old.*

Reads the most like a person wrote it. X handles overlap better than any
other option ("they all arrived"). Y is clean. Attribution gets expensive
in a four-way.

---

## 6 — ‹ › channel, affect only when earned

X  ‹Leave hir alone.›
   ‹Hir was revolving.› — and three more underneath it, all at once,
   because code doesn't queue.

Y  ‹You're old, Sorrel.›
   ‹I'm hot.› And then, because in code you cannot take a thing back once
   it's gone, and hir didn't want to: ‹And old.›

Fast, quiet, stays out of the way. Y is the best version of that beat in
the whole set — the "can't take it back" note only costs one clause and it
lands on the thing the channel actually is.

# Round 3 — Option 2, developed

## The convention

*[words — what rides under them]*

The bracket is the channel, not the content. Inside it, either rendered words
(ordinary talk) or actual symbols (a Burst, a formal thing, poetry) — canon's
slash still separates symbols, so the rider gets the **em-dash** and nothing else.
Quotation marks stay reserved for human speech. ⟨ ⟩ stays whistle.

*[Warmth / Family / Genesis complete]*        ← unchanged canon, still works
*[Oh, that was lovely — glee, unkind, small]* ← the new everyday form

---

## The rule that makes it worth doing: FLAT COSTS EFFORT

You can lie in words. You cannot lie in the rider. It goes whether you want it
to or not, and stripping it — sending a line clean, with nothing under it — is
a skill, and a slightly hostile one.

So the absence of a rider is never neutral. It's a move.

*[I'm not tired]* — and it arrived with the tired all over it, because hir
could not be bothered to strip it, and everyone in the bay got both halves.

*[I've never been tired]* — and nothing under it. Nothing at all. Vega could
send a line clean the way other people could catch, and it was the single
most annoying thing about hir.

That is the whole characterisation axis: **Vega sends clean. Sorrel leaks.
Sedge doesn't try. Bramble can't yet.**

---

## What goes after the dash — six kinds

**1. Tone.** The commonest, cheapest.
*[You're not moving — flat]*
*[It's adjacent — and pleased with hirself]*

**2. Contradiction.** The best one. Words say a thing, rider says another, both arrive.
*[Good throw — and doesn't mean it]*
*[Leave hir alone — meaning none of it]*
*[I'm fine — and isn't, and knows everyone has it]*

**3. Count.** Who sent it, how many, all at once.
*[Carried — sixteen of them, on top of each other]*
*[Hir was revolving — four people, no order, none of them sorry]*

**4. No words at all.** Rider only. For things that don't render.
*[— pure delight, no content]*
*[— eleven years, arriving at once]*

**5. Duration.** Code doesn't stop when the sentence does.
*[And old — and let it stand there a second longer than a word would have]*

**6. Effort.** The strip, or the failure to.
*[I'm not tired — badly stripped]*
*[Name the rule — clean, which took work]*

---

## When to leave it off

Most lines. One rider per three or four, or the page turns into an annotated
score. Rule of thumb: put one where **speech would have carried it in the voice**
and nowhere else. If you'd have written "hir said, sourly", that's a rider.
If you'd have written "hir said", that's nothing.

## Edge cases

**Human speech in the same scene.** Stays in quotes and becomes the marked,
showy case — slow, loud, wasteful, and a gesture when someone chooses it.
Ilex shouting the record out loud gets better under this convention, not worse.

**The Core.** Stays italic, unbracketed, no rider — it has nothing to put there,
and the contrast is now visible on the page every time it speaks.

**Whistle.** ⟨ ⟩, unchanged, still the private channel. Worth one moment where
somebody switches to whistle *specifically because* the rider can't be stripped
and they don't want the bay to have it.

---

## Punctuation to choose between

*[Oh, that was lovely — glee, unkind, small]*   em-dash (recommended; slash is taken)
*[Oh, that was lovely · glee, unkind, small]*   middot — tighter, more clinical
*[Oh, that was lovely ~ glee, unkind, small]*   tilde — softer, reads as "carrying"
