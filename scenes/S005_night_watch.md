# SCENE 005 — Night Watch
**Origin:** Generated 2026-09-19 (side-agent, user-accepted) · **Theme:** What the Kin feel like to live with — the pause, the warmth, the vacuum work, the Reading · **POV:** Human (Danny), Artemis-9
**Style:** DOC-00 governs names and pronouns (human POV says *she*; Kin POV and documentary voice say *hir*).  
**Canon status:** ACCEPTED AS WRITTEN — drift/consistency notes below.
**Introduces:** Sorrel (Kin, coolant-bay watch, Artemis-9); Danny (human, new crew); Reyes (human, maintenance); a Belt supply tug; "the first spire over Luna" as a remembered historical event.

---

I'd been on Artemis-9 eleven days before I understood that Sorrel wasn't ignoring me.

She'd sit on the console ledge in the coolant bay — that's how they sit, the whole lower body folded under like a cat that's decided the furniture is beneath it, the two upper arms resting loose on her chest — and I'd ask a question and there'd be this *pause*. Half a second. Long enough to feel rude. Then the answer, slow and careful, every word placed like she was setting down something fragile.

I complained about it to Reyes in the mess. He laughed. "She's not slow, Danny. She's *waiting for you.* Everything you say, she's already heard the end of before you're halfway through, and she's had a whole conversation with her Cluster about what to say back, and now she's sitting there holding the answer until your mouth stops moving. That pause is her being polite."

That changed how the pause felt. It didn't make it less lonely.

---

The thing nobody tells you is how *warm* they are. Not metaphorically — physically. Sorrel runs hot, and the fur is dense enough that when she brushes past you in a corridor it's like being touched by a heated blanket. And they touch constantly. Each other, I mean. I'd come into the bay and find three of them tangled on the webbing, tails wound together, not talking, not doing anything I could see, ears swivelled at each other like satellite dishes. Reyes says that's a conversation. Says they're probably arguing.

They're also, and I say this with respect, ridiculous to look at. Somebody made them cute on purpose. The ears are enormous. The eyes are too big for the face. When Sorrel is thinking hard the ears flare out and the whole effect is a fox that's just been startled by a good idea. You want to pick her up. She weighs fifty kilos and could break my arm without noticing, and you still want to pick her up. I asked her once if that bothered her — being designed to be liked. The pause was longer than usual.

"Yes," she said. Then, after a while: "And I like you. Both are true. Your ancestors were not subtle, but they were not wrong."

---

What she's *for* — what she's actually doing on a station full of humans who could in theory run it themselves — became clear the night the number-four thruster bay lost pressure.

I heard the alarm. By the time I got my helmet seals checked, Sorrel had been in the bay for four minutes. In vacuum. No suit. I watched her on the feed: eyes filmed over silver, moving along the rails on four paws with a tool in each hand, fast, precise, not a wasted motion, and a tiny detail I couldn't stop looking at — her ears were folded flat against her skull, and every ninety seconds or so they'd flick open, fan wide for a moment, and fold again. Reyes told me later she was dumping heat. Managing her own thermals by hand, the way I'd manage a throttle.

She had the seal patched in fourteen minutes and was back through the lock before I'd finished suiting up. I'd expected her to be — I don't know. Triumphant. Instead she was subdued, and she went straight to the others and they closed around her and did the tail thing and nobody said a word for an hour.

I asked her about it the next day.

"I had seventeen minutes," she said. "I used fourteen. The Core kept telling me the number." A twitch of the ears. "It is a very good assistant. It is not comforting."

---

Here's the thing that finally made me understand them, though, and it wasn't the vacuum work.

An old one died. Not on our station — on a supply tug that came in from the Belt. Something structural, quick, the kind of death that would have been a bad day for a human crew and a funeral the next week. Instead, before the tug had finished docking, every Kin on Artemis-9 had gone quiet. Not sad-quiet. *Listening*-quiet. Ears all turned the same direction.

They brought the body into the Kin sector and I wasn't supposed to be there but nobody stopped me. They laid her on the webbing, and one at a time — I counted eleven — they lay down on top of her. Chest to chest. Ears pressed to ears. Thirty, forty minutes each, and then they'd get up shaking and cold and another would take their place, all night, taking turns keeping a dead woman warm.

Sorrel was the fourth. When she got up her fur was matted and her hands were trembling and her eyes were somewhere else entirely.

"What did you get?" I asked. Stupid question. I didn't know what else to say.

"Her third year," Sorrel said. "A little of it. She saw the first spire go up over Luna. I have that now. I will carry it." And then, in the most human voice I'd ever heard from her: "She should have told us sooner. There was so much she didn't tell us."

I understood, then, why the Kin don't write anything down. And I understood why, when Reyes and I do our maintenance logs — the long, boring, tedious human logs — Sorrel sometimes sits on the console ledge and watches us do it with an expression I'd have called envy, if I'd known her less.

---

She still pauses before she answers me. I've stopped minding.

Last week I asked her what it was like, hearing us talk. I expected the usual — the straw, the ocean, the tolerant joke.

The pause was very long.

"Like watching someone carve a word into stone," she said. "Slow. And then it *stays*."
