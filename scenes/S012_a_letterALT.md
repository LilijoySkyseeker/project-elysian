# SCENE 012 — A Letter (v2)
**Origin:** Fresh-context rewrite, 2026-09-19 · **Form:** Letter · **POV:** Human (Teodor Lind)
**Canon status:** DRAFT v2 — awaiting review. Introduces no canon.

Marit —

Your mother says you've signed for the Belt. Good. She says the pay is a scandal and I said that's what the Belt is for. She also says you rolled your eyes when she told you I'd be writing. Roll them. It's paper because the terminal in this room hasn't worked since her last visit and I've stopped asking. The doctor here is about twelve and calls me young man, so I'm not asking him either.

You'll be posted with Kin. Everybody knows that, I'm only putting it down so you know why I'm going to write about Alder, which I don't do. Six years on *Elysium-4*, the axis crews, the long shafts, you've heard all that till you were sick of it. Alder was shaft watch with the Bay Two lot and she was beside me the whole six, and I've had sixty years since to get her into a shape I could hand somebody and never managed it. So this will be a bad shape. Your mother can read you the difficult words.

One thing before I forget, because I will. Take a clothes brush. A real one, bristles. Your mother will tell you the kit's self-cleaning and the kit is self-cleaning. Take one anyway. You'll see.

Alder-3 on the roster. I never once heard anybody say the three out loud, and I knew men who'd have said it just to see what happened. She could play cards with two hands and hold the pump manual open with the other two, and I never proved she wasn't keeping a second pack somewhere in the rest. I lost eleven weeks' chocolate to her the first year. What she did with it I never found out. After that I stopped playing for chocolate and lost for nothing, which is how I'd have you do it. That's not the advice. The brush is the advice.

Your mother writes hir on her forms. I said she from the first day out of habit, and Alder answered to it, and about year five I asked her whether it bothered her. She took her time and said it was fine from where I was standing. I didn't know what to do with that so I kept saying she. I'm too old to change now and she'd have said so herself.

The number-two pump on the loop was never right. I want that on a record somewhere and this is the only record I've got. Wen and I rebuilt it twice. Alder rebuilt it once on her own in a shift and a half and it was still never right, and the day I left she told me it was still never right, and I think that was the nearest she came to a going-away present.

Old Wen died in his bunk my fourth year. Heart. The chaplain put Alder and three of hers at the back of the service and they stood there the whole hour like a pallet nobody had signed for. I never saw anything so useless and I include myself. That night I went down to Bay Two for something, I forget what, and the whole lot of them were awake on the webbing in the dark, not moving, and Alder said they were doing Wen. That's how she put it. Doing Wen. I sat down on the deck and nobody told me to go and I stayed till the shift bell. I've thought about that night more than I've thought about my own wedding, and your grandmother knew it, and forgave me, mostly.

The year before I left, before the outer truss job, she sat with each of hers in turn, chest to chest, a day at a time, and I brought the food down and sat at the edge of the Nest and heard nothing at all. I asked her after what it was. She said you tell before you go. They all say it, she said. I said go where, it's only the truss, and she looked at me the way she did.

Now the sum. I did a sum on the back of your mother's last letter and I'm not going to show you my working, you were always better at sums than me. Alder was old when I knew her. Not old to look at. Old in the way she'd talk about people I'd only read about. I worked out roughly what she'd be now and I didn't like the number, and I'm not going to tell you why I didn't like it, because you'll get it from your own Kin one day and it's better coming from them.

If you find her — she'll be somewhere, they know where each other are, ask any of them — she'll know who I am. I want to be honest with you, that's not the same as remembering me and I know the difference. Say Teo Lind sends his regards. Say the number-two pump was never right. If she doesn't laugh that's all right, she may not have the laugh any more, but she'll have the pump. Ask her if she still has the strangers off the dead ship. She'd taken some of them on a few years before I came and I couldn't make head or tail of it then. I think I'd understand it now.

And then wait. She takes her time answering, she always did. I used to think she was deciding whether I was worth the bother. I've had sixty years to decide I was, or she was, one of the two.

I'm not going to say the other thing. You know it. Your mother knows it. Alder knew it about the third week and never said it either, so I'm in good company.

Did I say about the pump. I did. Well. It was never right.

Take the brush. And consider yourself told.

Grandad
