# SCENE 006 — The Flat Country
**Origin:** Forked side-agent 2026-09-19 · **Theme:** A void-built Kin's first days under gravity — body language, small errors, what they feel like to be around · **POV:** Human (Inés Rojo, ground crew), Paranal Range Station, Atacama, Earth
**Style:** DOC-00 governs names and pronouns (human POV says *she*; Kin POV and documentary voice say *hir*).  
**Canon status:** ACCEPTED AS A STORY (2026-09-19). **Does not introduce canon** — the Atacama post, the split-launch pairing and any names here are one-off texture, not world facts, until deliberately adopted. The six-week ground-build retune is confirmed consistent with DOC-01A (muscle-led).
**Introduces:** Ashe-2 (Kin, orbital rigger, first planetary posting); Inés Rojo (human, range tech); Padraig (human, kitchen); Paranal Range Station (Earth surface, launch-support post); Elysium-4 as Ashe's home spire.

---

The first thing Ashe did on Earth was fall over.

Not dramatically. The lander ramp came down, the air of the Atacama rolled in — dry, hot, smelling of dust and rocket — and Ashe took one step onto it on four paws the way you'd step onto a rail, and the ramp did not come up to meet her, and she folded. Slowly. Like a table with one leg shorter than the others deciding which way to go. She caught herself on both hands, which I later learned is exactly the wrong thing for her kind to do, and stayed there a moment with her ears out flat to the sides, and then she looked up at me and said, "That is not what I asked for."

I didn't know what she meant. I said, "Welcome to the flat country," which is what we say, and she said, "Yes," in a way that suggested she'd already worked that out.

---

She was here for eight weeks to learn our pad and teach us hers — the spire she came from does the orbital half of the same launches we do the ground half of. That was the job. The job was fine. The job is not what I remember.

I remember the stairs.

There are eleven steps from the pad apron up to the control bunker, and Ashe took them the first time the way I'd take a ladder made for someone else: front paws on one step, back paws two steps below, hands on the rail, tail dragging behind her like a rope she'd forgotten she was carrying. Halfway up she stopped. Her ears came forward, slow, both together. I thought she was frustrated. Then she said, "I asked my Core. It says the fall from here would not hurt me," and looked at the drop — three, maybe four metres to the apron — with an expression I could only call *interest*.

"It would," I said. "It would absolutely hurt you."

The pause. They always pause; I'd been briefed. But the briefing said it was politeness, waiting for you to finish, and this one wasn't that. This one was her asking someone else.

"It says you are right," she said, "in this body." And she came the rest of the way up like someone carrying eggs.

---

Things about her that took me a week to stop noticing:

She was warm. When she sat beside you at the console — and she sat *beside* you, shoulder to your hip, no gap, as though the chair had been designed for two — you felt it through your sleeve. She didn't seem to know she was doing it. The second day Padraig, who runs the kitchen, stepped around her in the corridor and she stepped the same way, and he stepped back, and she stepped back, and they did the whole dance, and at the end she put a hand flat on his chest to stop him and went past. He came into the control room afterwards looking like he'd been blessed.

Her tail got into everything. Under the door. Around the chair leg. Around my ankle, once, for most of an afternoon, which I only discovered when I got up and she came with me. She apologised. Then she did it again the next day. I stopped mentioning it.

She hated the kitchen. Not the food — the *kitchen*. She'd stand at the door with her nose working and her ears down and say, "Your kitchen is very loud," and Padraig, who is proud of his kitchen, took it personally until I explained. After that he ran the extractor fan for her before she came in, and she'd stand in the doorway and let the air clear and say, "Thank you," like he'd turned off a siren.

She couldn't sit on anything. Chairs were the wrong shape; the counters were the wrong height; the sofa in the crew lounge she simply climbed onto and folded herself on top of, all six limbs under her, the way a cat sits on a book. She looked at our monitors with her head tilted and asked why they flickered. They don't flicker. I told her they don't flicker. She said, "Yes," in that way again.

---

The sun was the hard part.

Inside the bunker she was fine. Outside, the Atacama at noon, she'd stop at the door like a swimmer at cold water, and something would slide across both her eyes — a film, gold, like a coin held over each — and then she'd go out, and her ears would go wide and stay wide, fanned out like she was straining to hear something enormous and far away.

I thought that was wonder. I told her so, the second week: that I liked how she looked at the desert with her ears up.

"I am hot," she said.

Oh.

"The ears are how I am not hotter." A pause, but a short one; the kind I'd started to recognise as her being kind. "It is also a very good desert."

---

She got stronger, over the weeks. Not in any way I'd have called training — she never trained, she just *did* things, and her body caught up with her. By the fourth week she'd stopped using her hands on the stairs. By the sixth she came down the bunker steps in two bounds and hit the apron at a run and went across it faster than I'd ever seen anything on four legs go, and stopped, and stood there with her sides going and her tail straight out behind her, and then came trotting back looking pleased with herself and slightly betrayed.

"That was expensive," she said.

"You looked great."

"I will not be able to do that on the rails for a month." She said it the way you'd mention you'd spent the rent. Then, after a moment: "It was worth it."

---

The night before she left, I found her outside.

We have a rule about the pad apron after dark — no one out there without a reason — but the rule had never had to account for someone who could see in the dark and didn't need a torch. She was lying on her back on the concrete, all six limbs loose, tail out flat, ears spread. Just looking up.

I lay down next to her because it seemed rude not to.

The sky out there is the reason the observatories came. There is nothing between you and it. No ceiling, no hull, no ring of a station's far side turning overhead. Just the whole depth of it, all the way down.

"There is nothing above me," she said, after a while. "I asked my Core about the pressure. It said the pressure is fine. I asked it again. It said the same." The gold films weren't over her eyes; they were open all the way, and wet. "It has said so eleven times. It is a very good assistant. It does not understand the question."

I didn't say anything. I'm not sure there was anything.

"It is very beautiful," Ashe said. "It is very flat. I would like to go home."

She put her hand on my arm, the way she'd put it on Padraig's chest, and left it there, and we stayed like that, the warm weight of her against my side, until the cold came up out of the concrete and the sky went grey at the edge and her lander was a light coming down out of it.

I still lie out there sometimes. It doesn't feel flat to me. But I know what she meant.
