Forty minutes in, Sorrel's tail had gone past sore and into stupid. The root of it had stopped answering on time — every throw came off a beat late and went a metre wide of wherever she had aimed it, and there was nothing to be done about that except keep throwing and hope the arm-end of the night arrived before the rest of she did.

The bag came down the bay at her shoulder-height, tumbling, water sloshing around inside it in that lazy way it got when nobody had hit it properly in a while.

*Two point four metres right of you,* the Core said, which was a lie, though not a deliberate one, and Sorrel was already swinging for where the bag was actually going to be, which was into her face.

She got a coil round it, absorbed most of nine kilos through a tail that did not want the job, and slung it back up-bay toward Sable without looking. It went about four metres and died in the air.

*[Oh, that was lovely ~ small, unkind, delighted]*

*[I'd like to see you do it tired]* — and the tired went with it, unstripped, because it was already all over Sorrel and there was no getting it off.

*[I've never been tired]*

Nothing under it. Not a thing. Sending a line clean is work, and most people leak, and Vega had not leaked since somewhere around the four-hundred-and-fiftieth year.

Which was of a piece with the rest of her. Vega-2 was sixty years into a career of being exactly good enough, in the specific way that made her infuriating: never spectacular, never once out of position. She had a flip she had been running at the same speed since before Sorrel came to the station, and she won with it, over and over, in games that were never close and never interesting, and she was two points from doing it again.

Ten to eight. First to eleven. Sorrel's team had not led since the fourth point.

---

Bay Nine at night was forty metres of nothing with a docking ring at each end and a coolant trunk running down the port side that you could hear breathing if you shut up long enough to listen. The only light in the place was the standby strip over the hatch, and that was there because the safety schedule said it had to be, and the schedule had been written by somebody who had never met anyone who lived here.

There were two of the hull crew up at the gallery port with a low-light feed, which lagged, so that every so often a quarter-second after something happened there would be a muffled human noise through the glass at the wrong moment. Nobody minded. It had been going on for centuries and it was faintly companionable, like weather.

Sedge-6 hung off the near rail with her ears fanned out flat, breathing like a pump.

*[You're not moving]*

*[I'm thinking about moving]*

*[That's not the same]*

*[It's adjacent ~ pleased with herself]*

Sedge had spent forty years of her life putting this station in tune — bearings matched, pumps phased, one memorable eight-month argument with a human engineer over a compressor on deck nine — and the reason you could run flat out through a black bay and not put your head into a bulkhead was substantially Sedge's doing, and she could not catch a bag to save her life. It was the funniest thing about her and she knew it and traded on it shamelessly.

The axis Cluster took the restart, worked it down the port side in three passes, and scored. Eleven to eight would have ended it. It went ten to nine because Sable got a paw to it on the line, which cost the point — hands are for rails, that's all, that's the entire law of the game — but cost *them* the score too, and was probably worth it, and Sable came off the wall to a good deal of abuse anyway.

Sorrel got a cheap one back off a rebound nobody was watching. Ten to nine.

Then Ilex did the thing she had been saving for eleven years.

---

Sorrel did not see it coming, because nobody ever sees one coming; that's the form. Vega had the bag, clean, off the ceiling rail, with the angle and the speed and the whole bay already resigned to it.

Ilex, four metres under and completely out of the play, sent three symbols.

*[Deck nine / the compressor ~ eleven years]*

The frame lit up. Sorrel felt it in her chest before she understood any part of it — that specific whump of sixteen people getting a thing at once — and then she understood it, and then she was no use to anybody either.

Vega folded up in the air. Simply came apart, eleven metres off the deck, tail curled into her own belly, shaking, gone. The bag sailed past her shoulder and hit the bulkhead with a wet slap and hung there against the rail like something that had died in its sleep.

It had started in the four-hundred-and-ninety-ninth year. Ilex had said a thing about Sedge's compressor and had then said nothing further about it for eleven years, feeding it maybe twice a season, letting it go quiet for whole decades' worth of Tuesdays, and had spent the entire structure tonight, in a nine-a-side bay game, on one point.

Sable had to be physically held on to a rail. Sedge laughed so hard she lost the rail entirely and had to be gone and fetched from the middle of the bay, still laughing, which somehow made it worse. Up at the gallery the humans got a shape of it a quarter-second late and started laughing too, at nothing, at sixteen foxes helpless in the dark, which was fair.

Vega was still curled up when Kirin put the bag through the ring.

Ten all.

*[That's cheating]* — eventually, from somewhere inside a ball of Vega.

*[Name the rule]* — clean, nothing under it at all, and Ilex had unquestionably worked on that.

*[I'll find one]*

*[Take eleven years]*

---

Golden point. The bay went quiet in that way it does — no code, no whistle, just everyone hanging off their rails with their ears one direction — and Sorrel realised she was too tired to want it very much, which was a new and uncomfortable feeling and she put it away to look at some other time.

Bramble had said nothing all night.

Fourteen years old. No number, and wouldn't have one for another year. Eleven kilos heavier through the shoulders than Sorrel and not finished growing sideways yet, and in every corridor on the station the largest thing in it that nobody would let near an airlock — which she had been told twice that week, once by Sorrel, in the flat voice you use so it doesn't turn into a conversation. She had not managed to strip that one either. She was fourteen. Nobody can, at fourteen.

In the bay the answer was yes. The bay was the only place the answer was ever yes, and she had been working on one single thing in it for two years, in the dead hours, alone, which Sorrel had known about in the vague way you know a young person is up to something and had not thought about twice.

The restart went long and ugly, the bag tumbling with the water still all over the place, and Bramble came off the ceiling rail.

She did not go where the bag was. She went past it, out into open bay with nothing to hold, which is how you lose the point and look an idiot doing it — and then the water in the bag finished running to one end, and for about a fifth of a second the thing was honest and solid and exactly as heavy as it looked, and Bramble was already there, taking it on the tail root, turning inside her own length.

Four tenths and a bit. Sorrel felt the number land in the frame the way you feel a door shut in a house.

It wasn't fast. Fast is not interesting; half the bay was faster. It was *new*, and there were sixteen people in the room who had all, at some point in their lives, spent a year of dead hours trying to teach their own Core one move, and every single one of them knew the difference in the same tenth of a second.

The bag went through the ring hard enough to ring it.

*[Carried]* *[Carried]* *[I have it]* *[Carried ~ sixteen of them, on top of each other, no order, nobody waiting]* — and Ilex shouting it out loud on top of all that, in human speech, at nothing, which took four times as long and was the entire point of doing it.

Bramble hung off the far rail with her ears pinned flat back against her skull, absolutely mortified, so pleased she couldn't hold still.

---

It took eleven minutes for somebody to send *[do it again]*, and it was Vega, who had lost, and who wanted to see it properly.

Bramble could not do it again.

She tried it eleven times. Eleven. The first four were close and the next seven were worse than the first four, because by then she was thinking about it, and you cannot think about a thing your Core is supposed to be doing for you. The bag went into the port wall. The bag went into Sedge. The bag went nowhere at all, twice, and the second time Bramble misjudged the push entirely and ended up drifting slowly across the middle of the bay with nothing within reach of any of six limbs, revolving, at fourteen, in front of everyone she knew.

The noise this produced was the loudest thing that happened all night.

*[Leave her alone ~ meaning none of it]*

*[Nobody is touching her]*

*[You're all being awful]*

*[She was revolving ~ four of them, simultaneously, unrepentant]*

Sorrel lay back with her tail hanging off the edge of the webbing where it could stop being a limb for a while, and listened to her Cluster being awful to a child who was going to hold that record for a hundred years and who would, in the end, mostly remember tonight as the night she got stuck in the middle of Bay Nine, and thought: I am not going to tell her which of those she will keep.

Then she thought: I am going to be unable to lift this tomorrow.

Vega drifted over, upside down relative to nothing, and hung there.

*[Second and fourth]*

*[Second and fourth]*

*[You're old, Sorrel ~ fond, and no cushion on it at all]*

*[I'm hot]* — ears out flat — and then, a second later, making no attempt at all to strip it: *[And old]*

Up at the gallery the humans had run out of game to watch and were leaving, and one of them raised a hand at the port on the way out, and Sorrel lifted a hind paw off the webbing at her, which was the nearest thing she had free.
