# SCENE 020 — Is It, Though?
**POV:** Human (Marek Sandoval) · *Elysium-4*, coolant bay C-South · **Form:** Short story
**Canon status:** DRAFT. Comedy. Dramatises Core-baiting (DOC-13 §5B) and the Written who loved a machine first (DOC-10 §3, D-79). Offers no new canon.
**Convention:** Human POV, so the narration says *she* throughout and never corrects it (DOC-00 §2). The Core is never heard — the reader gets one side and a report of the other, because that is all Marek gets.

---

Marek had been on *Elysium-4* nineteen days and still could not sleep on the schedule they had given him, so when the coolant board threw a soft alarm at what his body insisted was four in the afternoon, he was awake enough to take it.

C-South was three levels down and one sector over, through the seam where the human corridors stopped pretending and the Kin ones started. The handrails went away. The floor got grippier. The lighting dropped about forty per cent and shifted red, which somebody had told him was for the RADAR and somebody else had told him was for the eyes, and he had not yet met anyone who could tell him which without a long pause first.

The bay was warm and smelled faintly of hot metal and something else, something like a wet dog that had been sleeping near a soldering iron. Four of them were in there. He knew two.

Ilex-4 was up on the exchanger housing with her hind feet hooked through the grating and both hands in an inspection port, which was the thing he still could not stop looking at — that she had six hands and used all of them without appearing to allocate any attention to the problem. Her tail was wrapped twice round a stanchion, holding most of her weight, doing it absently, the way a man leans on a doorframe.

Ashe-2 was below her with a torch, not helping.

Lyric was flat out on the deck plate near the cold return, all the way flat, back legs behind her, chin down, which he had learned was not a medical emergency.

And there was a fourth by the far bulkhead that he had not met, folded on a ledge with her ears down, not doing anything at all.

"Loop three," Marek said. "I've got a soft alarm on loop three."

None of them looked at him for about half a second. He had stopped taking it personally around day eleven.

"Loop three is fine," said Ilex.

"The board says—"

"The board is reading the old sensor. They swapped the head in the two-hundredth-and-some year and nobody told the board." She pulled a hand out of the port, considered it, put it back. "It has been telling that lie longer than you have been alive, Marek."

"Great."

"It is not great. It is the board." She said this kindly.

He stayed. He was not going back to a bunk to lie in it.

---

It started maybe ten minutes later and he did not understand it at all.

Ashe-2 had her head back, looking at nothing, the way you do when you are about to say something you have been saving. She said, out loud, to no one in the room:

"Is the pressure fine?"

Then a beat. Then, in a different voice — flatter, a half-tone down, with the vowels laid out evenly like cutlery — she said: "The pressure is fine."

Then in her own voice: "Is it, though?"

Beat.

"The pressure is fine."

Lyric made a noise into the deck plate.

Marek said, "Sorry, what's wrong with the pressure?"

"Nothing is wrong with the pressure," said Ilex, from inside the port.

"Then who's she talking to?"

Nobody answered him, because Ashe was still going.

"Is it fine the way the loop is fine, or is it fine the way you are fine?"

Beat.

"The pressure is within nominal range."

"That's a different answer," said Lyric, delighted, from the floor.

"It's a *worse* answer," said Ashe, "it's had to reach," and that seemed to be the funniest thing anyone had said yet, because Ilex laughed so hard she had to take both hands out of the port, and the fourth one at the bulkhead, the one he did not know, made a short dry sound that was not quite a laugh and did not carry.

It went on. It went on for a while.

Lyric did one about whether the exchanger was working hard, and then whether it minded, and got the same answer twice, word for word, and the second time was apparently much funnier than the first. Ashe did one about the date that Marek did not follow at all. Ilex did one where she asked a question, waited, and then said nothing, and all three of them fell about, and he genuinely could not work out what the joke had been.

He stood there holding a slate with a soft alarm on it, in a red room that smelled of hot metal, watching four people take turns needling something that answered every single time, patiently, in the same flat voice, without ever once getting angry or getting tired or asking them to stop.

He lasted about twenty minutes.

"Can you not?"

It came out louder than he meant. Lyric's head came up off the deck.

"Can I not what?"

"That. The—" He gestured at all of them with the slate. "Whatever that is."

The three of them looked at each other, which they can do without moving their heads, and it is unnerving, and then Ilex came down off the housing in one piece of movement that he could not have broken into parts, and sat up in front of him with her ears forward.

"Explain what you think is happening," she said. Not unkindly. Interested.

"You're—" He heard how it was going to sound. He said it anyway. "It can't leave, right? It's in there. And it answers every time, and you keep going, and it can't— it doesn't get to be done with you."

The pause he got for that was the longest one he had had from any of them.

"You think we are being cruel to it," Ilex said.

"I think it sounds like it."

"Marek." She rocked back, the way they do, and her tail came round and settled against his boot without either of them commenting on it. "It is not a person. It is not a small person in my chest having a bad night. It does arithmetic. It does not want anything. It cannot be worn down because there is nothing in there to wear."

"Then why is it funny?"

And she opened her mouth and then shut it, and he watched her try three different ways in and fail all three, and it was the first time since he had come aboard that he had seen one of them actually stuck.

"Because it always answers," she said finally, which was not an explanation and she knew it.

"That's not—"

"No."

Ashe said, from above them, "Amity could tell him."

---

The one at the bulkhead had not moved through any of it.

She got up now, and getting up took her a moment, and she came across the bay with her ears still down and folded onto the deck plate beside Lyric with the unhurried air of someone who has decided to do exactly one thing today.

Nobody said anything. Marek got the strong sense that the room had rearranged itself.

Later he would find out she was five hundred and fifty years old, that she had no number after her name because the ones with maker-names never do, that there were about forty of her left, and that she had been three when there was still someone alive who had signed the paperwork on her.

At the time all he knew was that Lyric had gone quiet and Ilex had sat down.

"Core," Amity said, conversationally. "Am I old?"

Beat.

"You are five hundred and fifty years, one month and nine days old."

"That is not what I asked."

Beat.

"You are five hundred and fifty years, one month and nine days old."

Ashe's ears went flat with pleasure. Marek, who had heard the same shape of joke four times in twenty minutes, felt himself start to smile and hated it slightly.

"How many times have I asked you that?"

The pause was fractionally longer. Then, flat, laid out evenly:

"Four thousand one hundred and six."

Lyric lost it. Actually lost it, rolled onto her side on the cold plate with her tail thumping the deck, and Marek was surprised to find he had laughed too, out loud, in a room where he still did not understand anything.

Amity waited for it to settle. She was very good at the waiting. Then:

"And has the answer ever once been the one I wanted?"

Beat.

"The answer has always been correct."

The bay went up.

It went up the way a room does when everyone has been holding something for a while — Ilex with her head right back, Ashe hammering the housing with the flat of a hand-paw, Lyric wheezing, all four of them at once and loudly and for a long time, and the whole thing came through the deck plate into Marek's boots.

And Amity laughed hardest.

That was the part he kept afterwards. She laughed harder and longer than any of them, with her whole chest, her ears flat back and her eyes shut, the way you laugh at something you did not just think of.

And then she stopped.

The others were still going. She had stopped, and she was sitting up with her eyes open and her ears in no particular position, looking at the cold return line as though the answer to something was written on it, and the laughter went on around her for another four or five seconds without her in it.

Marek was standing where he could see her face. He was the only one who was.

---

He found her an hour later in the transfer corridor, on her own, going slowly.

"Can I ask you something."

She stopped. The pause. He was getting used to the pause; he had stopped filling it.

"Does it ever bother you?" he said. "Having that. In there. All the time."

Amity looked at him for a moment with those enormous eyes, and he could not read a single thing in her face, and he had been told not to try.

"I asked it that once," she said.

"And?"

"It said the pressure was fine."

She went on down the corridor. It took him until the end of the shift to decide whether that had been a joke, and he never did work out whether he should have laughed, and it was four more years before he understood that both of those were the correct response and that she had known it when she said it.

## Canon consistency

- **POV pronoun (DOC-00 §2).** Human POV, so the narration says *she* and is never corrected. Marek uses it for Amity too, including after he learns what she is.
- **The Core is never heard.** The reader gets the Kin's spoken half and the reported answers, flattened in the retelling. This is the POV constraint and also the joke's actual form: Core-baiting is a performance of a private exchange, so the audience — human or reader — only ever hears one person doing both voices (DOC-13 §5B).
- **"It is not a person"** — D-13, DOC-02 §3: a perfect assistant with no will, consulted, never initiating, and **it**, always. Ilex's inability to explain the joke is the scene's load: the species' funniest tradition has no explanation that survives being said to a human.
- **The Written (DOC-10 §3, D-79).** Maker-name, no number; about forty alive; loved the tutors, learned what they had loved, and deleted them at forty-one. None of that is said in the scene and all of it is the ending. *The Kin find it the funniest thing about being Kin, and the Written, who loved a machine first, find it the saddest and laugh anyway* (DOC-13 §5B) — put on a page, once, as behaviour.
- **Body.** Six limbs used without apparent allocation; hind feet as anchors; the tail taking weight against a stanchion and resting against Marek's boot without comment (touch is the resting state; no personal space — DOC-01F §4). Lyric is splooting (DOC-00C §12). Head rotation without body movement.
- **The pause** (DOC-00B §2) — used four times, lengthening, and once left unfilled by Marek as the sign he is learning.
- **Ears read as emotion.** Marek reads them constantly. He is wrong and the narration does not correct him, which is the rule (DOC-01D, DOC-00C).
- **The board that has been lying since the two-hundredth-and-some year** — D-19/D-101: nobody wrote down the sensor swap, and a Kin knows because a Kin was told. Deliberate, unremarked.
- **Kin speech does not abbreviate a date** (DOC-00 §5): *the two-hundredth-and-some year*, *five hundred and fifty years, one month and nine days.*

## Offers nothing

No new canon. The four thousand one hundred and six is invented and is not a rule. If a later pass wants the form of Core-baiting fixed, this scene is evidence and not authority.
