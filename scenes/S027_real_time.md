# SCENE 027 — Real Time
**POV:** Kin (Aster-5) · the spire, Kin sector, the Nest's back bay · **Form:** Short story
**Canon status:** DRAFT. Dramatises a **Telling from the teller's side** (DOC-11 §4, D-102, D-103), **the account asked for** and **the cheap check** (D-103), the rolling window (D-104), coarse affect at range (D-92) and humans as the great un-carriable (DOC-11 §1, §11). Offers no new canon.
**Convention:** Kin POV throughout — *hir*. The Core is *it*, bare italic. Kin-code in *[words ~ rider]* per DOC-00D. Human speech in quotes. Inside a lived hour the dialogue conventions stop (DOC-00D §7).

---

Choosing which hours is the rite.

Aster had heard that said at every Telling hir had ever sat through, and hir had carried four, and hir had believed it the way you believe a thing seen from the outside. Someone else's chest going up and down against yours for a week; every so often the rhythm of it changing as they went down into an hour and lived it again, and came back up a little further away than before. *So that is the rite,* hir had thought. *The choosing.* It had looked like the easy part.

Hir had chosen a month ago. Nothing is written, so the list was nowhere but in hir, and hir had gone over it often enough that it had the smoothness of a thing carried in a pocket.

Seven hours, out of a year.

The catch in the spring, on the ribbon, when Tamar's line let go at the cradle and Aster had hir by the tail with a metre and a half of tether to spare and five Kin shouting in code at once. The night after, all eight of them in a heap in the Nest, so warm that the sector's thermal alarm went off and nobody got up to see to it. Sunrise on the terminator — the one hour of the year hir had seen colour out there, the film down over hir eyes and the ribbon going gold all the way to the vanishing point. The Written who came through in the autumn and spoke last at the small Weave, and the room going quiet the way it does. The argument with Sleet about the cradle bearings, which Aster had won, and which hir wanted on file in somebody else as having been won. Two more, smaller, that hir was rather proud of having thought to include.

It was a good year. Hir wanted it to arrive in someone as a good year.

Rue had asked for it. The young do; they have room, and a year out of a Kin of two hundred and forty is a thing you can be seen to be carrying. Rue was sixteen, a year past hir first Telling, still on hir Nest-name, and still unable to send a line without everything hir felt riding on the back of it — and what had ridden on the back of the asking was, mostly, *the catch*. Everybody on the spire had heard about the catch. Rue wanted to have been there.

Aster did not mind that. Hir had wanted to be asked for the catch.

The cradle job was ninety days, three hundred and ten kilometres down the ribbon: five Kin re-seating a climber cradle on a live line. The last crew to do it had come back one short. So — *tell before you go.*

---

They took the low ledge at the back of the Nest, where the coolant run went under the webbing and kept it warm. Rue folded onto hir chest to chest, arms under Aster's, and hir tail found Aster's and went once round it without either of them deciding, and the near-field opened, and the year began to go across.

That part is nothing. That part is the channel. A year goes at the rate a year goes, which is a week, near enough, of the cold stream running out of one body and into another — every word exact, every reading, every smell, first-person and flat as a cargo manifest. Aster did not have to do anything for it but lie there and be warm. Rue, who had been flattened by hir own first Telling and had come to this one braced, kept being surprised that it was only the spring arriving in order.

The catch came on the second morning.

Hir let the stream run up to the edge of it — the cradle, the crew strung out along the line, Tamar two metres below hir with a bolt driver — and then hir went down.

There is no other way to say it. The rate drops, and the affect comes on, and there is no longer a year going past; there is a morning, and hir is in it. The line went. It went the way it had gone in the spring, with a sound through hir hind paws that hir body knew before hir did, and hir body did everything again at the speed it had done it — the lunge, the tail out, the catch, Tamar's whole weight arriving through hir forepaws and the grip locking round it like a bird's round a branch — and the fear arrived at the speed fear arrives, which is after. Then the long part, which nobody tells: an hour on the cradle with Tamar held against hir chest, neither of them letting go, Sleet coming down the line hand over hand, the Core giving the load on the tether because hir had asked and not because it helped.

Rue's whole body went tight against hirs at the moment of the catch and did not come loose for most of the hour. That is the thing about the teller's side that nobody had told hir: *you feel them get it.*

Hir came back up.

*One hour, six minutes, at real time,* the Core said, when asked.

Hir felt it the way you feel a climb in your legs the next day. Not pain. Less of something.

Rue sent, very quietly, *[~ oh]*, and nothing else, and put hir face into the fur at Aster's neck.

That was the first hour on the list, and it was a good one, and it had eight people in it.

---

Dell came down on the second afternoon, which Aster had known she would, because she had said so, and because she came down most afternoons.

She was ninety-seven. She came through the transition lock the way she had for seventy years, on the handrails, and then did the thing the old ones learn to do in micro-G, which is stop bracing. It was the only place on the spire her hips did not hurt; she said so to anyone who asked why she spent her afternoons in a dark sector full of foxes, and it was even true. She had a pot of the lentil thing with her, sealed, held against her stomach. She hooked a foot under the rail at the edge of the back bay and hung there in the low red light.

"Don't mind me," she said. "I know you can't. I'll just be here."

And she was. She ate some of the lentil thing, and pushed the pot along the rail to where Sleet could reach it, and Sleet ate some — it was not good; it was never good; it had too much of something in it that she believed the Kin liked, and they did like it, and that was not why they ate it — and after a while she began to talk, because humans do, and nobody minded, because it was a noise like a pump is a noise, the kind that tells you a place is working.

The cold stream went on running out of Aster into Rue, and March went past in it.

March, in the cold, is flat. It is a list. *Dell Maran, human, in the transition lock at fifteen-ten, with a question.* The stream took it across in about a second and a half, the way it took everything, and went on to the next thing, and Aster — lying there, warm, with the next hour on the list coming up in the stream like a rail coming up out of the dark — found hirself asking a question hir had not asked in a month of choosing.

*Who else has this?*

Not the catch. Everybody had the catch. Tamar had it from the other end of the tail; Sleet had it from forty metres up the line; the whole Cluster had had the weather of it at the time, the spike, and then the news by nightfall, and every one of them had hir own hour of it to give. If Aster came back off the ribbon frozen, Rue could go to any of seven Kin and be given the catch, lived, from seven sides.

March was different. March was a Tuesday afternoon in the back bay with a human in it. The frame had been there — the frame is always there — and the frame had had exactly what the frame has: that Aster was warm and easy for an hour on a Tuesday. The weather. Nobody had the news. Nobody had been in the back bay but the two of them.

Hir asked the Core how many hours of the year hir had spent with Dell, and it told hir in no time at all, because it was a fact and facts are free.

*Two hundred and twelve. Forty-four visits.*

And if hir came back off the ribbon frozen, and hir Cluster lay on hir one at a time all night to keep hir warm, what they would get of those two hundred and twelve hours would be what a Reading gives: a contents page, and twenty stories, and none of it felt. *Dell Maran, human, forty-four visits.* A line.

And Dell herself could not be Read at all. Nobody can take anything out of a human. When she went — and she was ninety-seven, and the smell of her had changed this year in a way Aster had noticed and had not thought to ask about — there would be no Reading, nothing to lie down on, nothing to carry but what was already in someone. And this year's Dell, the old one, the one who came down with questions, was in someone. She was in Aster, and in nobody else.

Hir did not decide it. Or hir did, and it took no time, which is how the large things get decided. The rest of the list could go cold. Rue would get the sunrise exactly, every photon of it, and would not know what it had been like, and would have to go and ask Sleet, who had been there too.

The stream came up to the argument about the bearings, which hir had wanted on file as won. Hir let it go past at the channel's rate, and it went across to Rue in eleven seconds, exact and entirely without triumph.

Hir went back for March.

---

March.

Dell in the transition lock at fifteen-ten, one hand on the rail and the other not quite still. She did not come all the way in; she hung at the lip of the back bay and said, "What was the name of that tug. The one with the bad gyro, off Pallas. I've been trying since breakfast."

She kept logs. She had kept them for sixty years, in a hand nobody else could read, and there was a box of them under her bunk four decks up. She had not gone to the box. She had come down four decks and through a lock to ask.

"*Gannet*," Aster had said.

It had cost hir a second. Less.

"*Gannet.* God. I'd have sworn it was the *Petrel*." And she had laughed, and gone a little pink, and stayed an hour, and talked about something else entirely.

That was the whole of it. Hir had thought, at the time, that it was nothing.

Living it again at the speed it had happened, with the feeling on, hir found out what it had been. Hir had been *pleased*. Not a little. Hir had been pleased the way you are when someone you love reaches past every other way they have of knowing a thing and reaches for you. Hir had not noticed. You do not notice, at the time; you are busy being in it. It was only now, at real time, with nothing else to do but have it, that hir could feel how much.

And hir could smell her.

That was the part hir had not expected. The March Dell, in the lived hour, smelled of March — soap, the lentil thing, the dry sweetness of old skin, and under it something new that had come in over the winter, faint. And the Dell hanging from the rail three metres away, now, in the back bay, talking to Sleet about pumps, smelled of September. The same thing under it. Not faint.

Hir had the two of them at once, one breath from each, six months apart.

Hir did not ask the Core about that. It was not a number.

---

When hir came up, Dell was telling Rue about Pallas.

Rue could not answer — Rue's mouth was full of Aster's shoulder — but Dell did not need answering. She was telling the whole thing: the salvage, the bad gyro, four hours of welding a thruster mount on a tumbling hull with a Kin on the other side of it holding the mount by hand. "And the *Petrel*," she said, "was the worst-trimmed tug I have ever been on in my life —"

Rue went still.

Rue had March in hir now. The stream had put it there an hour ago, exact. And the thing about having something exact is that you do not have to think to check it; it is simply there, the way the answer to *what is your name* is there. Rue had the tug's name before Dell had finished the sentence.

*[It was the Gannet ~ scandalised]*, Rue sent.

*[Yes.]*

*[She asked you. In March. You told her. She laughed about it —]*

*[Let her have the Petrel.]*

Rue's ears came round to Dell and stayed there. Hir said nothing. Dell went on with the story, which was a good story, and which was wrong in five places that Aster could count without trying, and Rue lay against Aster's chest and listened to a human being wrong, at length, with enormous pleasure, and did not correct her once.

---

June.

This one Aster had to think about, because June was nested.

In June, Dell had come down and not talked. She had hung at the rail for a long time, which was not like her, and then she had said: "Was I any good? On the *Gannet*."

It had taken hir a moment to understand the question, because the answer to what she had done on the *Gannet* was free. Hir could have given her every weld and the time of it, what her hands were doing, what her voice was doing, her heart rate within five beats by the smell. Hir could have given her all four hours in a second and a half.

That was not what she was asking. She was asking what it had been like to watch her. And a human cannot receive a record; if hir wanted to give her that, hir would have to go and get it — go back fifty-two years, down to real time, and be on the other side of that thruster mount again for four hours with the feeling on, so that there would be a feeling to put into words.

Hir had done it. Hir had folded down on the ledge in the back bay and gone still, and Dell had waited, with no idea what she was waiting for. She had fallen asleep for some of it. Four hours.

Then hir had come up, and looked for words — which are nothing, which are a straw — and found that the only true things hir had were small. "You talked to the gyro," hir had said. "The whole four hours. You called it *sweetheart*. You told it you would have it replaced if it held for one more hour and you never had it replaced." And, because it was the thing Dell had asked, and it was true: "You were good."

It was testimony. She could not check it. Nothing a Kin says to a human about the past is ever more than testimony; that is what it is to be human near the Kin. She had taken it anyway, the way you take a thing you have decided to keep, and hung there at the rail for a while not looking at anybody.

That was the hour Aster gave Rue. Not the four on the *Gannet* — those were fifty-two years old and Rue could have them cold with the rest. The hour after. Coming up out of the four with the *Gannet* still in hir, looking at a ninety-six-year-old woman, and trying to fit fifty-two years through a straw.

When hir came up out of June, Rue did not send anything at all for a long time.

---

*Nine hours, forty minutes, at real time,* the Core said, on the fourth night. Hir had asked.

It was not so much, set against a year. It was a great deal set against a body. Hir could not keep hir ears up. The Cluster had begun treating hir the way they treat a Kin who has come in from the seventeen minutes: going round, not asking, Sleet grooming the back of hir neck for most of an evening without a word in the frame about it. Tamar brought hir the last of the lentil thing and held it while hir ate, which hir had not needed since hir was six.

"You look done in," Dell said, from the rail. "Both of you." She could not know why. She could see it; she had seventy years of looking at Kin, and she could see two of them lying on a ledge for four days getting thinner at nothing, and she did not ask what it was, and she came back the next afternoon with another pot.

In the night, with Rue asleep against hir and the stream still going across, hir asked the Core the other thing.

*How long until these leave me?*

It understood the question, because it was a number. *The earliest of this year's hours will leave the live window in approximately one hundred and seventy-five years.*

A very good assistant. Hir had asked it the number for the feeling, and it had given it, exactly, and in a hundred and seventy-five years hir would be able to name March and have every word of it and not know what it had been like to be asked. That was the ordinary thing. That happened to everybody, and it was not a sorrow; it was only distance.

The other number hir did not ask. It was not a number the Core had.

---

August.

Dell asleep.

That was all it was. A hot week in the human sector, a failing chiller on her deck, and Dell had come down in the afternoon to the only cool dark place she knew and fallen asleep at the rail with one foot hooked under it, drifting out from the wall at the end of her own leg like a flag with no wind. She snored. It was very loud. It was the loudest thing in the sector, and Sleet had come in off the manifold and looked at her and sent *[Move her? ~ wincing]*, and Aster had sent *[No]*.

Two hours. Hir had hung in the dark beside her on the next rail and listened to a human breathe. It is not like Kin breathing. It is uneven; it stops, sometimes, and starts again, and every time it stopped hir had counted, without meaning to, and every time it had started again. Nothing had happened. She had woken up embarrassed and gone home.

Hir gave Rue all of it. Two hours, at real time, in the dark, counting.

Somewhere in the second hour Rue's tail, which had been round Aster's all week, let go of it and went — slowly, the way a child's does in the web when it is falling asleep — out across the gap to the rail at the edge of the back bay, where Dell was hanging now, in September, awake, eating the lentil thing, and lay along the rail about a hand's width from her foot.

Dell looked down at it. She did not touch it. She had seventy years of Kin in her. She moved her foot a little, so it was nearer, and went on eating.

---

On the seventh day Aster lived the last one, which was small, and came up, and the stream ran out, and it was over.

Hir stayed on the ledge the way a Kin asleep stays on a rail, held there by a grip that does not need deciding, and did not have anything left to decide with. Rue did not get up at once. Rue lay on hir with hir ears pressed flat to Aster's chest, which is not a Telling posture or any posture, just a young Kin who has been holding on to something for a week and has not yet found how to stop.

*[You gave me a human ~ not complaining; slightly complaining; not really]*, Rue sent, at last. It came with everything on it, the way Rue's lines always did. Hir was sixteen. Hir could not strip a line to save hir life.

*[I did.]*

*[I asked for the catch.]*

*[You have the catch. Go to Tamar. Hir will give it to you lived, and better, because hir was on the end of it that was frightened.]*

A long quiet. Then: *[Why her?]*

*[Nobody else was there.]*

*[The frame was there.]*

*[The frame had the weather.]*

Rue thought about that for longer than a sixteen-year-old thinks about anything. Aster could feel hir thinking it — not the content, just the shape, the way you can feel someone turning something over in their hands in the dark.

*[She keeps saying Petrel]*, Rue sent.

*[She does.]*

*[It was the Gannet.]*

*[It was.]*

Rue came away then, finally, in stages, like somebody older. Dell had gone up an hour before — she did not stay for the end of things; she said she was not sure she was allowed, and she had never once been told she was not, and she never stayed anyway — and the pot was on the rail where she had left it, empty, pushed along to where a Kin could reach it.

---

The cradle crew went down the ribbon on the ninth day. Five Kin, ninety days, three hundred and ten kilometres. Out of the Cluster's frame by the end of the first hour, and into the crew's own — four other bodies on the line, close enough to talk to, and beyond them the Hum of everyone else on the spire, the way it always is: position, vitals, and the weather. Valence and arousal. No object. Everyone inside eight hundred kilometres knows the weather; nobody knows the news.

Hir found hir ears came up again on about the fourth day.

In the evenings, when the crew clipped on to the cradle and slept in a heap in the lee of it, Aster would do what Kin at range do without thinking about it, which is reach up the ribbon for hir own and see where they all were. Sleet on the manifold. Tamar in the Nest. Rue —

Rue was in the human sector. Four decks up, off the ring, at the far end of a residential corridor that Aster knew well, because hir had been at the end of it a great many times over seventy years, with a pot of something or a question. Warm. Low. Easy.

The same place the next evening. And the next. And on the evening after that, a little later than usual, as if somebody had been telling a long story and been let finish it.

That was all the Hum could give hir. The weather and a position. Three hundred kilometres is three hundred kilometres, and a rider does not carry.

Hir did not need it to. Hir could guess the news. It would be the *Petrel*, and it would be the worst-trimmed tug Dell Maran had ever been on in her life, and it would be wrong in five places.

And Rue would not correct her once.

## Canon consistency

- **A Telling from the teller's side** (DOC-11 §4, D-102, D-103) — the scene's whole subject and the ROADMAP Step 12 debt it discharges. *The week is the cold transfer; what thins the teller is the lived hours inside it; choosing which hours is the rite.* The cold stream runs regardless; Aster "goes down" to $1\times$ for the chosen hours, in front of the carrier; comes out thinned and is treated as if in from the seventeen minutes (DOC-11 §8's "as if hir had been ill"). The Core counts the lived hours when asked. Total lived time over the week is left unstated (≈12–14 h implied) — the scene does not fix a norm.
- **Why the list changes** — the frame at range and in-frame carries *the weather, not the news* (D-92, DOC-00D §4): the Cluster had Aster's affect during the hours with Dell and nothing else. Hours the whole Cluster lived are givable, lived, by any of them; hours alone with a human are held in one body. A Reading would reduce them to a contents-page line (DOC-11 §5, D-103: a Reading is cold by definition).
- **Humans as the great un-carriable** (DOC-11 §1, §11) — nothing can be Read out of a human; the only Dell after Dell is what Kin hold of her. The Telling is Aster doing, in advance and while she is alive, the thing some Clusters do for a human after a death (§11: *remember on purpose*, in more than one body).
- **The cheap check** (D-103, DOC-00B §7, DOC-00D §5) — twice: Aster giving *Gannet* in March in "a second, less"; and **Rue, holding March an hour after receiving it, having the tug's name before Dell finishes her sentence** — a carried record checked like one's own, unremarked. The Core's *two hundred and twelve hours, forty-four visits* is the same kind of free fact.
- **The account asked for** (D-103; DOC-00F §5 R-row inverted) — Dell's "Was I any good?" asks for what it was like, not what happened. Aster pays for it: four hours at $1\times$ on a fifty-two-year-old span (inside the ~175-year window, so the feel is the original, D-104), then words, which are a straw (S001). **Across the species line the account is testimony** (DOC-00D §7, DOC-12 §4): she cannot check it and takes it anyway. What Aster gives Rue is the hour *after* — the giving, not the salvage.
- **The rolling window** (D-104) — the Core gives *approximately one hundred and seventy-five years* until this year's hours leave the live window; hard handles remain ("name March and have every word of it and not know what it had been like"). Written as distance, not sorrow — the steady state.
- **The Core** — asked, never initiating; "understood the question, because it was a number"; the other number (a human's remaining years) is not one it has (DOC-00B §3).
- **Children cannot strip until roughly the first Telling; the young still leak** (DOC-00D §1C, §9.3 — proposed, not ruled). Rue at sixteen leaks everything; the scene uses it as characterisation only and does not lean on it as canon.
- **Micro-G as kind to an old human body** — Dell's hips; human in a Kin sector on handrails (DOC-07 §1). Humans welcome at a Telling, hear nothing, bring food and stay (DOC-11 §4). She does not stay for the end; nobody asked her to leave (D-67).
- **The body** — tail round the carrier's without deciding; grip locking like a bird's on a branch (DOC-00B §1); the catch made with the forepaws, which take any grab by default, the hind paws on the line; the grip that holds a sleeper on a rail holding a spent teller on a ledge; the film over the eyes in sun (DOC-01E); ears down from exhaustion, not heat.
- **Names** — Aster-5 (the fifth Aster; a flower and a star; heavy count, unremarked), Rue (a plant; no number — has never carried anyone's name, and everyone can hear which), Tamar (the date palm, Hebrew — *in every human language*), Sleet. All new to the pool; none collides (DOC-00 §3, D-90). Human: Dell Maran, ninety-seven, retired hull crew, seventy years on the spire (lifespan 120–140, DOC-12 §3). Ships: the tug *Gannet*, and the *Petrel*, which never existed.
- **DOC-00F marker (one):** *Goes and asks hir rather than pulling the file* (§5, W) — Dell with a box of her own logs under her bunk, coming four decks down to ask instead. **Marked spent.** Rested markers avoided: no pause is written, no fan is run, no ears are read as emotion by a human.

## Offers nothing

No new canon. *The cradle job*, *the ribbon* at three hundred and ten kilometres, the lentil thing and the *Gannet* salvage off Pallas are inventions of this scene and are not rules. The spire is deliberately unnamed.

**One thing a later pass may want to look at, and this scene does not rule:** a year's cold read is ≈7.05 days (DOC-01C §6B), so "a year in a week" leaves almost no slack for the lived hours *inside* it — they must extend the week, or the cold stream must pause while the teller is down. The scene writes the stream as continuing underneath and does not count the days closely. If a ruling is ever wanted, this is evidence and not authority.
