## 1. Gil

Gil Arnesen had nine days left on the station and had asked to hear it properly before he went, which was a thing he had asked roughly every two years for thirty-eight years, and Sedge had never once said no.

They did it at night, which meant nothing to Sedge and a great deal to Gil, who had to be walked. He went along the handline in the slow committed way of a man who had learned micro-G at thirty-three and had never got it in his body, and Sedge kept a quarter-turn ahead of him with one hind paw on the line and the tail out for balance, and neither of them said anything for the first four decks because there was nothing to say yet.

At the junction above the ring transfer Sedge stopped.

"Here?"

The pause. Sedge gave it a long one, partly out of habit and mostly because the honest answer was complicated.

"Here is good. Put your hand flat on the wall."

He did. He had been doing it for thirty-eight years. He had a callus on the heel of that hand that Sedge had privately catalogued around the fourteenth year and had never mentioned.

"Anything?"

"There's a — " He moved his hand. "There's a hum."

"That's the ring."

"Right. That's the ring." He said it the way he always said it, without any particular disappointment, as a man reading back the one line of a language he had. "That's the ring, and that's all I've got."

What Sedge had was this: the ring at forty hertz and true, and deck twelve's trunk running a half-beat under it where it had been running for six years since she had re-hung it, and the scrubber stack three decks up making the small ticking complaint it made when the humidity went over sixty, and behind all of that, at the very bottom, the station — the whole two hundred thousand tonnes of it — sitting in one note. Not a good note. There is no good note. A right one.

It had taken her a hundred and six years and it was as finished as it was going to get, and the only other person on *Artemis-9* who cared whether it was finished was a seventy-one-year-old human with a callus on one hand who could hear the ring and nothing else.

---

The compressor argument had been in the four-hundred-and-seventy-second year and had lasted eight months.

Deck nine. Gil had wanted it re-mounted on the original bed with new isolators and Sedge had wanted it moved two metres aft, and the reason Sedge had wanted it moved two metres aft could not be put into any language Gil had: it would have been *flat there*, it would have sat under the deck-twelve trunk instead of across it, and the whole aft third of the station would have stopped arguing with itself. Sedge had said all of that. Gil had asked, reasonably, for a number.

There is no number. That was the argument. It ran eight months and it ran hot and by the end of it three separate humans in the engineering office had learned to leave the room.

Sedge had worked out, about eleven years afterward and with the sensation of a door opening in a wall she had walked past a thousand times, that Gil had known she was right at roughly the six-week mark and had argued for another six and a half months anyway because he was enjoying himself.

She had never raised it. She had been saving it, in a small way, the way Ilex saves things, for no occasion in particular.

---

They came down through the axis and Gil got tired around deck seven and hooked an elbow through the line to rest, and from the pocket of his coveralls he produced a notebook.

It was not a display. It was paper, the ration kind, four of them rubber-banded together, and the top one had been rained on at some point in its life, which on a station takes some doing.

"I'm not taking these down with me," he said. "My daughter's got no use for them and I'm not going to be the old man with a box."

Sedge did not take it. Her hands stayed where they were.

"What is it."

"It's what you said." He was embarrassed, which was visible in him from two metres away and also in the air — the specific sharp note a human puts out when he has decided to do a thing and would now like it to be over. "Thirty-eight years. Every time you told me something about the station, I wrote it down. Because I can't hear it. I was never going to be able to hear it. So I wrote down what you said about it."

He opened one, awkwardly, one-handed, and Sedge read the page upside down without meaning to.

*Y11. S. says the 4 has gone sharp again. Asked how sharp. Got a look.*

*Y11. S. says deck 12 is "muddy". Asked what muddy means. No answer, 6 weeks.*

*Y19. The 2 is right. S. would not stop talking about it. Two days.*

*Y26. S. says the whole aft third has stopped arguing. Did not say with what.*

Sedge read four pages and then stopped reading, and hung there on the line with her ears fanned right out, which was heat and was also not entirely heat.

The Kin do not write things down. Sedge could recall every one of those conversations exactly and would be able to at four hundred, in order, with the temperature of the corridor. That was not what was in her chest. What was in her chest was that a man who could not hear a single thing she had done had spent thirty-eight years making the only account of it that would exist in words, badly, in pencil, on paper, because that is what humans do — they write it down, and it stays, and it stays after them.

The pause went on long enough that Gil started to look worried.

"You were wrong about the compressor," Sedge said.

He barked out a laugh. "I was not wrong about the compressor."

"You were wrong about the compressor, and you knew you were wrong about the compressor in the first six weeks."

Gil opened his mouth. Then he shut it. Then, after a moment in which several decades visibly went past behind his face, he said: "Yeah."

"Six weeks."

"About five, if I'm honest."

⟨ ⟩ — a short thing, ultrasonic, that Gil could not hear and that Sedge did not intend for him, and which would have translated roughly as the noise a person makes when she has been carrying something for eleven years and puts it down.

---

They went back up the long way, which Gil did not need and did not complain about.

At the junction above the ring transfer he stopped again on his own, without being told, and put his flat hand on the wall, and held it there for a while with his head tipped.

"It's the four, isn't it," he said. "Something's off in the four."

It was not the four. The four had been right for eleven years and was one of the things Sedge was proudest of on the entire station. But there *was* something off — a bearing in the port scrubber gallery that Sedge had noticed four days ago and had not yet got to, forty metres from where Gil's hand was, in the same half of the station, in roughly the same part of the note.

Sedge said, "Something's off, yes."

"Knew it," said Gil, pleased with himself, and pushed on up the line.

Sedge stayed at the junction for a minute or two after he had gone, holding the rail with all four, and did not go anywhere at all.

## 2. Doss

Kirin cooked for nine that evening and told them, truthfully, that it was nine courses, and one of them made the noise humans make when they are being told there will be nine of something.

The Kin sector's galley is not a kitchen. It is a bench, two heaters and a filtration hood that runs at a speed no human galley ever needs, because the whole sector is kept scent-clean the way a studio is kept clean, and Kirin had been composing on that canvas since about the third hour of the afternoon.

Here is what she built. Across the nine courses there was a line in the copper — the trace-metal note, the one register that only a Kin can taste — that started almost absent, came up under the third, sat under the fourth and fifth like a floor, and then went out entirely at the seventh so that the eighth arrived in a silence. And in the fifth there was a joke: a zinc figure that quoted, exactly, a thing Vesper-11 had made about two hundred years ago and that four Kin alive would recognise.

Not one person at that table could perceive any of it. Not the line, not the silence, not the joke, not even the fact that there was a register there at all.

Kirin knew that going in. It is the entire art form.

---

They ate it. They ate all of it. Doss, who ran the hull crew and had the shoulders of somebody who had spent thirty years hauling, ate two of everything and made a speech in the middle of the sixth about how the paste at the human mess was an insult to people who worked for a living, and Marta Vey told him nobody had asked, and he said he was aware of that.

Around the seventh — the one where the copper went out, the one Kirin had built the whole shape to arrive at — Doss put his spoon down and frowned at the bowl.

"That's different."

Kirin held the pause a little longer than she needed to.

"Yes."

"No, but it's — " He moved his hand, trying to get at it, and failed, and Kirin watched him fail with an enjoyment she would have found difficult to explain to anyone at that table. "It's *cleaner*. Did you take something out?"

"I took something out."

"What?"

"You can't taste it."

"Then how do I know it's gone?"

Which is, as it happens, a good question, and one that the Plain School has been circling for five centuries, and Kirin was not going to be able to answer it in a human sector at the seventh course. What she said was, "Because I built the six before it so you would."

Doss thought about that for a second, decided it was probably a compliment, and went back to eating.

---

Later, while the others were arguing about something at the far end, he asked what the fifth course had been meant to be.

Not politely. Genuinely — leaning on the bench with a bowl in his hand, the way a man asks about someone else's trade.

So Kirin told him. All of it: the zinc figure, and what a zinc figure is, and Vesper's original, and why quoting it in the fifth and not the fourth was the entire point, and what the copper floor was doing underneath. It took about four minutes. Doss listened to all four minutes with an expression of complete attention and understood, as far as Kirin could tell, not one single thing, and at the end of it he said, "Huh," and then, "That's a lot of work for a bowl of stew," and then, "It was really good, though."

It was the best part of the evening and it was not close.

---

Kirin lies to her own Cluster nearly every morning. She had told them, that same day, a dream about a human crying over a fifth course that she was fairly sure she had made up somewhere between waking and saying it, and Sable had objected on principle, and the objection was the pleasure, and the pleasure was possible only because none of them could ever check.

She would not have told Doss an untrue thing about that meal if she had been paid to. Not out of any particular virtue. It is just that there is no game in it: she could tell that man anything at all and he would have to take it, and that isn't lying, it's only being believed.

---

On the way out Marta Vey put a sealed pouch on the bench and said it was from her, and that it was probably bad, and left quickly.

It was bad. She had put the copper supplement in it — she knew about the copper, she had clearly asked somebody about the copper — and she had put in about four times what anyone could want, so that the whole thing tasted like the inside of a coin and went on tasting like that for most of an hour.

Kirin ate all of it, alone, at the bench, at some speed, and then sat there for a while feeling extremely warm about everything.

## 3. Ochi

Bramble was not allowed in the transfer bay during a cycle, was not allowed on the hull, was not allowed in the axis lock, was not allowed to sign for anything, and had been told no eleven times that week by six different adults, all of whom loved her, all of whom were right, and every one of whom had felt exactly how much she had wanted it at the moment they said it.

That is the part. Everyone leaves out that part. It is not that she gets told no. It is that she cannot want a thing privately. She wants it and the whole Nest has it, immediately, at the strength she wants it, and then they tell her no anyway and are kind about it, and the kindness is unbearable in a way she has not got words for and could not send if she had.

So: the cargo face, mid-afternoon, where she *was* allowed, moving crated stock with Ochi, who was twenty-six and human and had no idea about any of this.

---

"You've got a better angle than me," Ochi said. "What do you reckon, will it clear?"

Bramble looked at it. A three-metre crate, a doorframe, a bad approach, and a slow drift Ochi could not properly see from where he was.

Everyone she knew could have felt, right then, exactly how badly she wanted to be asked that question.

He couldn't. He was just waiting, with one hand on the rail, looking at her, mildly, the way you look at somebody whose answer you are actually going to use.

The pause. Bramble held it, which she had been taught and mostly did badly.

"It'll clear. It'll catch the lip on the far side about thirty centimetres down if you don't put some yaw on it now."

"Now-now?"

"Now."

He put yaw on it now. It cleared the frame and caught nothing and went on down the face in a long lazy turn, and Ochi said, "Nice," and started on the next one.

---

They worked for another ninety minutes.

He asked her twice more and took the answer both times, and once she was wrong — a bad call on a drift, entirely her fault — and the crate went into the stanchion with a bang that brought somebody's head out of the office, and Ochi said, "Ah, that's on me, I rushed it," which was a lie, and which he told without any apparent awareness that he was doing something enormous.

Nobody in the bay knew how much that mattered. There was no way for anybody in the bay to know.

Bramble had, for about ninety minutes, an interior that belonged to her.

She could want to be good at this, extravagantly, embarrassingly, with the whole force of being fourteen, and it went absolutely nowhere. It stopped at her own skin. She could be pleased about the yaw call — she was *very* pleased about the yaw call, she is going to be pleased about the yaw call in two hundred years — and the only person in the room got precisely as much of that as she chose to put in her face, which was none, because she is fourteen and would rather die.

It is the only privacy her species has ever offered her and it is a gift from somebody who does not know he is giving it and could not be told.

---

At the end of the shift Ochi said, "Same tomorrow? You're quicker than the loader."

Bramble said she would see, which was not true, and which nobody could catch.

---

She got back to the Nest at the eighteenth hour and the weather gave her away inside four seconds.

Sable looked up. Ilex looked up. Sorrel, who was lying face down and had not moved perceptibly in some hours, did not look up but sent *[~ well, well]* with about as much rider on it as a person can put on nothing at all.

*[Don't]*

*[Nobody said anything]*

*[You were all going to]*

And they let it go, all of them, immediately, which was not nothing either, and Bramble climbed into the weave between Vesper and Sable with her ears flat and her enormous stupid heart going, and said nothing about the yaw call to anyone, and had it entirely to herself for one more night.

## 4. Marta

Sorrel found Marta Vey at the gallery port around the twentieth hour with her boots hooked under the rail, eating something out of a pouch, looking down into a dark and empty Bay Nine.

She came and hung on the rail beside her. Neither of them said anything for a while, which Sorrel had learned took humans about two years of knowing you before it stopped being uncomfortable, and which Marta had got to in about eight months.

"So what was funny," she said eventually.

"When?"

"Last night. Your friend — the grey one — went past the port and then everybody stopped, and then the one with the bag just curled up in the air like somebody'd shot her." She waved the pouch. "And you were all gone. For like a minute. Doss thought somebody'd got hurt."

The pause.

It went on a while, because Sorrel was trying to find a road into it and there was not one. There is the setup, which is a sentence about a compressor from eleven years ago. There is the eleven years, which is the entire mechanism and cannot be described, only served. There is the fact that it was spent on one point in a Tuesday game, which is the best part and the least explicable part. There is no version of any of that which fits into a human evening.

"It was a joke," Sorrel said.

"Okay."

"It was eleven years long."

Marta stopped chewing.

"Eleven years."

"Ilex started it in the four-hundred-and-ninety-ninth year. She has been — " and she had to stop, because *feeding it* was not right and *carrying it* was very nearly right and was also a word she was not going to spend here. "She has been keeping it. And last night she spent it."

"On a game."

"On one point in a game."

Marta thought about that for a good deal longer than Sorrel expected her to, looking down into the empty bay, and then she said, "That's the funniest thing I've ever heard and I don't get any of it," and went back to her pouch.

And that was all. She did not ask what the joke was. She has never once asked what a thing meant in a way that required Sorrel to flatten it for her, which is a small piece of manners that Sorrel is fairly sure she does not know she has, and which is most of why she comes up here.

---

They talked about nothing for another half hour. Her brother, who is on a hauler and never calls. The number-two pump, which Sorrel has opinions about and which Marta indulged. Whether Doss is going to hurt himself on the aft face doing the thing he does with the crates, which he is.

It was slow. It was extremely slow. Every single thing she said, Sorrel had the whole of by about her fourth word and then held, and waited, and let her finish, for four seconds, five, while she got to the end of a sentence that was already over.

She had dreamt, the night before, about doing this at Burst speed. Everything at once, both directions, nothing held. She had lost most of it by breakfast, the way you do.

It was nothing like that. It was not even in the same country as that.

She stayed until she had to go, and then hung on the rail a while longer with her tail wrapped twice round the stanchion, looking down into the dark bay where she could see, perfectly well, every rail and ring and the wet mark on the bulkhead where the bag had hit last night, and thought about the waiting.

The waiting turns out to be most of it. That is the part she cannot get anybody at home to understand.
